Glucose is the body's main fuel, and its level is held in a tight range by two hormones with opposite jobs: insulin lowers it, glucagon (plus cortisol, adrenaline and growth hormone) raises it. Diabetes is what happens when insulin is missing or doesn't work. This lesson covers how glucose is measured, how diabetes is diagnosed, and the long-term markers.

How the body controls glucose

After a meal, glucose rises and the pancreas's beta cells release insulin. Insulin acts like a key that lets glucose into muscle and fat cells and tells the liver to store glucose as glycogen. Between meals, glucose falls and glucagon tells the liver to release its stored glucose.

  • Type 1 diabetes: the immune system destroys the beta cells, so there is no insulin. Usually young, often presents in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Autoantibodies (anti-GAD, islet cell, anti-insulin) and a low C-peptide support it.
  • Type 2 diabetes: insulin is made but the body resists it, and production slowly falls behind. Usually linked to obesity and age.
  • Gestational diabetes: insulin resistance caused by pregnancy hormones.
Why DKA happensWith no insulin, cells starve even while glucose is high in the blood. The body burns fat instead, and the liver turns fatty acids into ketone bodies (acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate, acetone). These are acids, so the result is a high anion gap metabolic acidosis with high glucose. Potassium often reads high (it shifts out of cells) even though the body's total potassium is low.

Measuring glucose

  • Hexokinase is the reference method. Hexokinase converts glucose to glucose-6-phosphate, and a second enzyme (G6PD) uses it to produce NADPH. The rise in absorbance at 340 nm is proportional to glucose. It is very accurate and has few interferences.
  • Glucose oxidase is specific for glucose (it ignores other sugars). It produces hydrogen peroxide, which is measured with a color reaction. Reducing substances such as ascorbic acid and uric acid can interfere with that peroxide step.

Specimen matters: glucose in an unspun tube falls by about 5-7% per hour because cells keep using it. Use a gray-top (sodium fluoride) tube or separate promptly. Whole blood glucose (like a fingerstick) reads about 10-15% lower than plasma glucose.

Diagnosing diabetes and prediabetes

Any one of these confirms diabetes (repeated to be sure, unless the patient has obvious symptoms):

  • Fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL (fasting = no calories for at least 8 hours).
  • 2-hour glucose ≥200 mg/dL during a 75 g OGTT.
  • HbA1c ≥6.5%.
  • Random glucose ≥200 with classic symptoms (thirst, frequent urination, weight loss).

Prediabetes is the zone in between: fasting 100-125 (impaired fasting glucose), 2-hour 140-199 (impaired glucose tolerance), or A1c 5.7-6.4%.

Memory hook: the numbersFasting: 100 / 126. Two-hour: 140 / 200. A1c: 5.7 / 6.5. In each pair, the first number starts prediabetes and the second starts diabetes.

The oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

The patient eats normal amounts of carbohydrate for 3 days (so the body isn't in "starvation mode"), fasts 8-12 hours, then drinks 75 g of glucose. Blood is drawn fasting and at 2 hours. A healthy pancreas brings glucose back down by 2 hours; in diabetes it stays high.

Gestational diabetes is tested at 24-28 weeks. In the one-step 75 g test, any one value at or above fasting 92, 1 hour 180, or 2 hours 153 mg/dL is positive. The two-step method starts with a 50 g non-fasting 1-hour screen, and only abnormal screens get a full 100 g test.

Long-term markers: HbA1c and fructosamine

Glucose sticks to proteins slowly and permanently (glycation). The more glucose around, the more sticks.

  • HbA1c is glucose attached to hemoglobin. Red cells live about 120 days, so A1c reflects the average glucose over the last 2-3 months. Estimated average glucose (eAG) = 28.7 × A1c − 46.7; an A1c of 7% ≈ 154 mg/dL.
  • Fructosamine is glucose attached to serum proteins (mostly albumin, which lasts about 20 days), so it reflects the last 2-3 weeks. It is useful in pregnancy or when A1c is unreliable.
Exam trap: when A1c liesA1c depends on red cell lifespan. If red cells die early (hemolytic anemia, bleeding), they have less time to collect glucose, so A1c reads falsely low. Some hemoglobin variants (HbS, HbC) can interfere with certain methods. Use fructosamine in those patients.

Hypoglycemia: insulin and C-peptide

The pancreas makes insulin as one long molecule, proinsulin, and cuts it into insulin plus a spare piece called C-peptide, in equal amounts. Injected insulin has no C-peptide. That gives a neat way to find the cause of low glucose:

  • High insulin + high C-peptide → the pancreas is overproducing: insulinoma or a sulfonylurea drug (which squeezes the pancreas).
  • High insulin + low C-peptide → insulin was injected.

Ketones and kidney damage

The urine dipstick ketone pad uses nitroprusside, which reacts with acetoacetate (and acetone, weakly) but not with beta-hydroxybutyrate. In severe DKA, most ketone is beta-hydroxybutyrate, so the dipstick can underestimate how sick the patient is. A specific blood beta-hydroxybutyrate test fixes this.

High glucose damages the kidney's filters over years. The earliest sign is a little albumin leaking into the urine: a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 30-300 mg/g (microalbuminuria). Standard dipsticks are not sensitive enough to detect it.

Check your understanding

Fasting glucose 118, 2-hour OGTT 165. Category?

Prediabetes: impaired fasting glucose (100-125) and impaired glucose tolerance (140-199).

A patient with sickle cell anemia has an A1c of 5.0% but home glucose readings of 200 mg/dL. Why?

Shortened red cell survival (and possibly hemoglobin variant interference) makes A1c falsely low. Use fructosamine.

Glucose 35 mg/dL, insulin high, C-peptide low. Cause?

Injected (exogenous) insulin, because pancreatic insulin always comes with C-peptide.

Why can a DKA patient have only "small" ketones on the dipstick?

Most of the ketone in DKA is beta-hydroxybutyrate, which nitroprusside doesn't detect.

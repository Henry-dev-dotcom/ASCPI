Analyzers are fast and accurate, but they can be fooled. A big part of a lab scientist's job is spotting when a number can't be right and knowing how to fix it. This lesson explains the common CBC errors, then the other hematology tests and markers you will meet.

How an analyzer counts cells

Most analyzers use the Coulter (impedance) principle. Cells are suspended in a salt solution that conducts electricity and pulled one at a time through a tiny opening with an electric current across it. A cell doesn't conduct well, so each one causes a brief rise in resistance: a pulse. The number of pulses = the cell count and the size of each pulse = the cell's volume. That is how the MCV is measured directly.

Modern analyzers add light scatter (lasers) and sometimes fluorescent dyes to sort white cells into types. Results come with histograms (graphs of cell sizes) and flags that warn you to look at a smear.

  • RDW (red cell distribution width) measures how much red cells vary in size. It rises early in iron deficiency, because new small cells mix with old normal ones. It is usually normal in thalassemia trait, where all the cells are evenly small.
  • MPV (mean platelet volume) measures platelet size. Large platelets suggest the marrow is pushing out young ones (as in ITP).
  • Hemoglobin is measured by breaking open the red cells and reading the color (cyanmethemoglobin at 540 nm, or a cyanide-free SLS method). Hematocrit is calculated: RBC × MCV.

The four classic CBC errors and how to fix them

1. Cold agglutinins. Some patients have IgM antibodies that make red cells clump at room temperature. The analyzer sees each clump as one huge cell. So the RBC count is falsely low and the MCV falsely high. The hemoglobin is still right (it is measured after lysing all cells), so the calculated values go wrong: the Hct (RBC × MCV) is falsely low and the MCHC is falsely very high (often over 37). Fix: warm the sample to 37 °C for about 15-30 minutes and rerun.

2. Lipemia, very high WBC, or icterus. The hemoglobin method reads color through the liquid. Anything that makes the liquid cloudy (fat, huge numbers of white cells) scatters light, and the machine reads it as extra hemoglobin. So Hgb, MCH and MCHC are falsely high. Fix: replace the plasma with an equal volume of saline (or use a plasma blank) and rerun the hemoglobin.

3. Platelet clumping. EDTA can trigger antibodies that clump platelets. Clumps aren't counted as platelets, so the platelet count is falsely low. Big clumps can be counted as white cells, so the WBC may be falsely high. Fix: check the smear for clumps, then recollect in sodium citrate.

4. Microclots. A poorly mixed or slow draw lets small clots form, which trap cells (especially platelets) and can block the analyzer. Counts come out low. Fix: recollect. A clotted EDTA sample can't be rescued.

Memory hook: the warning signAn MCHC above about 36-37 is a red flag. Ask: cold agglutinins? Lipemia or high WBC? Spherocytes? Then use the rule of three (Hgb × 3 ≈ Hct) to check your fix worked.

The ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate)

The ESR measures how far red cells fall in a tall tube in one hour. Normally red cells repel each other (they carry a negative surface charge) and fall slowly. In inflammation, proteins like fibrinogen and immunoglobulins cancel that charge, the cells stack into rouleaux, and heavy stacks sink fast. So inflammation and rouleaux raise the ESR.

Things that lower the ESR are cells that can't stack (sickle cells, spherocytes, acanthocytes) and too many cells crowding the tube (polycythemia). Technical errors matter: a tilted tube or a warm room makes it falsely high; vibration disturbs the result; an old sample (over 2 hours at room temperature) gives a falsely low result.

Other special tests

  • G6PD screen (fluorescent spot test): normal G6PD makes NADPH, which glows under UV light. No glow = deficiency. It can be falsely normal right after a hemolytic crisis, because the old, most deficient cells are already gone and young cells have more enzyme.
  • Osmotic fragility: red cells are put into salt solutions that get more and more dilute. Water rushes in and cells swell. Spherocytes have no spare membrane, so they burst sooner: increased fragility in hereditary spherocytosis. Target cells (extra membrane) show decreased fragility.

Flow cytometry markers: the cell's ID badge

Every cell type carries its own set of surface proteins, numbered as "CD" (cluster of differentiation) markers. Flow cytometry tags them with fluorescent antibodies and counts them cell by cell. Learn them in groups:

  • Stem cells: CD34.
  • T cells: CD3 (all T cells), CD4 (helper), CD8 (cytotoxic).
  • B cells: CD19, CD20. CLL adds CD5 and CD23.
  • Myeloid cells: CD13, CD33, CD117.
  • Monocytes: CD14, CD64.
  • Megakaryocytes/platelets: CD41, CD61.
  • PNH: loss of CD55 and CD59.

Flow cytometry also reads two light signals: forward scatter tracks cell size, and side scatter tracks granularity (neutrophils, full of granules, scatter the most).

Molecular tests in hematology

Some blood cancers are defined by a single genetic change, and finding it confirms the diagnosis and guides treatment:

  • BCR-ABL1 (from t(9;22)) = CML. Measured over time by quantitative PCR to check the response to treatment.
  • JAK2 V617F = polycythemia vera (in about 95%), and about half of essential thrombocythemia and myelofibrosis.
  • PML-RARA (from t(15;17)) = APL.

Check your understanding

RBC 2.0, MCV 130, MCHC 40, clumps on the smear. What do you do?

Suspect cold agglutinins. Warm the sample to 37 °C and rerun. The RBC and MCV should return to sensible values and the MCHC should fall into range.

Why does extreme leukocytosis falsely raise hemoglobin?

The huge number of white cells makes the lysed sample cloudy. Cloudiness blocks light, which the analyzer reads as extra hemoglobin.

Name two things that falsely raise the ESR and one that falsely lowers it.

Raise: tilted tube, warm room. Lower: a sample too old (over 2 hours), sickle cells or spherocytes.

Which markers would you expect on CLL cells?

B-cell markers CD19 and CD20 (often dim), plus CD5 and CD23.

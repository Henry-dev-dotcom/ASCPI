A culture can only be as good as the specimen. A badly collected sample grows the wrong organisms, misses the right ones, or sends the doctor down the wrong path. This lesson covers how specimens should be collected and judged, which organisms to expect at each body site, and how results are reported. Exam questions here are often about what to do, not just what grows.

Why the specimen rules exist

Blood cultures. Skin bacteria can fall into the bottle during the draw. To separate real infection from contamination:

  • Clean the skin well (chlorhexidine, let it dry) and disinfect the bottle tops.
  • Draw two sets from two separate sites (each set is an aerobic plus an anaerobic bottle), with enough blood. Volume is the biggest factor in detecting bacteremia.
  • Interpretation: coagulase-negative staph in one bottle out of four is usually a contaminant. The same organism in multiple sets suggests real infection. S. aureus, E. coli, S. pneumoniae or Candida are treated as significant even in one bottle.

CSF. The most urgent specimen in micro. Deliver and process immediately at room temperature, and never refrigerate it: N. meningitidis, H. influenzae and S. pneumoniae die in the cold.

Urine. Urine picks up skin and urethral bacteria on the way out, so collect a midstream clean catch. Quantity separates infection from contamination: ≥100,000 CFU/mL of one organism is the traditional cut-off (lower counts can matter in symptomatic patients or catheter samples). With a 0.001 mL calibrated loop, each colony equals 1,000 CFU/mL. In a suprapubic aspirate (needle through the abdominal wall into the bladder), any growth is significant, because it bypasses the urethra. Refrigerate if delayed, or use a boric acid preservative tube.

Sputum quality. A "sputum" that is mostly saliva is useless. Screen the Gram stain under low power: more than 25 white cells and fewer than 10 squamous epithelial cells per field means the sample came from the lungs. Many squamous cells means mouth contamination, so reject it and ask for a new one.

Expected organisms by body site

Knowing what usually causes infection at each site helps you judge whether a result makes sense:

  • Blood: S. aureus, E. coli, Klebsiella, streptococci, enterococci, Candida. (CoNS is usually a contaminant.)
  • Urine: E. coli by far the most common, then S. saprophyticus (young women), Klebsiella, Proteus, Enterococcus, Pseudomonas (catheters, hospital), Candida.
  • Upper respiratory: S. pyogenes (strep throat), C. diphtheriae, B. pertussis.
  • Lower respiratory: S. pneumoniae (most common bacterial pneumonia), H. influenzae, Moraxella, Klebsiella (alcoholics), Legionella, Mycoplasma, M. tuberculosis, Pneumocystis (AIDS), Pseudomonas in cystic fibrosis.
  • Stool: Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, E. coli pathotypes (O157), Yersinia, Vibrio, C. difficile (after antibiotics).
  • Genital: N. gonorrhoeae, C. trachomatis, T. pallidum, Gardnerella, Trichomonas, Candida, HSV, H. ducreyi (chancroid).
  • Skin and soft tissue: S. aureus, S. pyogenes, Pseudomonas (hot tubs), Pasteurella (cat and dog bites), Vibrio vulnificus (seawater wounds).
  • Bone: S. aureus usually; Salmonella in sickle cell disease.

Meningitis: the cause depends on age

  • Newborns: group B strep, E. coli, Listeria. These come from the mother's vagina and gut at birth.
  • Children: S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae (rare now thanks to the Hib vaccine).
  • Adults: S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis.
  • Older adults: S. pneumoniae, Listeria (weaker cell-mediated immunity), Gram-negative rods.

Vaginitis: three causes, three patterns

Healthy vaginal pH is about 3.8-4.5, kept acidic by lactobacilli. The pH and a wet mount sort out the causes:

  • Bacterial vaginosis (Gardnerella and anaerobes replace lactobacilli): pH above 4.5, a fishy smell when KOH is added (positive "whiff test", from amines), clue cells (epithelial cells coated with bacteria so their edges look fuzzy), and few white cells (it isn't really inflammatory).
  • Candida: normal pH, thick white discharge, budding yeast and pseudohyphae on KOH.
  • Trichomonas: pH above 4.5, motile flagellates on a fresh wet mount (check quickly, they stop moving as the slide cools), many white cells.

Rejecting specimens

A lab must refuse specimens that would give misleading results. Reject and ask for a new sample if it is:

  • unlabeled or mislabeled, leaking, or in the wrong container or transport medium,
  • too old or stored at the wrong temperature,
  • a type that can't be interpreted: a 24-hour sputum (overgrown with mouth flora), a Foley catheter tip (always contaminated by urine), a swab of a surface ulcer for anaerobes.

Transport rules to remember: anaerobic transport for anaerobe cultures; don't refrigerate CSF or gonorrhoea specimens; test stool promptly or use the right preservative.

Reporting

  • Critical values (call the clinician immediately): positive blood culture, positive CSF Gram stain or culture, and a suspected select agent.
  • Reportable diseases must go to public health (the list varies by state): TB, Salmonella, Shigella, measles, syphilis, gonorrhoea, Chlamydia, hepatitis, and the bioterror agents.
  • Corrected reports are clearly marked as corrected, the original result is kept on record, and the clinician is told.

Infection-control screening looks for carriers rather than disease: MRSA (nasal swab, by mecA PCR or chromogenic agar), VRE (rectal swab, bile esculin agar with vancomycin), and ESBL/CRE (rectal swab, selective agar or PCR).

Check your understanding

A sputum Gram stain shows 5 white cells and 40 squamous cells per low-power field. What do you do?

Reject it. It is mostly saliva. Request a new deep-cough specimen.

A urine plated with a 0.001 mL loop grows 45 colonies. What is the count?

45 ÷ 0.001 = 45,000 CFU/mL.

Vaginal pH 5.0, fishy odor with KOH, clue cells. Cause?

Bacterial vaginosis (Gardnerella and anaerobes).

Which three bacteria cause most newborn meningitis?

Group B strep, E. coli and Listeria monocytogenes.

This short lesson ties the blood bank to two bigger topics: transplantation (where blood groups and HLA decide what is safe) and quality assurance (the daily checks and records that keep transfusion safe). Both are tested as practical "what would you do?" questions.

Solid organ transplants

For kidney, heart, liver and other organs, two compatibility layers matter:

  • ABO compatibility: A and B antigens are on blood vessel walls in organs too, not just on red cells. A recipient's anti-A or anti-B would attack an ABO-incompatible organ's blood vessels within minutes (hyperacute rejection).
  • HLA: the recipient may already have antibodies against the donor's HLA (from pregnancy, transfusion or a previous transplant). An HLA crossmatch (recipient serum against donor lymphocytes) detects these preformed antibodies. A positive crossmatch predicts hyperacute rejection, so that organ isn't used. Matching at HLA-DR matters most for kidney survival.

Stem cell (HPC) transplants: the blood type can change

In a stem cell transplant, the patient's marrow is replaced by the donor's, so the patient's blood group gradually becomes the donor's. Because ABO is not used to choose stem cell donors (HLA matters more), ABO mismatches are common and create two kinds of problem:

  • Major mismatch: the recipient has antibodies against the donor's red cells (example: recipient group O, donor group A; the recipient's anti-A attacks the new A cells). Risks: hemolysis of red cells in the graft, delayed red cell recovery or pure red cell aplasia (the antibodies keep destroying new red cell precursors).
  • Minor mismatch: the donor's plasma or lymphocytes carry antibodies against the recipient's cells (example: recipient A, donor O). Lymphocytes transferred with the graft ("passenger lymphocytes") make anti-A and can cause sudden hemolysis 1-2 weeks later.

Choosing blood products during the switch follows one rule: red cells must be compatible with both the recipient's and the donor's plasma, and plasma must be compatible with both the recipient's and the donor's red cells. Group O red cells and group AB plasma and platelets always satisfy it. All cellular products are irradiated.

Worked exampleRecipient group A, donor group O. Red cells: must suit the donor's anti-A and anti-B → group O. Plasma: must not attack the recipient's A cells → group A or AB.

Quality assurance in the blood bank

Blood bank errors can kill, so quality checks are strict and documented:

  • Reagent QC on each day of use: reagent red cells, antisera (anti-A, anti-B, anti-D) and AHG are tested with positive and negative controls every day they are used. A reagent that fails isn't used for patients.
  • Storage equipment: refrigerators (1-6 °C), freezers and platelet incubators/agitators are monitored continuously with alarms, and checked and recorded daily. The alarm must sound before products reach an unacceptable temperature. If a unit has been out of range, quarantine it, investigate, and document; the medical director decides its fate.
  • Keeping samples: the patient's pretransfusion sample and a segment from every unit given are kept, refrigerated, for at least 7 days after transfusion. If a reaction occurs later, they can be retested.
  • Records: donor, patient and transfusion records are kept for the required time (often 5 years or longer; some records much longer), and every unit must be traceable from donor to recipient ("vein to vein").
  • Errors and incidents are reported, investigated for root cause, and corrected. Serious events may be reported to the FDA.

Check your understanding

A group O recipient receives group A stem cells. What kind of mismatch is it, and what risk does it carry?

A major mismatch. The recipient's anti-A can destroy donor red cells and red cell precursors, causing hemolysis, delayed red cell recovery or pure red cell aplasia.

Which red cell and plasma groups are always safe during an ABO-mismatched stem cell transplant?

Group O red cells and group AB plasma (and platelets).

How long are patient samples and unit segments kept after transfusion, and why?

At least 7 days, so they can be retested if a delayed reaction is investigated.

Before any red cells are given, the blood bank checks three things: the patient's ABO and Rh type, whether the patient has any unexpected antibodies (the antibody screen), and whether the chosen unit is compatible (the crossmatch). The tool behind most of this is the antiglobulin (Coombs) test. Understand how it works and the rest of the lesson falls into place.

Why we need antihuman globulin (AHG)

An IgM antibody is a big, five-armed molecule that can grab two red cells at once and clump them on its own. An IgG antibody is small. It sticks to red cells but can't bridge the gap between them (red cells repel each other with their negative surface charge). So IgG-coated cells look normal in the tube.

AHG is an antibody made against human antibody. It links one IgG on one cell to an IgG on the next cell, building a bridge that makes visible clumps. So AHG reveals IgG (and complement) that would otherwise be invisible.

DAT and IAT: same reagent, different question

  • DAT (direct antiglobulin test): patient's red cells are washed and AHG is added straight away. It asks: are these cells already coated in the body (in vivo)? Positive in HDFN (baby's cells coated with mother's antibody), hemolytic transfusion reactions (donor cells coated), autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and drug-induced hemolysis.
  • IAT (indirect antiglobulin test): patient's serum is first incubated with reagent or donor red cells at 37 °C (in vitro), then washed and tested with AHG. It asks: is there antibody in the serum that will coat these cells? Used for the antibody screen, antibody identification, the AHG crossmatch and weak D testing.

Polyspecific AHG contains anti-IgG and anti-C3d (complement). Monospecific reagents show which one is present: IgG-only points to warm antibodies; C3d-only points to cold agglutinins and some drug reactions.

Making sure a negative is truly negative: check cells

After every negative AHG test, add check cells (red cells pre-coated with IgG). They should clump, proving the AHG was present and active. If they don't clump, the test is invalid and must be repeated. The most common reason is poor washing: leftover free serum IgG soaks up (neutralises) the AHG before it can react.

Exam trap: AHG errorsFalse negatives: inadequate washing (AHG neutralised), a delay before reading or adding AHG (antibody falls off the cells), AHG forgotten, under-centrifuging. False positives: over-centrifuging, contaminated reagents or glassware, dirty tubes, a positive DAT on the cells being used.

Enhancement media help IgG antibodies attach faster and more strongly: LISS (low ionic strength saline, which reduces the shielding cloud of ions around cells), PEG (removes water so cells and antibody meet more often), and albumin.

The antibody screen

The patient's serum is tested by IAT against 2 or 3 group O screening cells whose antigens are known. Group O is used so that ABO antibodies don't react. Together, the cells carry all the common, clinically important antigens. If any cell reacts, the patient has an unexpected antibody and needs identification.

Identifying the antibody with a panel

A panel is a set of about 10-16 group O cells, each with a known antigen profile (written on the antigram sheet). Work through it logically:

  1. Look at the autocontrol (patient's serum with their own cells). Negative = alloantibody (to an antigen the patient lacks). Positive = autoantibody or a recent transfusion.
  2. Look at the phase. Reacting at immediate spin or room temperature suggests IgM (cold); at AHG suggests IgG (warm, usually significant).
  3. Rule out. Take each non-reactive cell. Any antigen it carries is unlikely to be the target, so cross it off. Use homozygous cells for antibodies that show dosage (Rh, Kidd, Duffy, MNS), because a weak antibody may miss a heterozygous cell.
  4. Rule in. The remaining antibody should fit every reactive cell.
  5. Confirm with the rule of three. To be 95% confident, have at least 3 antigen-positive cells that react and 3 antigen-negative cells that don't. Then confirm the patient's own cells lack that antigen.
Worked exampleAn Fy(a+b−) cell is non-reactive at AHG, while an Fy(a−b+) cell reacts. The non-reactive cell is homozygous for Fya, so anti-Fya can be ruled out. The reactive Fy(a−b+) cell points toward something else, such as anti-Fyb.

Autoantibodies complicate things. A warm autoantibody (IgG) reacts with every panel cell and the autocontrol, and the DAT is positive. It can hide a real alloantibody, so the lab removes the autoantibody by adsorption (see below). A cold autoantibody (IgM, often anti-I) reacts at room temperature; prewarming serum and cells to 37 °C before mixing avoids it.

The crossmatch and pretransfusion rules

  • Sample age: if the patient was transfused or pregnant in the last 3 months, the sample must be no more than 3 days old, because new antibodies can appear quickly.
  • Immediate-spin crossmatch: donor cells and patient serum spun right away. It only checks ABO compatibility. Allowed when the antibody screen is negative and there is no history of clinically significant antibodies.
  • AHG crossmatch: required when the screen is positive or there is an antibody history. Also give antigen-negative units.
  • Electronic (computer) crossmatch: no tube test, just a validated computer check. Needs a negative screen, no antibody history, and two ABO types on record (one from a separate sample).
  • Emergency release: if there is no time, give group O red cells (D-negative for girls and women of childbearing age), with the physician's signed statement. Testing continues afterward.

RhIG dose: worked out step by step

One 300 µg vial covers 30 mL of fetal whole blood (15 mL of fetal red cells). After delivery, a rosette test screens for a large fetomaternal bleed; if positive, the Kleihauer-Betke test (or flow cytometry) measures it.

  1. Fetal blood (mL) = KB % fetal cells × 50 (the 50 assumes about 5,000 mL of maternal blood volume).
  2. Vials = fetal blood ÷ 30.
  3. Round: decimal 0.5 or more → round up; below 0.5 → round down.
  4. Add one extra vial as a safety margin.
Worked exampleKB shows 1.2% fetal cells. Fetal blood = 1.2 × 50 = 60 mL. 60 ÷ 30 = 2.0 → 2 vials, + 1 = 3 vials.

Reading and grading reactions

Agglutination is graded from 4+ (one solid clump, clear background) through 3+, 2+, 1+, to W+ (weak, tiny clumps) and 0 (no clumps). Hemolysis counts as a positive reaction (the antibody bound complement and lysed the cells). Centrifuging correctly matters: too little lowers sensitivity; too much packs cells so hard they look clumped.

Gel (column) and solid-phase methods are automated and standardised: agglutinated cells are trapped at the top of a gel column, and unagglutinated cells pass to the bottom.

Drug-induced hemolysis: three mechanisms

  • Drug adsorption (penicillin): the drug sticks to the red cell, and antibodies to the drug attack the cell. DAT positive for IgG.
  • Autoantibody (methyldopa): the drug triggers a true warm autoantibody against red cells, which looks just like warm AIHA.
  • Immune complex (quinidine): drug-antibody complexes land on red cells and activate complement, causing intravascular hemolysis. DAT positive for C3d.

Special serologic techniques

  • Titration: serial two-fold dilutions measure antibody strength. The titer is the reciprocal of the highest dilution that still reacts. In pregnancy, an anti-D titer of 16 or more is considered critical.
  • Adsorption: incubating serum with red cells pulls an antibody out of the serum. Used to remove a warm autoantibody (with the patient's own cells or selected donor cells) so hidden alloantibodies can be seen.
  • Elution: frees antibody off red cells (with acid, heat or chemicals) so it can be identified. Used in HDFN, delayed transfusion reactions and AIHA workups.
  • Neutralisation: soluble antigen blocks an antibody, for example Lewis substance (from saliva) for anti-Lewis, hydatid fluid for anti-P1.
  • Lectins (plant extracts that act like antibodies): Dolichos biflorus = anti-A1; Ulex europaeus = anti-H.
  • DTT / 2-ME: break IgM (to see if an antibody is IgM or IgG) and destroy Kell antigens. Chloroquine or EDTA-glycine acid strip IgG off DAT-positive cells so the cells can be antigen typed.
  • Genotyping predicts red cell antigens from DNA, useful when the patient was recently transfused or has a strongly positive DAT.
Exam trap: Wharton's jellyCord blood samples can be contaminated with Wharton's jelly from the umbilical cord, which causes false clumping. Wash the cells at least 6 times and retest.

HLA and platelets: HLA class I (A, B, C) is on all nucleated cells and platelets. Patients who don't respond to platelet transfusions (low post-transfusion count) often have HLA antibodies; they need HLA-matched or crossmatched platelets.

Check your understanding

An antibody screen is negative, but check cells added afterward don't agglutinate. What do you do?

The test is invalid, most often from poor washing. Repeat the test.

When can an immediate-spin crossmatch be used?

When the antibody screen is negative and the patient has no history of clinically significant antibodies.

KB shows 0.8% fetal cells. How many RhIG vials?

0.8 × 50 = 40 mL. 40 ÷ 30 = 1.33 → round down to 1, then add 1 = 2 vials.

Which technique would you use to identify an antibody coating a newborn's red cells in HDFN?

Elution, then test the eluate against panel cells.

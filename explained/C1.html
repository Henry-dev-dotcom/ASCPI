Clinical chemistry measures substances dissolved in blood and body fluids: sugars, proteins, enzymes, salts, hormones and drugs. Almost every chemistry result comes from a handful of instruments, and most calculation questions come from a handful of formulas. Learn how the instruments "see" a substance and you can predict their errors, which is what the exam asks about most.

The 8 big ideas, explained

  1. Beer-Lambert law: absorbance is proportional to concentration. The more colored molecules in the path of light, the more light they absorb. Double the concentration, double the absorbance. That is why one standard of known concentration lets you calculate an unknown.
  2. Glucose falls in an unspun tube. Red and white cells keep eating glucose (glycolysis) at about 5-7% per hour. A gray-top tube contains sodium fluoride, which poisons the enzyme enolase and stops glycolysis. Otherwise, separate the serum within an hour.
  3. Diabetes cut-offs: fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL, 2-hour OGTT ≥200, or HbA1c ≥6.5%.
  4. Troponin is the most specific marker of heart muscle damage, because its cardiac forms (I and T) are found only in heart muscle.
  5. Each enzyme points to an organ: ALT = liver, AST = liver plus heart and muscle, ALP and GGT = bile ducts, lipase = pancreas.
  6. Anion gap = Na − (Cl + HCO3), normally about 8-16. A high gap means an unmeasured acid is present.
  7. Bilirubin type tells you where the problem is: high direct (conjugated) = the bile can't get out (blockage); high indirect = too much made (hemolysis) or poor processing (Gilbert).
  8. High TSH with low free T4 = primary hypothyroidism. The thyroid is failing, so the pituitary shouts louder.

Spectrophotometry: the workhorse

Most chemistry tests turn the substance into something colored, then measure how much light the color absorbs. The path of light is: lamp → monochromator (picks one wavelength) → cuvette (holds the sample) → detector (photodetector) → readout.

  • Transmittance (%T) is the fraction of light that gets through. Absorbance (A) is how much was absorbed. They are linked by A = 2 − log %T. So 100% T means A = 0; 10% T means A = 1; 1% T means A = 2.
  • Beer's law in full is A = a × b × c (absorptivity × light path length × concentration). In practice: Cunknown = (Aunknown ÷ Astandard) × Cstandard.
Worked exampleA 100 mg/dL standard reads A = 0.250. A patient reads A = 0.500. Patient = (0.500 ÷ 0.250) × 100 = 200 mg/dL.

Common errors: stray light (light of the wrong wavelength reaching the detector) makes high absorbances read too low and bends the curve at the top. Dirty or scratched cuvettes, bubbles and fingerprints add false absorbance. Wavelength accuracy is checked with holmium oxide or didymium filters, which have sharp known peaks.

Other instruments and what each one is for

  • Ion-selective electrodes (ISE) measure Na, K and Cl. A membrane lets only one ion through and produces a voltage that depends on that ion's activity. Indirect ISE dilutes the sample first and assumes normal water content. If the plasma is full of lipid or protein, there is less water, so sodium reads falsely low (pseudohyponatremia). Direct ISE (undiluted, used in blood gas machines) avoids this.
  • Flame emission photometry (older method for Na, K, Li): the flame excites atoms and they emit light of their own color.
  • Atomic absorption (metals like lead, zinc, calcium, magnesium): a hollow-cathode lamp made of the metal being tested shines light that only that metal's atoms absorb. Very specific.
  • Fluorometry measures light emitted by a molecule after it absorbs light. It is more sensitive than absorbance because it measures light against a dark background.
  • Nephelometry measures light scattered at an angle by particles such as antigen-antibody complexes (used for immunoglobulins, CRP). Turbidimetry measures the light blocked straight ahead.
  • Osmometry measures osmolality by freezing point depression: more dissolved particles = lower freezing point.
  • GC-MS (gas chromatography-mass spectrometry) separates compounds and identifies them by their mass "fingerprint". It is the gold standard to confirm a positive drug screen.
  • Electrophoresis separates proteins by charge in an electric field at pH 8.6. Serum proteins line up from the anode: albumin, alpha-1, alpha-2, beta, gamma.
Memory hook: absorb, emit or scatter?Spectrophotometry and atomic absorption absorb. Flame photometry and fluorometry emit. Nephelometry scatters. Knowing which one a method uses tells you what can interfere with it.

Standard curves and calibration

To turn absorbance into concentration, the analyzer runs standards (known concentrations) and draws a line of absorbance against concentration. If the line is straight, Beer's law holds and the patient result is read from the line.

  • A reagent blank (reagents without sample) sets zero, so the reagent's own color isn't counted.
  • The line is only trusted between the lowest and highest standards (the linear or reportable range). A result above the top standard must be diluted, rerun, and multiplied by the dilution factor. Never extend the line by guessing.

Quick calculations

  • Dilution factor = total volume ÷ sample volume. 0.5 mL serum + 4.5 mL diluent = 5.0 mL total → 1:10. Multiply the result by 10.
  • Molarity (mol/L) = grams per liter ÷ molecular weight.
  • Percent solution (w/v) = grams per 100 mL. A 10% NaCl solution has 10 g per 100 mL, so 250 mL needs 25 g.
  • Unit conversions: glucose mg/dL ÷ 18 = mmol/L (so 90 mg/dL = 5 mmol/L). Cholesterol mg/dL × 0.0259 = mmol/L.
Worked exampleGlucose is over the linear limit. A 1:4 dilution reads 150 mg/dL. Report 150 × 4 = 600 mg/dL.

Check your understanding

A solution transmits 1% of the light. What is its absorbance?

A = 2 − log 1 = 2 − 0 = 2.0.

Why does a very lipemic sample give a falsely low sodium on indirect ISE but not on direct ISE?

Indirect ISE dilutes a fixed volume and assumes it is about 93% water. Lipid takes up part of that volume, so less sodium-containing water is sampled. Direct ISE measures sodium activity in the water phase without dilution.

Which method confirms a positive urine drug screen?

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (or LC-MS/MS).

Convert 180 mg/dL glucose to mmol/L.

180 ÷ 18 = 10 mmol/L.

Mycobacteria and fungi are slow, tough organisms that need their own methods. Mycobacteria have a waxy wall that resists ordinary stains and lets them survive harsh treatments. Fungi are identified mostly by how they look, both the colony and the microscopic structures. This lesson explains the classification schemes so the details make sense.

Mycobacteria: the waxy-walled rods

The mycobacterial cell wall is packed with mycolic acids, long waxy fats. This explains almost everything about them:

  • They resist Gram stain but, once stained with carbolfuchsin, hold the dye against acid-alcohol: acid-fast.
  • They survive harsh chemicals, which lets the lab digest and decontaminate specimens (with NALC-NaOH). The NALC thins the mucus; NaOH kills faster-growing contaminating bacteria, which would otherwise overgrow the slow mycobacteria.
  • They grow slowly (M. tuberculosis takes about 2-8 weeks), because nutrients pass slowly through the waxy wall.

M. tuberculosis

The cause of TB, spread by coughed-up droplets. Identification clues:

  • Slow grower with rough, buff ("cauliflower") colonies on Löwenstein-Jensen.
  • Niacin positive and nitrate positive.
  • Cording: cells line up in rope-like strands (cord factor).
  • Today, isolates are usually identified by nucleic acid probes, PCR (which also detects rifampin resistance) or MALDI-TOF.

Cultures are handled in BSL-3 conditions because aerosols spread infection. TB testing of patients (skin test, IGRA) is in the immunology lessons.

Non-tuberculous mycobacteria: the Runyon groups

Other mycobacteria are grouped by growth speed and pigment. Test pigment by growing two cultures, one in the dark and one exposed to light:

  • Group I, photochromogens: yellow pigment only after light exposure. M. kansasii (TB-like lung disease) and M. marinum (skin lesions from fish tanks or pools; prefers 30 °C, because it lives in cooler skin).
  • Group II, scotochromogens: pigment even in the dark ("scoto" = dark). M. gordonae (tap water, usually a contaminant), M. scrofulaceum (neck lymph nodes in children).
  • Group III, nonphotochromogens: no pigment. M. avium complex (MAC), the classic infection in advanced HIV/AIDS.
  • Group IV, rapid growers: grow in under 7 days. M. fortuitum, M. chelonae, M. abscessus (skin and surgical-site infections).

M. leprae (leprosy) can't be grown in lab culture; it is diagnosed from skin biopsies, and grows in armadillos and mouse footpads.

Memory hookPhotochromogens need photons (light). Scotochromogens color in the dark ("scoto" = shadow). Group III is "non" (no color).

Fungi: yeasts, molds and dimorphs

Fungi come in two basic forms:

  • Yeasts: single round cells that reproduce by budding. Colonies look creamy, like bacteria.
  • Molds: long threads (hyphae) forming fuzzy colonies. Hyphae may have cross-walls (septate) or not (aseptate). Molds are identified by their spores (conidia) and how they are arranged.

Dimorphic fungi switch between the two depending on temperature: mold at 25 °C (in soil) and yeast at 37 °C (in the body). Remember "mold in the cold, yeast in the heat."

The dimorphic fungi (and where they live)

These cause lung infections when spores are inhaled, and each has a home region and a signature structure:

  • Histoplasma capsulatum: Ohio and Mississippi river valleys, bird and bat droppings (caves, chicken coops). Mold shows tuberculate macroconidia (spiky balls). In the body it is a tiny yeast hiding inside macrophages.
  • Blastomyces dermatitidis: also eastern and central US. In tissue, large yeast with broad-based budding and a thick wall.
  • Coccidioides immitis: the deserts of the southwestern US ("Valley fever"). The mold makes barrel-shaped arthroconidia that are highly infectious; plates must be handled only in a biosafety cabinet. In tissue it forms spherules filled with endospores, not a budding yeast.
  • Sporothrix schenckii: "rose gardener's disease", entering through thorn pricks and spreading up the lymph channels. Cigar-shaped yeast in tissue.

Yeasts

  • Candida albicans: the most common yeast infection (thrush, vaginitis, line infections). Its quick ID test is the germ tube: in serum at 37 °C for 2-3 hours it sprouts small tubes with no pinch at the base. It also forms chlamydospores on cornmeal agar.
  • Cryptococcus neoformans: a yeast with a thick polysaccharide capsule (seen with India ink), urease positive, found in pigeon droppings. Causes meningitis in AIDS patients. The cryptococcal antigen test on CSF or serum is the most sensitive diagnosis.

Molds you must recognise

  • Aspergillus: septate hyphae with acute-angle (about 45°) branching. Causes allergic disease, fungus balls in old lung cavities, and invasive infection in patients with low neutrophils.
  • Mucor and Rhizopus (Mucorales): broad, ribbon-like hyphae with few or no septa and right-angle branching. Invade blood vessels and destroy tissue, classically in uncontrolled diabetics (ketoacidosis), starting in the sinuses.

Dermatophytes: skin, hair and nails

Dermatophytes feed on keratin, so they infect only skin, hair and nails (ringworm, athlete's foot):

  • Trichophyton: skin, hair and nails (all three).
  • Microsporum: skin and hair (not nails).
  • Epidermophyton: skin and nails (not hair).
Memory hook: which dermatophyte skips whatMicrosporum skips the nails; Epidermophyton skips the hair; Trichophyton does all three.

Check your understanding

An AFB isolate turns yellow only after exposure to light. Which Runyon group, and a likely species?

Group I photochromogen, such as M. kansasii (or M. marinum).

Why are respiratory samples treated with NALC-NaOH before mycobacterial culture?

NALC thins mucus and NaOH kills faster-growing contaminating bacteria. Mycobacteria survive because of their waxy wall.

Tissue shows spherules containing endospores in a patient from Arizona. Organism?

Coccidioides immitis.

Broad aseptate hyphae with right-angle branching in a diabetic's sinus biopsy. Which group?

Mucorales (Mucor or Rhizopus).

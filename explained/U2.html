The microscope shows what the dipstick can only hint at: actual cells, casts and crystals. Casts are the most valuable finding, because they can only form inside the kidney's tubules, so whatever is trapped in a cast tells you what is happening inside the kidney. This lesson explains how to prepare the sediment and how to interpret each element.

Preparing the sediment

Standardisation matters, because results are reported as counts per field:

  1. Mix the urine and spin 10-12 mL at about 400 RCF for 5 minutes.
  2. Pour off the supernatant, leaving a fixed volume (about 0.5-1 mL), and resuspend gently.
  3. Examine under low power to scan for casts (report per LPF; they collect near the coverslip edges) and under high power to count cells (per HPF).

Reduce the light (or use phase contrast) to see pale hyaline casts and mucus. Polarised light shows crystals and the Maltese cross pattern of cholesterol droplets. Useful stains: Sternheimer-Malbin (general cells and casts), Hansel (eosinophils, for drug-induced interstitial nephritis), Sudan III / Oil Red O (fat; stains triglyceride, not cholesterol), and Prussian blue (hemosiderin).

Cells

  • Red cells: small, pale discs without a nucleus. In concentrated urine they shrink (crenated); in dilute urine they swell and lose hemoglobin (ghost cells). Dysmorphic red cells (odd shapes, blebs) have squeezed through the glomerulus and point to glomerular bleeding. Normal-shaped cells suggest bleeding lower down (stones, tumours, infection). Don't confuse red cells with yeast (which bud) or oil droplets (which vary in size). Acetic acid lyses red cells but not yeast.
  • White cells: mostly neutrophils, with a granular, lobed look. More than a few per HPF = infection or inflammation. In dilute urine they swell and their granules move (Brownian motion), sparkling as "glitter cells".
  • Squamous epithelial cells: large, flat, with a small nucleus. They come from the urethra and vagina, so many of them simply mean the specimen is contaminated.
  • Transitional (urothelial) cells: round or pear-shaped, from the renal pelvis, ureters and bladder. Increased after catheterisation; clusters with odd nuclei may suggest a tumour.
  • Renal tubular epithelial (RTE) cells: the most significant epithelial cell. They come from inside the kidney, so many of them mean tubular injury (acute tubular necrosis, toxins, rejection).
  • Oval fat bodies: RTE cells (or macrophages) filled with fat droplets, which show a Maltese cross under polarised light. They mean fat is leaking into urine: nephrotic syndrome.

Casts: moulds of the tubule

Casts form in the distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct, where the tubular cells secrete Tamm-Horsfall protein (uromodulin). When urine flows slowly, is acidic and concentrated, this protein gels into a cylinder shaped like the tubule. Anything floating nearby (cells, fat, debris) gets trapped inside. When the cast washes out, it carries a sample of what was in the kidney.

Why casts locate the problemA red cell in urine could have come from anywhere: kidney, ureter, bladder or contamination. A red cell inside a cast must have been in the renal tubule when the cast formed, so the bleeding is from the kidney (glomerulus). That is why casts are so valuable.
  • Hyaline: just protein, no cells. A few are normal, especially after exercise, dehydration, fever or stress.
  • Red cell cast: glomerular bleeding, the hallmark of glomerulonephritis. Always significant.
  • White cell cast: inflammation or infection in the kidney: pyelonephritis, interstitial nephritis. Separates kidney infection from simple cystitis.
  • Epithelial (RTE) cell cast: tubular damage.
  • Granular cast: cells inside a cast that broke down over time; non-specific. Many coarse "muddy brown" granular casts suggest acute tubular necrosis.
  • Waxy cast: the final stage of breakdown. Brittle, glassy, with cracked edges, formed after prolonged stasis: chronic kidney failure.
  • Fatty cast: contains fat droplets (Maltese crosses): nephrotic syndrome.
  • Broad cast: formed in widened, damaged tubules or collecting ducts: chronic kidney failure ("renal failure casts").
Memory hook: the cast ladderCellular cast → (time and stasis) → coarse granular → fine granular → waxy. The further down the ladder, the longer the urine sat still, and the worse the kidney.

Crystals: let pH guide you

Crystals form when a substance is concentrated beyond its solubility, and solubility depends on pH. So always check the pH first.

In acid urine:

  • Uric acid: yellow-brown, rhombic plates or rosettes; strongly polarise. Seen in gout and chemotherapy (cell breakdown).
  • Calcium oxalate: the "envelope" (dihydrate, colorless square with an X) is common and usually harmless (diet: spinach, vitamin C). The oval or dumbbell (monohydrate) form in large numbers suggests ethylene glycol poisoning. Also seen in neutral urine.
  • Abnormal crystals, always reported: cystine (colorless hexagons; inherited cystinuria; confirm with cyanide-nitroprusside), leucine (spheres with concentric rings) and tyrosine (fine needles in sheaves), both in severe liver disease; cholesterol (flat plates with a notched corner; nephrotic syndrome); bilirubin (yellow needles); sulfonamide (drug crystals, sheaves).

In alkaline urine:

  • Triple phosphate (struvite): colorless "coffin lid" prisms. Formed when urea-splitting bacteria (Proteus) make urine alkaline; linked to infection stones.
  • Ammonium biurate: yellow-brown "thorny apple" spheres, often in old specimens.
  • Calcium phosphate (prisms, plates) and calcium carbonate (small dumbbells; fizz with acetic acid).
  • Amorphous phosphate: white granular precipitate (dissolves in acetic acid). Compare amorphous urates in acid urine: pink "brick dust" that dissolves with heat.
Exam trap: crystal look-alikesCystine and uric acid can both look hexagonal. Uric acid is yellow-brown and strongly polarises; cystine is colorless, with weak polarisation. Confirm cystine with cyanide-nitroprusside.

Other findings

  • Yeast: oval, budding, no central pallor; may form pseudohyphae (common in diabetics).
  • Trichomonas: pear-shaped with jerky motility; must be seen moving to identify confidently.
  • Sperm, mucus threads (normal), and Schistosoma haematobium eggs (terminal spine) in travellers with blood in their urine.

Check your understanding

Why does a red cell cast prove bleeding comes from the kidney?

Casts form only inside the renal tubules. A red cell inside a cast had to be in the tubule, so it came from the glomerulus.

Which cast separates pyelonephritis from cystitis?

The white cell cast, which shows inflammation inside the kidney.

Colorless hexagonal crystals in acid urine. Which crystal, and how do you confirm it?

Cystine; confirm with the cyanide-nitroprusside test.

Coffin-lid crystals in alkaline urine with a positive nitrite. What does this suggest?

Triple phosphate (struvite) crystals from an infection with urea-splitting bacteria such as Proteus.

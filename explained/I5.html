Immunology tests all rely on one event: an antibody binding its antigen. What differs is how you see that binding: as a visible precipitate, as clumping, as color from an enzyme, as fluorescence, or as scattered light. Understand each method's principle and you can predict when it gives a false result. This lesson also covers transplant immunology.

Precipitation: the lattice and the zones

When soluble antigen and antibody meet, each antibody can grab two antigens and each antigen can be grabbed by several antibodies, so they build a large lattice that becomes visible as a precipitate. The lattice forms best when the amounts are balanced, the zone of equivalence.

  • Prozone = too much antibody. Every antigen gets its own antibodies, no cross-links form, and the result is falsely negative. Fix: dilute the serum.
  • Postzone = too much antigen. Every antibody arm gets its own antigen; again no lattice, again a false negative. Fix: repeat later when more antibody is present, or dilute the antigen.
Memory hookProzone = pro-antibody (antibody excess). Postzone = antigen excess. The "hook effect" in sandwich immunoassays is the same idea as postzone: so much antigen that it saturates both antibodies.

Classic precipitation methods:

  • Ouchterlony (double diffusion): antigen and antibody diffuse toward each other from wells in a gel, and the pattern of lines shows whether two antigens are identical, partly identical or unrelated.
  • Radial immunodiffusion (RID): antibody is in the gel; antigen diffuses out from a well and forms a ring. The ring size reflects the antigen concentration. It was used for IgG, IgA and IgM.
  • Immunofixation: proteins are separated by electrophoresis, then specific antisera "fix" (precipitate) the band. It identifies monoclonal proteins.

Agglutination: clumping particles

If the antigen is on a particle (a red cell, a latex bead, a bacterium), antibody makes the particles clump, which is easy to see. It is very sensitive and used in blood typing, latex tests (cryptococcal antigen, RF) and the Monospot. Prozone and postzone apply here too.

Titers measure antibody strength: make serial two-fold dilutions (1:2, 1:4, 1:8...) and find the highest dilution that still reacts. The titer is the reciprocal: reacting at 1:64 = titer 64. A fourfold rise between acute and convalescent samples is significant.

Labeled immunoassays: ELISA and friends

For tiny amounts, a label is attached to an antibody or antigen to make a signal: an enzyme (ELISA, makes color), a fluorescent dye, or a chemiluminescent molecule (makes light). Three formats matter:

  • Sandwich (two-site): for antigens big enough to bind two antibodies. A capture antibody holds the antigen; a labeled detection antibody binds on top. More antigen = more signal. Used for hCG, TSH, troponin. Very high antigen can cause a hook effect (falsely low).
  • Indirect: for detecting antibody in a patient. Antigen is fixed to the well, patient antibody binds, then a labeled anti-human IgG detects it. More antibody = more signal. Used for HIV and hepatitis antibody screens.
  • Competitive: for small molecules (drugs, hormones like T4). Patient analyte and a labeled version of the analyte compete for a limited number of antibody sites. The more patient analyte, the less label binds: more analyte = less signal (inverse relationship).
Exam trap: competitive assays run backwardsIn a competitive assay a low signal means a high concentration. Biotin interference also runs in opposite directions in the two formats: falsely low in sandwich assays, falsely high in competitive ones.

Other techniques

  • Western blot: proteins (such as viral proteins) are separated by size on a gel, transferred (blotted) onto a membrane, and then patient serum is added. Bands show which specific proteins the patient has antibodies to. It is used to confirm Lyme disease.
  • Immunofluorescence: direct (a labeled antibody binds antigen in tissue) or indirect (patient antibody binds first, then labeled anti-human IgG). Indirect IF on HEp-2 cells is the ANA test.
  • Nephelometry: measures light scattered by immune complexes; used for IgG, IgA, IgM, complement, CRP and others. Fast and automated.
  • Monoclonal antibodies are produced by hybridoma technology: a single antibody-making B cell is fused with an immortal myeloma cell, giving an endless supply of one identical antibody. Most modern immunoassay reagents are monoclonal.

Predictive value depends on prevalence

Sensitivity and specificity belong to the test. Predictive values also depend on how common the disease is in the people tested. When a disease is rare, even a very specific test gives many false positives compared with true positives, so the positive predictive value (PPV) is low. Test a high-risk group (high prevalence) and PPV rises. That's why screening tests in low-risk populations are always followed by confirmatory tests.

Transplant immunology

The immune system recognises a transplanted organ as foreign mainly through HLA (the human MHC). The more HLA differences, the stronger the rejection.

  • HLA matching: HLA-A, -B and -DR matter most, with DR the most important for kidney grafts.
  • Crossmatch: recipient serum is tested against donor lymphocytes (by complement-dependent cytotoxicity or flow cytometry) to detect preformed anti-HLA antibodies. A positive crossmatch predicts hyperacute rejection.

Types of rejection, by timing and mechanism:

  • Hyperacute: minutes to hours; preformed antibodies (ABO or HLA) attack the graft's blood vessels at once.
  • Acute: days to weeks; mainly T-cell mediated (and sometimes antibody). Usually treatable with stronger immunosuppression.
  • Chronic: months to years; slow scarring and narrowing of vessels; hard to treat.

Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) is rejection in reverse: donor lymphocytes attack the recipient. It happens when immune cells are transferred to someone who can't reject them: a stem cell transplant, or an unirradiated blood product given to an immunocompromised patient. Skin, gut and liver are hit.

Check your understanding

A latex cryptococcal antigen test is negative but India ink is full of yeast. What happened, and what do you do?

Antigen excess prevented lattice formation (a prozone/hook-type effect). Dilute the CSF and repeat.

In a competitive immunoassay, the signal is very low. Is the analyte concentration high or low?

High. More patient analyte displaces the labeled analyte, lowering the signal.

Serial two-fold dilutions react up to 1:64. What is the titer?

64.

A kidney is rejected within minutes of the vessels being connected. Mechanism?

Hyperacute rejection from preformed antibodies (ABO or anti-HLA) attacking the graft's vessels.

Laboratories are regulated because wrong results harm patients, and labs are dangerous places to work. This lesson explains who makes the rules and what they require (regulation), how to protect yourself and others from biological, chemical and fire hazards (safety), and what to do the moment something goes wrong (emergencies).

Who does what: the organisations

Think of it as law vs inspection vs guidance:

  • CLIA '88 (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments, run by CMS): the federal law covering every lab that tests human samples for health care. It sets test complexity levels (waived, moderate, high), personnel qualifications, QC and proficiency testing.
  • CAP (College of American Pathologists): accredits and inspects labs (every 2 years). Labs accredited by CAP have "deemed status": they are treated as meeting CLIA.
  • The Joint Commission: accredits hospitals, including their labs. COLA accredits mainly physician office labs.
  • FDA: clears or approves tests, instruments, reagents and regulates blood products and blood banks.
  • OSHA: protects workers: the bloodborne pathogens standard, chemical hygiene and hazard communication.
  • CLSI: writes consensus standards and guidelines (order of draw, susceptibility breakpoints, method evaluation). Guidance, not law, but widely required by inspectors.
  • AABB: standards for blood banks and transfusion.
  • CDC: disease surveillance, infection-control guidance, and it advises on CLIA.
  • ISO 15189: the international quality standard for medical labs.
Memory hookCLIA = the law. CAP / TJC = the inspectors. OSHA = your safety. FDA = the products. CLSI = the how-to manual.

Proficiency testing (PT)

PT checks a lab's accuracy from the outside. An approved provider sends unknown samples, the lab tests them and reports results, and the provider grades them against other labs.

  • Typically 3 events a year, 5 samples per event.
  • Passing score: 80% for most tests, but 100% for ABO and Rh typing and compatibility testing (no room for error).
  • PT samples must be tested like patient samples (same staff, same methods). Never send PT samples to another lab or discuss results with other labs before the deadline. That is "PT referral", a serious CLIA violation that can cost a lab its certificate.

Waived tests (simple tests with little risk of error: urine dipstick, glucose meter, rapid strep, urine hCG) need only a certificate of waiver and must follow the manufacturer's instructions exactly.

Record retention (typical US requirements, check local rules): patient test records and QC 2 years; blood bank records 5 years or longer; pathology reports 10 years.

Biological safety

Standard precautions treat all blood and body fluids from every patient as potentially infectious, because you can't tell who is infected by looking.

  • PPE: gloves, fluid-resistant lab coat, and face/eye protection when splashes are possible.
  • Hand hygiene: alcohol rub is fine for clean hands, but use soap and water when hands are visibly dirty and after contact with C. difficile (alcohol doesn't kill its spores).
  • Sharps: never recap needles; drop them straight into a puncture-resistant sharps container, and replace it when it is two-thirds full.
  • OSHA bloodborne pathogens standard: employers must offer hepatitis B vaccine free (within 10 days of starting), provide PPE and training, and keep an exposure control plan reviewed every year.

Blood spills: put on gloves, cover with absorbent material, flood with freshly made 1:10 bleach (about 0.5% hypochlorite; diluted bleach loses strength within a day), leave for at least 10 minutes, wipe up and discard as biohazard waste.

Biosafety levels match containment to danger:

  • BSL-1: organisms not known to cause disease in healthy adults.
  • BSL-2: the standard clinical lab: S. aureus, Salmonella, hepatitis B, HIV in clinical specimens. Biosafety cabinet for splash or aerosol work.
  • BSL-3: organisms spread by air that cause serious disease: M. tuberculosis cultures, Brucella, Coccidioides, Francisella. Controlled access and negative-pressure rooms.
  • BSL-4: exotic, deadly agents with no treatment (Ebola): full-body positive-pressure suits.

Chemical safety

  • Every hazardous chemical must have a Safety Data Sheet (SDS), which lists hazards, PPE, first aid and spill response. Staff must be able to reach it.
  • NFPA 704 diamond (on doors and containers): blue = health, red = flammability, yellow = reactivity, white = special hazards (W with a line = reacts with water; OX = oxidiser). Each is rated 0 (none) to 4 (severe).
  • Always add acid to water, never water to acid. Adding water to concentrated acid releases heat so fast it can boil and spatter. ("Do as you oughta, add acid to water.")
  • Store acids and bases apart, flammables in approved safety cabinets, and limit quantities on the bench.

Fire safety

Two acronyms:

  • RACE (what to do): Rescue anyone in danger, pull the Alarm, Contain the fire (close doors), Extinguish if it is small and safe (or Evacuate).
  • PASS (how to use an extinguisher): Pull the pin, Aim at the base of the fire, Squeeze the handle, Sweep side to side.

Fire classes: A = ordinary combustibles (paper, wood: "Ash"), B = flammable liquids ("Barrel"), C = electrical ("Current"; never use water), D = combustible metals.

Waste and shipping

  • Biohazard waste is decontaminated by autoclaving (steam at 121 °C, 15 psi, for 30 minutes or more) or incineration. Sharps go in rigid containers. Chemical waste is sorted by hazard class.
  • Shipping infectious specimens uses triple packaging: a leak-proof primary container, a leak-proof secondary container with absorbent material, and a rigid outer package. Category A (can cause life-threatening disease, such as Ebola cultures) is UN2814; Category B (most diagnostic specimens) is UN3373.

Emergency procedures: what to do first

  • Needlestick or cut: wash with soap and water immediately (don't squeeze, and don't use bleach on skin), report at once, and get evaluated. The source patient is tested if possible. HIV post-exposure prophylaxis works best when started within hours.
  • Splash to eyes, nose or mouth: flush with water or the eyewash for 15 minutes, then report.
  • Chemical on skin: remove contaminated clothing and rinse for at least 15 minutes (safety shower for large areas).
  • Spills: use the correct spill kit; mercury needs special cleanup.
  • Know where the alarms, exits, extinguishers, eyewash and shower are before you need them.

Check your understanding

Which agency issues CLIA regulations, and which accredits labs on its behalf?

CMS administers CLIA. Accrediting bodies such as CAP and The Joint Commission inspect labs and have deemed status.

What score is needed to pass proficiency testing for ABO grouping?

100%. (Most other analytes need 80%.)

The red section of the NFPA diamond shows a 4. What does it mean?

Extremely flammable.

A technologist gets a needlestick. What is the first step?

Wash the site with soap and water immediately, then report it and get evaluated.

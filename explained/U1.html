A routine urinalysis has three parts: the physical exam (color, clarity, specific gravity), the chemical exam (the dipstick), and the microscopic exam (next lesson). Urine is easy to collect but changes quickly, and each dipstick pad has its own chemistry with known interferences. This lesson explains why each result happens, which is how the exam asks about it.

The 8 big ideas, explained

  1. Test within 2 hours or refrigerate. Bacteria keep growing and metabolising, so urine changes on the bench (see below).
  2. Blood pad positive but no red cells seen = free hemoglobin or myoglobin (the pad detects heme, not intact cells).
  3. Nitrite + leukocyte esterase together point to a bacterial UTI.
  4. The protein pad mainly sees albumin. Sulfosalicylic acid (SSA) precipitates all proteins, including Bence Jones light chains.
  5. Casts tell you where in the kidney the problem is: RBC cast = glomerulonephritis, WBC cast = pyelonephritis, fatty cast = nephrotic syndrome, waxy cast = chronic kidney failure.
  6. Crystals depend on pH: acid urine gives uric acid, calcium oxalate, cystine; alkaline urine gives triple phosphate, ammonium biurate, amorphous phosphate.
  7. CSF tubes: tube 1 chemistry, tube 2 microbiology, tube 3 cell count.
  8. Joint crystals: gout (urate) = needles, strongly negatively birefringent; pseudogout (CPPD) = rhomboids, weakly positively birefringent.

Collecting the right specimen

  • First morning: the most concentrated, so best for detecting small amounts of protein, nitrite (urine sat in the bladder all night) and hCG (pregnancy).
  • Random: routine screening.
  • Midstream clean catch: for culture. Cleaning and letting the first part go flushes out urethral bacteria.
  • Timed 24-hour: for things released unevenly through the day (protein, creatinine clearance, hormones). Start by emptying the bladder and discarding that urine, collect everything for 24 hours, and include the final void at the end. (The first void belongs to the previous period.)

Why urine changes if left standing

Picture bacteria living in the warm urine:

  • Bacteria multiply (false high counts on culture) and eat glucose, so glucose falls.
  • Many bacteria split urea into ammonia, so the pH rises. Alkaline urine then dissolves casts and lyses cells.
  • Ketones evaporate (acetone) or are broken down, so they fall.
  • Bilirubin breaks down in light; urobilinogen oxidises to urobilin, so both fall.
  • Nitrite can rise (more bacteria converting nitrate) and turbidity increases.

Refrigeration slows this but can cause amorphous crystals to precipitate. Preservatives for special purposes: boric acid (culture transport), formalin (keeps cells and casts), thymol, sodium fluoride (stops glycolysis).

Physical examination: color and clarity

Color clues are about what pigment is present:

  • Dark yellow-amber with yellow foam when shaken: bilirubin.
  • Red and cloudy: intact red cells (they scatter light). Red and clear: a dissolved pigment: hemoglobin, myoglobin, porphyrins or beets.
  • Port-wine: porphyrins. Brown or black: old blood (methemoglobin), melanin, or homogentisic acid in alkaptonuria (darkens on standing).
  • Blue-green: Pseudomonas infection or methylene blue dye.
  • Milky: fat (chyluria), pus or amorphous phosphates.
  • Cloudy from cells, bacteria, mucus, or amorphous crystals: urates (pink "brick dust", acid urine, dissolve with heat) or phosphates (white, alkaline urine, dissolve with acetic acid).

Odor: fruity = ketones; maple syrup = maple syrup urine disease; mousy = PKU; ammonia = old sample or infection.

Specific gravity: how concentrated is the urine?

Specific gravity (SG) compares urine's density with water's. It shows whether the kidney can concentrate and dilute urine. Normal random urine is about 1.003-1.035.

  • A refractometer measures how much light bends, which reflects all dissolved solids. Glucose and protein raise the reading, and so does X-ray contrast dye (giving impossible values above 1.040).
  • The dipstick SG pad responds only to ions (it releases H+ in proportion to ionic strength), so glucose and protein don't affect it.
  • Osmolality (number of particles) is the best true measure of concentrating ability.
  • A SG stuck around 1.010 on repeated samples (isosthenuria) means the kidney can neither concentrate nor dilute, a sign of severe kidney damage. 1.010 is the SG of the plasma filtrate.

The dipstick: each pad's chemistry and its traps

  • Glucose (glucose oxidase-peroxidase): specific for glucose. Appears when blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold (about 160-180 mg/dL), or with a low threshold from a tubular defect. Vitamin C (a reducing agent) gives false negatives. Clinitest (copper reduction) detects any reducing sugar, such as galactose in infants with galactosemia.
  • Ketones (nitroprusside): detects acetoacetate (and acetone), not beta-hydroxybutyrate.
  • Protein ("protein error of indicators"): the dye changes color when protein binds at a fixed pH. It is most sensitive to albumin. Very alkaline urine (pH 9) overwhelms the buffer and gives a false positive. It misses Bence Jones proteins.
  • Blood (pseudoperoxidase): the heme in red cells, hemoglobin and myoglobin acts like an enzyme on the pad. Intact red cells give speckles; free hemoglobin gives uniform color. Vitamin C gives false negatives.
  • Bilirubin (diazo): detects only conjugated bilirubin (the water-soluble form). Light destroys it.
  • Urobilinogen (Ehrlich reagent): high in hemolysis and liver disease; absent in complete bile duct obstruction.
  • Nitrite (Griess reaction): many Gram-negative bacteria (especially Enterobacterales) convert dietary nitrate to nitrite. A positive needs bacteria that can do this and about 4 hours in the bladder. False negatives: Gram-positives like Enterococcus, urine not held long enough, vitamin C.
  • Leukocyte esterase: an enzyme from neutrophils (and monocytes), so it detects pyuria even if the cells have lysed. It does not detect lymphocytes.
  • pH (methyl red and bromthymol blue): normal range 4.5-8.0.
Exam trap: vitamin CAscorbic acid is a strong reducing agent. It interferes with the reactions on the glucose, blood, bilirubin, nitrite and leukocyte esterase pads, giving false negatives. Ask about vitamin C if results don't fit.

Hemoglobin or myoglobin? Both make the blood pad positive with no red cells. Check the patient's plasma: pink plasma = hemoglobin (hemolysis; haptoglobin binds a lot first, and the excess colors the plasma). Clear plasma = myoglobin (it is small and cleared into urine quickly). Myoglobinuria comes with a very high CK after muscle injury.

Confirmatory tests

  • SSA (sulfosalicylic acid): precipitates all proteins, including globulins and Bence Jones. Dipstick negative + SSA positive → think light chains.
  • Clinitest: reducing sugars (galactose, lactose, fructose). Positive Clinitest + negative glucose pad in a baby → galactosemia.
  • Ictotest: confirms bilirubin (more sensitive than the pad). Acetest: ketones.
  • Ferric chloride: green color in PKU (phenylketones) and with other metabolites.
  • Hoesch or Watson-Schwartz: porphobilinogen (acute intermittent porphyria).
  • Cyanide-nitroprusside: cystine (cystinuria).

Check your understanding

A urine sat at room temperature for 4 hours. Which results change, and how?

pH rises (ammonia from urea), glucose and ketones fall, bilirubin and urobilinogen fall, bacteria increase, and cells and casts break down.

Dipstick protein negative, SSA strongly positive. What protein might be present?

Bence Jones protein (free light chains), which the albumin-sensitive pad misses.

Positive blood pad, no red cells, clear plasma, CK 25,000. Cause?

Myoglobinuria from rhabdomyolysis.

A patient with an Enterococcus UTI has a negative nitrite. Why?

Enterococcus (a Gram-positive) doesn't reduce nitrate to nitrite.

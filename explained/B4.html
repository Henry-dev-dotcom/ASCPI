A donated unit of whole blood is rarely given as it is. It is spun and split into components (red cells, plasma, platelets, cryoprecipitate), and each one is stored differently because each one survives differently. This lesson explains the reasoning behind storage rules, donor rules, quality checks, and when each product is actually used.

Making components from one donation

About 450 mL (± 10%) of blood is collected into a bag with about 63 mL of anticoagulant-preservative. The arm is scrubbed (chlorhexidine or iodine), and the first 30-40 mL is diverted into a side pouch, because that first portion carries skin bacteria that the needle may have picked up.

A gentle spin separates red cells from platelet-rich plasma; a hard spin then separates platelets from plasma. Plasma frozen quickly becomes fresh frozen plasma (FFP). Thawing FFP slowly in the cold leaves a cold-insoluble precipitate: cryoprecipitate.

Storage: each component has its own rules

  • Red cells: 1-6 °C. Cold slows red cell metabolism and bacterial growth. Shelf life depends on the preservative: CPD 21 days, CPDA-1 35 days (adenine helps cells keep making ATP), and additive solutions such as AS-1 42 days. One unit raises an adult's hemoglobin about 1 g/dL (Hct about 3%).
  • Frozen red cells: glycerol protects cells from ice crystals; stored ≤ −65 °C for 10 years. Used for rare blood types. After thawing and washing out the glycerol, use within 24 hours (the system is opened).
  • Platelets: 20-24 °C with constant gentle agitation, for 5 days (up to 7 with extra bacterial testing). Platelets stored cold are quickly cleared from the circulation, and agitation keeps gas exchange going (stagnant platelets produce lactic acid and the pH drops). The catch: room temperature also lets bacteria grow, so platelets carry the highest risk of bacterial contamination. One random (whole-blood-derived) unit raises the count about 5,000-10,000/µL; one apheresis unit 30,000-60,000/µL.
  • FFP: ≤ −18 °C for 1 year. It contains all the clotting factors. Thawed at 30-37 °C; once thawed, keep at 1-6 °C and use within 24 hours (or relabel as "thawed plasma" for up to 5 days; factors V and VIII slowly fall).
  • Cryoprecipitate: ≤ −18 °C for 1 year. Rich in fibrinogen, factor VIII, vWF, factor XIII and fibronectin. After thawing, a single unit must be used within 6 hours; a pool (open system) within 4 hours.
  • Granulocytes: 20-24 °C, no agitation, 24 hours at most. Must be irradiated and given as soon as possible.
  • Washed red cells: washing removes plasma proteins, but opens the bag, so use within 24 hours. For patients with severe allergic reactions or IgA deficiency with anti-IgA.
The storage lesion: why old red cells aren't perfectAs red cells sit in the fridge, they lose 2,3-DPG (the molecule that helps hemoglobin release oxygen), so the oxygen curve shifts left and old cells hold on to oxygen tightly at first. The cells' sodium-potassium pumps slow down, so potassium leaks out into the plasma; that matters for newborns and massive transfusion. pH also falls and some cells hemolyse.

Modifications: leukoreduced and irradiated

  • Leukoreduced (fewer than 5 × 10⁶ white cells per unit, usually by filter): white cells cause many problems. Removing them prevents febrile non-hemolytic reactions (cytokines from white cells), reduces HLA alloimmunisation (white cells carry HLA), and lowers CMV transmission (CMV lives in white cells).
  • Irradiated (at least 25 Gy to the centre of the bag, 15 Gy minimum anywhere): radiation stops donor lymphocytes from dividing. It is the only reliable way to prevent transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease (TA-GVHD), which is almost always fatal. Needed for fetuses (intrauterine transfusion), newborns receiving exchange transfusion, immunocompromised patients (stem cell transplants, some cancers), blood donated by relatives, and HLA-matched products.
Exam trap: leukoreduction vs irradiationLeukoreduction does not prevent TA-GVHD; too many lymphocytes still remain. Only irradiation does. Irradiation shortens red cell shelf life to 28 days (or the original expiry, whichever is sooner), because it damages the membrane and raises potassium.

Donor rules: protecting the donor and the patient

Every donor rule protects either the donor (safe to give blood?) or the recipient (blood safe to receive?):

  • Hemoglobin: women at least 12.5 g/dL (Hct 38%), men at least 13.0 g/dL (Hct 39%). Protects the donor from becoming anemic.
  • Weight ≥ 110 lb: 450 mL is safe to remove only from a large enough person.
  • Temperature ≤ 99.5 °F, pulse 50-100, blood pressure ≤ 180/100: signs of infection or poor health.
  • Intervals: whole blood every 56 days (8 weeks); double red cells every 16 weeks; platelet apheresis as often as every 7 days, up to 24 times a year.
  • Autologous donors (giving for themselves before surgery) have relaxed rules: Hgb at least 11 g/dL.

Required donor tests: ABO and Rh, antibody screen, HIV-1/2 (antibody and NAT), HCV (antibody and NAT), HBsAg, anti-HBc and HBV NAT, HTLV-I/II, West Nile virus NAT, syphilis, and Chagas (T. cruzi, once per donor). NAT (nucleic acid testing) finds the virus itself, shortening the "window period" before antibodies appear.

Deferrals: malaria travel (3 months after visiting, 3 years after living in an endemic area), recent pregnancy (6 weeks after delivery), behaviours that raise HIV or hepatitis risk, and many medicines.

Component quality control

  • Red cells: hematocrit no higher than 80%; leukoreduced units below 5 × 10⁶ WBCs.
  • Random platelets: at least 5.5 × 10¹⁰ platelets and pH at least 6.2 at the end of storage. Low pH means the platelets have been damaged.
  • Apheresis platelets: at least 3.0 × 10¹¹ (equal to about 6 random units).
  • Cryoprecipitate: at least 80 IU factor VIII and 150 mg fibrinogen per unit.
  • Equipment: blood bank refrigerators at 1-6 °C with continuous recording and an alarm set to sound before temperatures go out of range; platelet agitators checked.

Plasma derivatives and apheresis

Plasma derivatives are made by pooling plasma from thousands of donors and purifying single proteins (fractionation), with steps that kill viruses. Examples: albumin, IVIG, Rh immune globulin, hepatitis B and tetanus immune globulins, and factor VIII and IX concentrates.

Therapeutic apheresis removes something harmful from a patient's blood and returns the rest:

  • Plasma exchange: removes harmful antibodies or proteins. Used for TTP (also replaces ADAMTS13), Guillain-Barré, myasthenia gravis and hyperviscosity.
  • Red cell exchange: replaces sickle cells in sickle cell crises or stroke.
  • Leukapheresis: removes extreme white counts in leukemia. Photopheresis: treats white cells with UV light.

Patient blood management reduces the need for donor blood: cell salvage during surgery, autologous donation, and treating anemia before surgery.

Donor reactions: vasovagal fainting is the most common (lie the donor down, raise the legs, cool cloth, fluids). Apheresis donors can get a citrate reaction, because citrate anticoagulant binds their calcium (tingling lips and fingers); slow the return or give calcium.

When each product is actually needed

  • Red cells: usually when hemoglobin is below about 7 g/dL (below 8 with heart disease or orthopedic surgery), or with symptoms of poor oxygen delivery. Not for volume replacement alone.
  • Platelets: to prevent bleeding when the count is below 10,000/µL; below 50,000 with active bleeding or before surgery.
  • FFP: multiple factor deficiencies (liver disease, DIC), urgent warfarin reversal with bleeding, and plasma exchange for TTP.
  • Cryoprecipitate: fibrinogen below about 100-150 mg/dL (DIC, massive bleeding) and factor XIII deficiency.
  • Granulocytes: severe neutropenia with infection that isn't responding to antibiotics.
  • Massive bleeding: a balanced ratio of red cells, plasma and platelets (about 1:1:1), so clotting factors and platelets aren't diluted out.

Giving blood safely at the bedside

  • Only 0.9% saline in the same line. Dextrose makes red cells swell and burst; Ringer's lactate contains calcium, which overcomes the citrate and lets the unit clot; drugs may hemolyse cells.
  • Use a standard 170-260 micron filter.
  • Start slowly for the first 15 minutes and watch the patient closely, because the most severe reactions start early.
  • Finish each unit within 4 hours (bacterial growth risk).
  • Use a blood warmer for rapid or massive transfusion and for patients with cold agglutinins.

Check your understanding

Why are platelets stored at room temperature, and what is the downside?

Cold-stored platelets are cleared quickly after transfusion. Room temperature keeps them working, but it also allows bacterial growth, so platelets have the highest bacterial contamination risk.

A stem cell transplant patient needs red cells. Which modification is essential?

Irradiation, to prevent TA-GVHD. (Leukoreduction is also usual, but it doesn't prevent GVHD.)

A bleeding patient has fibrinogen of 80 mg/dL. Which product?

Cryoprecipitate, the concentrated source of fibrinogen.

Why can't lactated Ringer's be run in the same line as red cells?

It contains calcium, which overcomes the citrate anticoagulant and can cause the unit to clot.

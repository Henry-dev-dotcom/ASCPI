This lesson joins three topics that share the liver: lipids (fats that travel in protein "boats"), bilirubin (the yellow waste from old red cells that the liver processes), and heme synthesis (how the body builds the iron-carrying part of hemoglobin, and what goes wrong in porphyria and lead poisoning).

Why fats need lipoproteins

Fat and water don't mix, so cholesterol and triglycerides can't float freely in blood. They travel inside lipoproteins: balls with a fatty core and a coat of protein (apolipoproteins) that acts as an address label. Each lipoprotein has a different job:

  • Chylomicrons carry dietary fat from the gut. They are the biggest and lightest, so they float. Label: apo B-48 (plus C-II, which switches on the enzyme that unloads them).
  • VLDL carries triglyceride made by the liver out to the body. Label: apo B-100.
  • LDL is what is left of VLDL after the triglyceride is unloaded. It carries mostly cholesterol to the tissues, including artery walls: the "bad" cholesterol. Label: apo B-100, which docks on the LDL receptor.
  • HDL collects extra cholesterol from tissues and brings it back to the liver: the "good", protective cholesterol. Label: apo A-I.
  • Lp(a) is LDL with an extra apo(a) protein that looks like plasminogen. It promotes clotting and raises heart risk, and it is mostly genetic.
Memory hook: LDL and HDLLDL = Lousy (delivers cholesterol into arteries). HDL = Healthy (takes it home to the liver).

Reading a lipid profile

Desirable values: total cholesterol below 200, LDL below 100, HDL above 40 (men) or 50 (women), triglycerides below 150 mg/dL.

LDL is often calculated with the Friedewald equation: LDL = total cholesterol − HDL − (TG ÷ 5). The TG ÷ 5 part estimates the cholesterol carried by VLDL (VLDL has about 5 times more triglyceride than cholesterol).

Worked exampleTC 240, HDL 40, TG 150. LDL = 240 − 40 − 30 = 170 mg/dL.
Exam trap: when Friedewald failsIf triglycerides are above 400 mg/dL, or chylomicrons are present (non-fasting sample), the TG ÷ 5 assumption breaks down. Measure LDL directly instead.

Fasting: triglycerides need a 9-12 hour fast, because a meal floods the blood with chylomicrons.

The refrigerator test. Leave plasma in the fridge overnight. A creamy layer on top means chylomicrons (they are light and float). Uniform cloudiness throughout means VLDL. Both can happen together.

Lipoprotein electrophoresis, from the origin: chylomicrons (stay at the start) → LDL (beta) → VLDL (pre-beta) → HDL (alpha, farthest).

Two inherited disorders: familial hypercholesterolemia (faulty LDL receptor, so LDL can't be cleared and stays very high, giving early heart attacks) and lipoprotein lipase or apo C-II deficiency (chylomicrons can't be unloaded, so triglycerides are extremely high, plasma is milky, and pancreatitis is a risk).

Bilirubin: following the yellow pigment

Picture bilirubin's journey, and jaundice becomes easy:

  1. Old red cells are broken down by macrophages. Heme becomes unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin. It doesn't dissolve in water, so it rides on albumin and can't pass into urine.
  2. The liver takes it up and attaches glucuronic acid (using the enzyme UDP-glucuronyl transferase). Now it is conjugated (direct) bilirubin, which is water-soluble.
  3. Conjugated bilirubin goes out in bile to the gut, where bacteria turn it into urobilinogen. Most leaves in stool (as stercobilin, which makes stool brown); some is reabsorbed and a little appears in urine.

Now each type of jaundice follows from where the journey is blocked:

  • Prehepatic (hemolysis): too much bilirubin made. Indirect bilirubin rises. Urine bilirubin is negative (indirect can't enter urine), but urobilinogen is high (more pigment reaches the gut). Retics and LD are high.
  • Hepatic (hepatitis, cirrhosis): sick liver cells struggle at every step, so both types rise. Urine bilirubin is positive; ALT and AST are high.
  • Posthepatic (blockage, such as gallstones or a pancreatic tumor): bile can't get out. Direct bilirubin backs up into the blood and spills into urine (dark urine, positive bilirubin). None reaches the gut, so urobilinogen is low or absent and stools are pale. ALP and GGT are high.

Inherited causes: Gilbert (mild, common, slightly low conjugating enzyme, indirect rise during fasting or illness) and Crigler-Najjar (enzyme severely missing) raise indirect bilirubin. Dubin-Johnson and Rotor (problems moving conjugated bilirubin out of the cell) raise direct bilirubin.

Exam trap: lightBilirubin breaks down in light. Protect specimens (especially newborn samples) from light, or results will be falsely low.

Heme synthesis and the porphyrias

Heme is built in steps, a bit like an assembly line:

Glycine + succinyl CoA → ALA (by ALA synthase, the rate-limiting step, which needs vitamin B6) → PBG (porphobilinogen, by ALA dehydratase) → porphyrins (uro-, copro-, protoporphyrin) → heme (iron is added by ferrochelatase).

A blocked step causes the substances before it to pile up. That pile-up is what you measure:

  • Acute intermittent porphyria (AIP): PBG deaminase is low, so ALA and PBG build up. These early compounds are toxic to nerves, so attacks bring severe abdominal pain, confusion and nerve symptoms, but no skin disease. Urine may darken on standing in light. Screen PBG with the Watson-Schwartz or Hoesch test (Ehrlich reagent).
  • Porphyria cutanea tarda (PCT): uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase is low, so uroporphyrins build up. Porphyrins react with sunlight, so the problem is blistering skin on sun-exposed areas, not pain. The most common porphyria.
  • Congenital erythropoietic porphyria: severe light sensitivity and scarring, and red-brown teeth that glow under UV light.
Memory hook: early vs late blocksAn early block piles up ALA and PBG, which hurt nerves (AIP). A late block piles up porphyrins, which hurt skin in sunlight (PCT).

Lead poisoning blocks two enzymes: ALA dehydratase (so ALA rises) and ferrochelatase (so iron can't be added and free erythrocyte protoporphyrin / zinc protoporphyrin rises). PBG stays normal, which helps separate lead from AIP. Add coarse basophilic stippling and a microcytic anemia, and the picture is complete.

Myoglobin

Myoglobin is the oxygen-holding heme protein of muscle. In crush injury or rhabdomyolysis, it pours into the blood and is small enough to pass into urine, turning it red-brown. It can block and damage the kidney tubules. The urine dipstick blood pad is positive (it detects heme), but no red cells are seen, and the plasma stays clear (unlike hemoglobinuria, where plasma is pink). CK will be very high.

Check your understanding

Pale stools, dark urine positive for bilirubin, absent urobilinogen. Which type of jaundice?

Posthepatic (obstructive). Conjugated bilirubin can't reach the gut, so it spills into urine and no urobilinogen forms.

Why is urine bilirubin negative in hemolytic jaundice?

The excess is unconjugated bilirubin, which is bound to albumin and not water-soluble, so it can't be filtered into urine.

Severe abdominal pain, confusion, no rash, high urine PBG. Diagnosis?

Acute intermittent porphyria.

Triglycerides are 520 mg/dL. How should LDL be reported?

By direct LDL measurement, because the Friedewald calculation is invalid above 400 mg/dL.

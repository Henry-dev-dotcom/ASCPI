Serology diagnoses infections by measuring the body's antibody response, or by detecting pieces of the organism (antigens). It is essential when an organism is hard to grow (viruses, syphilis, Lyme). The key to every serology question is timing: which marker appears first, which shows a current infection, and which shows a past infection or immunity.

The general rule: IgM = recent, IgG = past or immune

After a first infection, IgM appears first and fades over weeks to months. IgG follows and usually stays for years. So:

  • IgM positive → recent or current infection.
  • IgG positive, IgM negative → past infection or vaccination (immunity).
  • A fourfold (two-dilution) rise in IgG titer between an acute sample and a convalescent sample 2-4 weeks later also proves a recent infection.
  • IgG avidity: early IgG binds weakly (low avidity) and strengthens over months. High-avidity CMV IgG means the infection is more than about 3 months old, which helps date infections in pregnancy.

Hepatitis B: the most-tested serology

Work out each marker's meaning and any pattern becomes readable:

  • HBsAg (surface antigen): the virus coat. Present = infected now (acute or chronic). It's the first marker to appear. Still present after 6 months = chronic infection.
  • Anti-HBs (antibody to surface antigen): the protective antibody. Present = immune, from recovery or vaccination.
  • Anti-HBc IgM (antibody to the core): appears early and marks acute infection. It is the only marker present in the window period, when HBsAg has cleared but anti-HBs hasn't appeared yet.
  • Anti-HBc total (IgG + IgM): shows infection at some time. Vaccines contain only surface antigen, so a vaccinated person never has anti-HBc.
  • HBeAg: shows the virus is multiplying actively, so high infectivity.
Reading the common patternsHBsAg +, anti-HBc IgM + → acute hepatitis B.
HBsAg −, anti-HBs +, anti-HBc + → recovered from past infection (immune).
HBsAg −, anti-HBs +, anti-HBc − → vaccinated.
HBsAg −, anti-HBs −, anti-HBc IgM + → window period of acute infection.
HBsAg + for more than 6 months, anti-HBc IgG + → chronic infection.

Other hepatitis viruses

  • Hepatitis A (faecal-oral): anti-HAV IgM = acute; IgG = past infection or vaccine. No chronic form.
  • Hepatitis C (blood): screen for anti-HCV antibody, then confirm current infection with HCV RNA. Antibody alone can't tell current from cleared infection.
  • Hepatitis D: a defective virus that needs the HBV surface coat, so it infects only people who also have hepatitis B.

Epstein-Barr virus (mononucleosis)

Heterophile antibodies are odd IgM antibodies made during mono that react with animal red cells (horse, sheep, beef). The Monospot detects them. Their classic identity: they are absorbed by beef red cells but not by guinea pig kidney (Forssman antibodies are the reverse). The heterophile test is often negative in children under about 4-5 years and early in illness.

EBV-specific antibodies give more detail: VCA IgM = acute; VCA IgG appears early and lasts for life; EBNA antibody appears only after a few months, so EBNA positive means past infection.

Syphilis: two kinds of test

Syphilis testing uses two families of tests that measure different things:

  • Nontreponemal tests (RPR, VDRL) detect "reagin", antibody against cardiolipin released from damaged cells, not against the organism itself. They are cheap screens, and their titer falls with successful treatment, so they monitor therapy. But other conditions that damage cells also produce reagin: biologic false positives in SLE, pregnancy, mononucleosis, hepatitis, IV drug use and old age.
  • Treponemal tests (FTA-ABS, TP-PA, EIA/CIA) detect antibody against Treponema pallidum itself. They confirm the diagnosis but stay positive for life, even after cure, so they can't monitor treatment.

Many labs now use the "reverse algorithm": a treponemal EIA first, then RPR on positives. Only the VDRL is accepted for testing CSF (neurosyphilis).

Exam trap: the prozoneIn secondary syphilis, antibody levels can be so high that they swamp the test: every antigen site is covered by separate antibodies, so no lattice (clumping) forms, and the RPR reads falsely negative. Diluting the serum restores the right balance and the test turns positive.

Stages of syphilis: primary (a painless chancre; nontreponemal tests may still be negative), secondary (rash including palms and soles, condylomata lata; tests strongly positive), latent (no symptoms, tests positive), and tertiary (gummas, aortic and brain damage).

Lyme disease

Caused by Borrelia burgdorferi from a tick bite. Early localised: the bull's-eye erythema migrans rash (diagnosed clinically; antibodies may not be there yet). Early disseminated: heart block, facial palsy, meningitis. Late: arthritis and nerve problems. Testing is two-tier: an EIA screen, then a Western blot or a second EIA to confirm.

Other serology

  • Rubella: IgG = immune; IgM = acute (dangerous in early pregnancy). TORCH = Toxoplasma, Other (syphilis, parvovirus, varicella), Rubella, CMV, Herpes: infections that harm the fetus.
  • Measles, mumps, rubella: IgM acute, IgG immune.
  • Parvovirus B19: IgM = recent infection.
  • West Nile virus: IgM in serum or CSF; blood donors are screened by NAT.
  • Post-strep complications: ASO (anti-streptolysin O) and anti-DNase B show a recent group A strep infection, supporting rheumatic fever or glomerulonephritis. Anti-DNase B is better after skin infections.
  • Fungal antigens: cryptococcal antigen (CSF, serum), Histoplasma antigen (urine), Aspergillus galactomannan (serum).
  • HIV: markers appear in the order RNA → p24 antigen → antibody; 4th-generation tests detect both antigen and antibody.

Tuberculosis testing

Tuberculin skin test (PPD, Mantoux): a small amount of TB protein is injected under the skin. If the person's T cells have met TB before, they gather at the site over 2-3 days (a type IV delayed reaction). Read at 48-72 hours, measuring the hardened area (induration), not the redness. Cut-offs depend on risk:

  • ≥5 mm: highest risk (HIV, close contacts of TB, immunosuppressed).
  • ≥10 mm: moderate risk (healthcare workers, recent immigrants, injection drug users).
  • ≥15 mm: low risk.

IGRAs (QuantiFERON, T-SPOT) are blood tests: the patient's T cells are exposed to TB-specific antigens, and the test measures interferon-gamma release. Advantages: not affected by BCG vaccination (the antigens aren't in BCG), one visit, no reading error.

Exam trap: what these tests can't doNeither the skin test nor an IGRA separates latent TB from active TB. Active disease is diagnosed by AFB smear, culture and PCR on clinical specimens.

Check your understanding

HBsAg negative, anti-HBs positive, anti-HBc negative. Interpretation?

Immune from vaccination (no core antibody means never infected).

A 3-year-old has symptoms of mono but a negative Monospot. Next test?

EBV-specific serology (VCA IgM, VCA IgG, EBNA). Heterophile antibodies are often absent in young children.

Why does the RPR titer help monitor syphilis treatment but the FTA-ABS doesn't?

The RPR titer falls after successful treatment; treponemal antibodies like FTA-ABS stay positive for life.

A healthcare worker vaccinated with BCG needs TB screening. Which test avoids a false positive from BCG?

An interferon-gamma release assay (QuantiFERON or T-SPOT).

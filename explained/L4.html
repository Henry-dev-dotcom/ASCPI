Lab instruments look very different, but each is built on a simple physical principle: light absorbed, emitted or scattered; an electrical signal; separation by charge, size or mass. Learn the principle and you can answer "which method measures X?" and "what interferes with it?". The second half covers molecular methods, which copy and detect DNA and RNA.

Light-based methods

  • Spectrophotometry / photometry: measures how much light a colored solution absorbs at a chosen wavelength. By Beer's law, absorbance is proportional to concentration. Most chemistry tests use it.
  • Fluorometry: a molecule absorbs light at one wavelength and emits light at a longer one. Because the detector looks at emitted light against a dark background (at a right angle), it is much more sensitive than absorbance. Weak points: quenching and background fluorescence.
  • Nephelometry measures light scattered at an angle by particles; turbidimetry measures the light blocked (straight through). Both are used for proteins and immunoglobulins (antigen-antibody complexes form particles).
  • Osmometry: dissolved particles lower the freezing point, so measuring the freezing point depression gives the total particle concentration (osmolality).

Separation methods

  • Electrophoresis: charged molecules move in an electric field, separating by charge (and size). Used for serum proteins, hemoglobins, isoenzymes and DNA.
  • Chromatography: a mixture is carried by a moving phase (gas or liquid) over a stationary phase. Each compound sticks to the stationary phase differently, so they come out at different times (retention time). Gas chromatography (GC) for volatile compounds (alcohols, drugs), HPLC for larger or heat-sensitive ones (vitamins, HbA1c, hemoglobin variants), thin-layer (TLC) as a simple screen.
  • Mass spectrometry (MS): molecules are given a charge (ionised) and sorted by their mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), giving a unique fingerprint. Combined with GC or LC (GC-MS, LC-MS/MS), it is the gold standard for drug confirmation and newborn screening (tandem MS), and MALDI-TOF uses it for microbe identification.

Electrochemistry: measuring with electrodes

  • Potentiometry (measures voltage at zero current): ion-selective electrodes for Na, K, Cl, and the pH electrode. The membrane lets only the target ion interact, creating a voltage that depends on its activity. The Severinghaus electrode for pCO2 is a modified pH electrode: CO2 diffuses through a membrane and changes the pH of a bicarbonate solution.
  • Amperometry (measures current): the Clark electrode for pO2. Oxygen is reduced at a cathode, and the current is proportional to the oxygen.
  • Coulometry: counts the total charge used in a reaction; the classic chloride titrator (silver ions generated until all chloride is used up). Used for sweat chloride.
Memory hook: the blood gas electrodespH and pCO2 are potentiometric (voltage); pO2 is amperometric (current). "Clark measures oxygen with Current."

Cell-counting and microbiology instruments

  • Flow cytometry: cells flow single file past a laser. Forward scatter reflects size, side scatter reflects granularity, and fluorescent antibodies identify CD markers. Uses: leukemia/lymphoma typing, CD4 counts, PNH, stem cell counts.
  • Hematology analyzers: impedance (Coulter) counts and sizes cells by the pulse each cell makes in an electric current; optical scatter and dyes produce the differential.
  • Automated microbiology: continuous-monitoring blood culture systems (detect CO2 from growth), ID and susceptibility cards, and MALDI-TOF.

PCR: copying DNA

The polymerase chain reaction makes millions of copies of one short DNA target so it can be detected. Each cycle has three temperature steps:

  1. Denaturation (about 95 °C): heat splits the double-stranded DNA into single strands.
  2. Annealing (about 50-65 °C): cooling lets short primers stick to each end of the target sequence. The primers define what gets copied.
  3. Extension (72 °C): a heat-stable DNA polymerase (Taq) builds new strands from the primers, using free nucleotides.

Each cycle doubles the copies, so after 30 cycles one target becomes about a billion (2³⁰) copies.

Memory hook: the PCR order"Don't Ask Everyone": Denature, Anneal, Extend (hot, cool, warm).

Variations:

  • RT-PCR: reverse transcriptase first converts RNA into DNA (cDNA), so RNA viruses like HIV and hepatitis C can be amplified.
  • Real-time (quantitative) PCR: a fluorescent signal is measured every cycle. The more target at the start, the fewer cycles it takes to cross the threshold, which gives a quantity (viral load).
  • NAAT is the general term for nucleic acid amplification tests, such as the urine/swab tests for Chlamydia and gonorrhoea.
Exam trap: PCR contaminationPCR is so sensitive that one stray copy of a previous product (amplicon carryover) can make a negative sample positive. Prevention: one-way workflow through separate rooms for reagent prep, sample prep and amplification/detection; filter pipette tips; UNG enzyme; and a no-template (negative) control in every run. If the negative control amplifies, the run is invalid.

Other molecular methods

  • FISH (fluorescence in situ hybridisation): fluorescent DNA probes bind specific chromosome regions in cells, showing translocations or extra copies (BCR-ABL, HER2, PML-RARA).
  • Sequencing: reads the exact order of DNA bases (Sanger, or next-generation sequencing for many genes at once). 16S rRNA sequencing identifies bacteria.
  • Blotting: Southern = DNA, Northern = RNA, Western = protein.
Memory hook: blots"SNOW DROP": Southern = DNA, Northern = RNA, O (nothing), Western = Protein.

Check your understanding

Which electrode measures pO2, and what does it measure electrically?

The Clark electrode; it measures current (amperometry).

What are the three steps of a PCR cycle, in order?

Denaturation (~95 °C), annealing (~50-65 °C), extension (72 °C).

A PCR no-template control shows amplification. What does it mean?

Contamination (usually amplicon carryover). The run is invalid.

Which blot detects RNA?

Northern blot.

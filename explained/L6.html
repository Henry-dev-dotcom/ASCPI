Lab operations traps test whether you know the right action, not just the right fact. The wrong answers are usually tempting shortcuts: repeating QC until it passes, changing the mean, using a convenient but wrong dilution. Each listed trap is explained below, followed by more classics.

Trap 1: treating 1-2s as a rejection

A single control outside ±2 SD happens by chance about once in 20 runs, so 1-2s is only a warning. It tells you to look harder at the other rules. Reject the run for 1-3s or R-4s (random error) or 2-2s, 4-1s or 10x (systematic error).

Trap 2: "rerun QC until it passes"

When QC fails, the correct sequence is: stop reporting, find and fix the cause (reagent, calibration, instrument), rerun controls, then review patient results since the last good QC. Simply rerunning the same control until one lands in range hides real problems and may release wrong patient results.

Trap 3: the wrong bleach

Spills of blood are disinfected with a 1:10 dilution of household bleach (about 0.5% sodium hypochlorite), made fresh daily, because diluted bleach loses strength. Leave it on for at least 10 minutes. Concentrated bleach is not "better"; it is a chemical hazard. 70% alcohol is not adequate for blood spills.

Trap 4: getting help with proficiency testing

PT samples must be handled exactly like patient samples, by the people who normally test patients. Never send them to another lab, compare results with other labs before the deadline, or retest them more than a patient sample would be. That is a serious CLIA violation (PT referral), even if the intent was just to "check".

More lab operations traps

Changing the QC meanNever shift the mean or widen limits to make QC pass. New control lots get their mean from at least 20 runs alongside the old lot.
Expired reagentsAn expired reagent can't be used for patients even if QC is in range.
Dilution arithmetic0.5 mL serum + 4.5 mL diluent is a 1:10 dilution, not 1:9. The total volume is the denominator.
Order of drawLight blue comes before red/gold, then green, lavender, gray. EDTA (lavender) drawn before chemistry raises potassium and lowers calcium.
Patient identificationRoom and bed number are never an identifier. Use two independent identifiers.
Needlestick first aidWash with soap and water. Don't squeeze, and don't apply bleach to the wound. Report immediately.
Fire extinguisher classesNever use water on a Class C (electrical) fire.
Most errorsThe preanalytic phase (identification, collection, transport) causes the most lab errors, not the analytic phase.
Critical valuesCalling and documenting is required; leaving a critical result in the computer for the clinician to find is not acceptable.

How to approach a lab operations trap

  1. Ask what protects the patient first. Holding results, recollecting, and calling the clinician usually beat speed or convenience.
  2. Prefer the answer that finds and fixes the cause over one that just makes the numbers look right.
  3. Remember that documentation is part of every correct action.

Check your understanding

One control is at +2.3 SD and all other rules pass. Accept or reject?

Accept the run. It is a 1-2s warning only, so look closely at the other rules and the next runs.

QC fails after a new reagent lot is loaded. What is the correct action?

Don't report patient results; troubleshoot (recalibrate, check the reagent), repeat QC, then review affected patient results and document.

A coworker suggests sending a PT sample to a sister lab to "double-check" before reporting. Is this allowed?

No. Referring PT samples is a serious CLIA violation.

Electrolytes are the charged salts dissolved in body water. They control fluid balance, nerve signals and heart rhythm, and they are tightly linked to acid-base balance. Blood gas questions look intimidating, but there is a simple step-by-step method that solves almost all of them, and this lesson teaches it.

The main electrolytes and what each does

  • Sodium (135-145 mmol/L): the main salt outside cells. It mostly reflects water balance: low sodium usually means too much water, high sodium means too little.
  • Potassium (3.5-5.1): the main salt inside cells. Small changes in blood potassium affect the heart. High K gives peaked T waves and dangerous arrhythmias; low K causes weakness and arrhythmias too.
  • Chloride (98-107): follows sodium and moves opposite to bicarbonate.
  • Bicarbonate (22-29): the main base buffer (it is the "total CO2" on a chemistry panel).
Why potassium is so easily falsely highCells hold about 30 times more potassium than plasma. Anything that lets potassium leak out of cells in the tube makes the result read high: hemolysis, delayed separation (cells leak over time, especially if refrigerated), fist clenching during the draw, and very high platelet or white counts (they release K as the sample clots). Contamination with K2EDTA from a purple tube is another classic cause (it also makes calcium very low).

Anion gap: finding hidden acids

Blood is electrically neutral: positive charges equal negative charges. We measure the main cation (Na) and the main anions (Cl and HCO3), but not every anion. The difference is the anion gap:

AG = Na − (Cl + HCO3), normally about 8-16 (some labs use 10-20 if K is added).

If an extra acid enters the blood, it uses up bicarbonate and leaves its own unmeasured anion behind, so the gap rises. Causes of a high anion gap metabolic acidosis are remembered as MUDPILES: Methanol, Uremia, DKA, Paraldehyde, Iron/Isoniazid, Lactic acidosis, Ethylene glycol, Salicylates.

Worked exampleNa 140, Cl 100, HCO3 10. AG = 140 − 110 = 30. High gap: look for lactate, ketones, kidney failure or a toxin.

A low anion gap is unusual. Think lab error, low albumin (albumin is a big unmeasured anion), or a positively charged paraprotein (IgG myeloma).

Osmolality and the osmolal gap

Osmolality counts all dissolved particles. We can calculate it from the main ones:

Calculated osmolality = 2 × Na + glucose ÷ 18 + BUN ÷ 2.8 (about 285-295 mOsm/kg).

Sodium is doubled to count its partner anions. If the measured osmolality (by freezing point) is much higher than the calculated one, something unmeasured is adding particles. A gap above about 10 suggests toxic alcohols: methanol, ethylene glycol, isopropanol, or ethanol itself.

Calcium and magnesium

About half of blood calcium is bound to albumin, and half is free (ionized). Only the ionized part is active. That's why low albumin lowers total calcium without causing symptoms, and why ionized calcium is the better test in very sick patients. Ionized calcium also rises in acidosis (H+ pushes Ca off albumin).

PTH raises calcium; the details are in the endocrine lesson. Magnesium is needed for PTH release and potassium handling, so low magnesium causes low calcium and low potassium that won't correct until magnesium is replaced, plus arrhythmias.

Acid-base basics

Normal arterial blood: pH 7.35-7.45, pCO2 35-45 mmHg, HCO3 22-26 mmol/L.

pH is a balance between an acid controlled by the lungs (CO2, which forms carbonic acid) and a base controlled by the kidneys (HCO3). The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation shows the balance:

pH = 6.1 + log [HCO3 ÷ (0.03 × pCO2)]. The ratio of HCO3 to dissolved CO2 is normally 20:1; as long as that ratio holds, pH stays normal.

  • A respiratory problem changes pCO2. Slow breathing (lung disease, sedatives) retains CO2 → acidosis. Fast breathing (anxiety, aspirin early on, altitude) blows off CO2 → alkalosis.
  • A metabolic problem changes HCO3. Gaining acid or losing base (DKA, diarrhoea, kidney failure) → acidosis. Losing acid or gaining base (vomiting, diuretics) → alkalosis.

The 3-step method for any blood gas

  1. Look at pH. Below 7.35 = acidosis; above 7.45 = alkalosis.
  2. Find the culprit. Which value explains the pH? If pH is low and pCO2 is high, it is respiratory acidosis. If pH is low and HCO3 is low, it is metabolic acidosis. (pCO2 acts like an acid; HCO3 like a base.)
  3. Check compensation. Is the other value moving to fix it? The body tries to restore the 20:1 ratio, so the compensating value moves in the same direction as the primary change. If pH is back in the normal range, the disorder is "fully compensated".
Worked examplespH 7.25, pCO2 30, HCO3 12 → low pH, low HCO3 = metabolic acidosis; pCO2 is also low (same direction) = respiratory compensation.
pH 7.52, pCO2 28, HCO3 22 → high pH, low pCO2 = respiratory alkalosis; HCO3 is still normal = acute, uncompensated.
pH 7.50, pCO2 48, HCO3 36 after vomiting → high pH, high HCO3 = metabolic alkalosis with respiratory compensation.
Memory hook: ROMERespiratory Opposite: pH and pCO2 move in opposite directions. Metabolic Equal: pH and HCO3 move in the same direction.

Lungs compensate within minutes; kidneys take days. That is why acute respiratory problems usually show little metabolic compensation.

Blood gas specimen rules

  • Use heparin (a dry heparin syringe), mix well, and keep it air-tight.
  • Air bubbles let gases equalise with room air (pO2 about 150, pCO2 near 0). So bubbles raise pO2 and lower pCO2 (and raise pH).
  • Analyze quickly. If delayed, ice the sample, because living cells keep consuming O2 and producing CO2 and acid.

Check your understanding

pH 7.30, pCO2 60, HCO3 29. Interpretation?

Respiratory acidosis (low pH, high pCO2) with some metabolic compensation (HCO3 mildly raised, same direction as pCO2).

Measured osmolality 330, calculated 290. What does this suggest?

An osmolal gap of 40, pointing to an unmeasured osmole such as ethylene glycol or methanol.

Potassium 6.8 on a grossly hemolyzed sample from a well patient with a normal ECG. Action?

Don't report it as valid. Recollect a properly drawn sample, because hemolysis releases red cell potassium.

An arterial sample arrives with a large air bubble. How are the results affected?

pO2 falsely high, pCO2 falsely low, pH falsely high. Reject and recollect.

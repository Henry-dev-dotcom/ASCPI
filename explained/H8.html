Exam traps are questions written so that a half-remembered fact leads to the wrong answer. They usually test whether you know the reason behind a fact, not just the fact. Below, each trap is explained, followed by more hematology traps that come up often.

Trap 1: "Target cells mean HbC"

Target cells are typical of HbC, but they aren't specific to it. A target cell forms whenever there is too much membrane for the amount of hemoglobin. That happens when hemoglobin is low (thalassemia, iron deficiency), when membrane cholesterol is high (liver disease), and when the spleen isn't there to trim extra membrane (after splenectomy). Read the rest of the question: microcytosis with normal iron points to thalassemia; jaundice points to liver disease.

Trap 2: "I'll find Heinz bodies on the Wright stain"

You won't. Heinz bodies are clumps of denatured hemoglobin. Wright stain colors hemoglobin pink, so a pink clump inside a pink cell is invisible. You need a supravital stain (crystal violet or new methylene blue) on living cells. On a Wright smear you only see the clues they leave behind: bite cells and blister cells.

Trap 3: spherocytes always mean the same thing

Spherocytes appear in both autoimmune hemolytic anemia and hereditary spherocytosis, and the smear can look identical. The DAT separates them: positive = antibody on the cells (autoimmune); negative = an inherited membrane defect. Burns and some toxins also make spherocytes, so always use the history.

Trap 4: a long aPTT always means a factor deficiency

If the patient is on heparin, a long aPTT is the expected drug effect. Calling it a factor deficiency, or adding factor tests, is a mistake. Before you interpret a long aPTT, check the medications and how the sample was drawn (a sample drawn from a heparinized line is a classic error). A long thrombin time with a normal reptilase time confirms heparin.

More traps worth knowing

Reticulocytes without correctionA retic count of 6% in a patient with Hct 20% is less impressive than it looks. Correct it: 6 × 20 ÷ 45 ≈ 2.7%. Always correct before deciding the marrow is "responding well".
Pelger-Huët cells counted as bandsBilobed Pelger-Huët neutrophils look like bands or metamyelocytes. Counting them as bands creates a false left shift. If every neutrophil has two round lobes and the patient is well, think Pelger-Huët.
Nucleated red cells inflating the WBCAnalyzers may count nRBCs as white cells. If the smear shows many nRBCs, correct the WBC: WBC × 100 ÷ (100 + nRBCs).
Auer rods rule out ALLAuer rods appear only in myeloid blasts. A blast with an Auer rod is myeloid, whatever else the question says.
Sickle screen in infantsA negative sickle solubility test in a baby under 6 months doesn't rule out sickle cell disease, because HbF is still high. Use electrophoresis or HPLC.
Factor XII and bleedingFactor XII deficiency gives a very long aPTT but no bleeding. Don't pick it as the cause of a bleeding patient's problem.
Lupus anticoagulantDespite the name, it causes clotting, not bleeding. In the test tube it prolongs the aPTT and doesn't correct on mixing.
Underfilled citrate tubeAn underfilled light-blue tube gives falsely long PT and aPTT. The fix is to recollect, not to report the result with a comment.

How to approach a trap question

  1. Read the whole case before the answers. Traps usually hide one detail that changes everything (a drug, an age, a temperature).
  2. Ask "why would this happen?" If your answer depends on a mechanism, check it fits every clue.
  3. Check for specimen problems first: a wrong tube, a delay, clots, lipemia or hemolysis.

Check your understanding

A smear shows bite cells after the patient took a sulfa drug, but no Heinz bodies are seen. Is G6PD deficiency ruled out?

No. Heinz bodies don't show on Wright stain. Use a supravital stain, and do a G6PD assay (ideally a few months after the episode).

Many target cells, MCV 68, normal ferritin, no jaundice. HbC or thalassemia?

Thalassemia is more likely: microcytosis with normal iron. Confirm with hemoglobin electrophoresis or HPLC.

A heparinized patient's aPTT is 85 s. The doctor asks for a factor VIII assay. What should you check first?

The heparin effect. The long aPTT is expected on heparin. Neutralise the heparin (or use a thrombin/reptilase time to confirm it) before interpreting factor results.

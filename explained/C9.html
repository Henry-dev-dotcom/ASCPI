Chemistry traps usually turn on a method limitation (what an instrument can't see or is fooled by) or a specificity issue (a test that rises for more than one reason). Each trap below comes with the reasoning, followed by more classic chemistry traps.

Trap 1: pseudohyponatremia shows on every analyzer

It doesn't. Indirect ISE dilutes the sample before measuring and assumes plasma is about 93% water. In severe lipemia or very high protein (myeloma), there is less water per unit of plasma, so the diluted sample contains less sodium and reads falsely low. Direct ISE (undiluted, used on blood gas analyzers) measures sodium in the water phase and gives the true value. Measured osmolality will also be normal, which proves the low sodium is false.

Trap 2: a "small" ketone result rules out severe ketosis

It doesn't. The dipstick uses nitroprusside, which reacts with acetoacetate (and weakly with acetone) but not with beta-hydroxybutyrate, the main ketone in severe DKA. As DKA is treated, beta-hydroxybutyrate turns into acetoacetate, so the dipstick can look worse while the patient is actually getting better.

Trap 3: CK-MB is as good as troponin

Troponin is more specific (cardiac forms exist only in heart muscle) and stays raised for 7-10 days. CK-MB is also found in skeletal muscle in small amounts and returns to normal in 2-3 days. That short life is CK-MB's one advantage: it is better for detecting a re-infarction a few days after the first one.

Trap 4: a high ALP means liver disease

ALP is made in the liver (bile ducts), bone, placenta and intestine. A high ALP in a growing teenager, a pregnant woman, or someone with Paget disease or bone metastases may have nothing to do with the liver. Check GGT or 5'-nucleotidase: high with ALP means liver; normal means bone or another source.

More chemistry traps

HemolysisRed cells contain far more potassium, LD, AST and phosphorus than plasma. A hemolyzed sample makes all four falsely high. Hemoglobin's color can also interfere with spectrophotometric tests.
Glucose in an old tubeAn unspun tube left for hours reads falsely low (cells keep consuming glucose) unless it was drawn in a fluoride (gray) tube.
EDTA contaminationVery high potassium with very low calcium (and low ALP and magnesium) in a well patient suggests EDTA carry-over from drawing in the wrong order. Recollect.
BiotinHigh-dose biotin supplements interfere with streptavidin-biotin immunoassays: sandwich assays (TSH, troponin) read falsely low, competitive assays (free T4) read falsely high, mimicking hyperthyroidism.
Hook effectAn extremely high analyte (hCG, prolactin, tumour markers) can saturate both antibodies in a sandwich assay and give a falsely low result. Dilute and repeat when the result doesn't fit the clinical picture.
Total calcium with low albuminLow albumin lowers total calcium while ionized calcium is normal. Correct for albumin or measure ionized calcium before calling it hypocalcemia.
Bilirubin and lightSamples left in light lose bilirubin and read falsely low.

How to approach a chemistry trap

  1. Does the result fit the patient? If not, suspect the sample (hemolysis, lipemia, wrong tube, delay) before suspecting disease.
  2. What does the method actually detect? Many traps hinge on a method missing something (beta-hydroxybutyrate) or being fooled by something (biotin, lipids).
  3. Is the marker specific? Ask which other organs or conditions raise it.

Check your understanding

Sodium 125 on the main analyzer, 139 on the blood gas machine, triglycerides 2500. Which is right and why?

139 (direct ISE). The main analyzer uses indirect ISE, which is falsely low with severe lipemia.

A patient's TSH is suppressed and free T4 high, but they feel well and take hair supplements. What do you suspect?

Biotin interference. Ask the patient to stop biotin (often for 2-3 days) and retest, or use a method not based on biotin-streptavidin.

Potassium 7.9, calcium 4.0 mg/dL, patient well. Likely cause?

K2EDTA contamination of the sample. Recollect, following the correct order of draw.

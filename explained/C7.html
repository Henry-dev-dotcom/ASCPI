Hormones are chemical messages. Endocrine questions look complicated, but almost all of them use the same idea: the feedback loop. Once you can follow a loop, you can work out any pattern of "high this, low that" without memorizing it. This lesson explains the loops, then the hormone disorders, vitamins and trace elements.

The feedback loop: a thermostat

Most hormone systems have three levels. The hypothalamus (brain) senses a need and signals the pituitary, which signals the target gland, which makes the final hormone. When there's enough final hormone, it switches the top levels off (negative feedback), just like a thermostat switching off the heater when the room is warm.

So to find where the problem is, compare the pituitary hormone with the gland hormone:

  • Gland hormone low and pituitary hormone high → the gland has failed, and the pituitary is shouting at it (primary disease).
  • Gland hormone low and pituitary hormone low → the pituitary isn't sending the signal (secondary disease).
  • Gland hormone high and pituitary hormone low → the gland is overproducing on its own, and the pituitary has gone quiet.

Thyroid: the loop in action

TRH (hypothalamus) → TSH (pituitary) → T4 and T3 (thyroid). TSH is the most sensitive first test.

  • High TSH, low free T4 = primary hypothyroidism. The thyroid is failing (most often Hashimoto thyroiditis, with anti-TPO antibodies), so TSH climbs.
  • Low TSH, high free T4 = hyperthyroidism, most often Graves disease. Antibodies (TSI) stimulate the TSH receptor, so the thyroid overproduces and the pituitary switches TSH off.
  • Low TSH, low free T4 = secondary (pituitary) hypothyroidism.

Measure free T4 rather than total T4. Most T4 is bound to thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG), and TBG rises in pregnancy and with estrogen, raising total T4 without changing thyroid function.

Suppress the excess, stimulate the deficiency

Single hormone levels go up and down through the day, so many conditions are confirmed with dynamic tests. The rule: if you suspect too much hormone, try to suppress it; if you suspect too little, try to stimulate it. A normal gland responds; a diseased one doesn't.

  • Cushing syndrome (too much cortisol: weight gain, purple stretch marks, high glucose): give dexamethasone, a cortisol-like drug that should switch off ACTH. In Cushing, cortisol fails to suppress. With high-dose dexamethasone, pituitary (Cushing disease) usually does suppress; ectopic ACTH from a tumour does not.
  • Addison disease (adrenal failure: low cortisol, low sodium, high potassium, dark skin): give synthetic ACTH (cosyntropin). Cortisol fails to rise. ACTH is high because the pituitary is trying to compensate.
  • Acromegaly (too much growth hormone): GH fails to suppress after a glucose drink; IGF-1 is high.

Other hormone disorders to know

  • Conn syndrome (primary hyperaldosteronism): an adrenal tumour makes too much aldosterone, which retains sodium and wastes potassium. Result: high blood pressure, low potassium, high aldosterone, low renin (renin is switched off by feedback). In secondary hyperaldosteronism, renin is high.
  • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia: usually 21-hydroxylase deficiency. Cortisol can't be made, so ACTH stays high and the building blocks pile up. 17-hydroxyprogesterone is high (newborn screening test), and androgens rise.
  • Pheochromocytoma: an adrenal tumour making adrenaline-type hormones (catecholamines). Episodes of high blood pressure, sweating and palpitations. Test plasma or urine metanephrines (best), catecholamines and VMA. Neuroblastoma in children also raises VMA and HVA.
  • Carcinoid tumour: makes serotonin; test urine 5-HIAA (its breakdown product). Avoid bananas, pineapple, walnuts and avocado beforehand; they contain serotonin.
  • Prolactinoma: high prolactin, causing milk production and loss of periods.

Steroid vs peptide hormones. Steroid hormones (cortisol, aldosterone, estrogen, testosterone) are made from cholesterol, cross cell membranes and act inside the cell. Peptide hormones (insulin, GH, ACTH, PTH) can't cross membranes and act on surface receptors. Catecholamines are made from the amino acid tyrosine.

Calcium, PTH and phosphorus

PTH raises blood calcium by pulling it from bone, holding it in the kidney, and activating vitamin D. It also makes the kidney excrete phosphorus. So calcium and phosphorus usually move in opposite directions:

  • Primary hyperparathyroidism (usually an adenoma): high PTH, high calcium, low phosphorus.
  • Hypoparathyroidism (often after neck surgery): low PTH, low calcium, high phosphorus.
  • Hypercalcemia of malignancy: tumours make PTH-related peptide, so calcium is high but the real PTH is suppressed (low).
Exam trap: high calcium and PTHHigh calcium with high PTH points to the parathyroid. High calcium with low PTH points elsewhere: cancer (PTHrP), too much vitamin D, sarcoidosis.

Pregnancy and tumour markers

  • hCG: pregnancy, and trophoblastic disease (molar pregnancy, choriocarcinoma) or testicular tumours. Very high levels can cause a falsely low result in sandwich assays (the hook effect); dilute and repeat.
  • AFP: made by the fetal liver. High maternal AFP suggests a neural tube defect (open spine lets AFP leak) or twins. Low AFP (with low estriol, high hCG and high inhibin A) suggests Down syndrome. In adults, AFP is a marker for liver cancer and germ cell tumours.
  • Other tumour markers: PSA (prostate), CA-125 (ovary), CEA (colon, mainly for monitoring), CA 19-9 (pancreas). These are best for monitoring known cancer, not for screening healthy people.

Vitamins: fat-soluble vs water-soluble

A, D, E and K are fat-soluble: they need fat to be absorbed and are stored in the body. Fat malabsorption (cystic fibrosis, pancreatic disease) causes all four to fall.

  • A: night blindness. D: rickets (children), osteomalacia (adults); measure 25-OH vitamin D, the storage form. E: red cell hemolysis and nerve damage. K: bleeding with a long PT, because factors II, VII, IX and X need vitamin K.

The B vitamins and C are water-soluble:

  • B1 (thiamine): beriberi; Wernicke encephalopathy in alcoholics.
  • B3 (niacin): pellagra: the "3 Ds", dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia.
  • B6: neuropathy and sideroblastic anemia (heme synthesis needs it).
  • Folate: megaloblastic anemia and neural tube defects in pregnancy. B12: megaloblastic anemia plus nerve damage.
  • C: scurvy: bleeding gums, poor wound healing (collagen needs vitamin C).

Trace elements

  • Copper: in Wilson disease, copper builds up in liver and brain and ceruloplasmin (the copper carrier) is low. Look for a Kayser-Fleischer ring in the eye.
  • Zinc: poor wound healing, rash, poor taste. Iodine: goitre and hypothyroidism. Selenium and chromium: antioxidant defence and glucose metabolism.
  • ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) is raised in sarcoidosis.

Check your understanding

TSH low, free T4 low. Where is the problem?

The pituitary (secondary hypothyroidism). A failing thyroid would make TSH high.

Hypertension, potassium 2.9, high aldosterone, low renin. Diagnosis?

Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome).

Why does a malignancy cause high calcium with low PTH?

The tumour makes PTH-related peptide, which raises calcium. The high calcium switches off the real parathyroid glands.

A patient with fat malabsorption has a long PT. Which vitamin is low and why does it matter?

Vitamin K. Without it, the liver can't finish making factors II, VII, IX and X, so clotting slows (VII falls first, so PT prolongs).

The immune system is the body's defence force, with two branches. The innate branch is the fast, general-purpose first responder. The adaptive branch is slower but precise, and it remembers. Almost every immunology lab test detects some part of that defence: antibodies, cells, complement or cytokines. This lesson gives you the map.

The 8 big ideas, explained

  1. Each antibody class has a job. IgM comes first and fixes complement best; IgG is the most abundant and crosses the placenta; IgA guards mucous membranes; IgE drives allergy.
  2. Cell markers are ID badges: T cells CD3; helper T CD4 (HIV's target); cytotoxic T CD8; B cells CD19/CD20; NK cells CD16/CD56.
  3. Complement: C1q starts the classical pathway; C5b-9 punches holes; C3b tags microbes for eating; C5a calls in white cells.
  4. Hypersensitivity, "ACID": Anaphylactic (I), Cytotoxic (II), Immune complex (III), Delayed (IV).
  5. ANA screens for lupus; anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith confirm it.
  6. Hepatitis B: HBsAg = infected now; anti-HBs = immune; anti-HBc IgM = acute infection or window period.
  7. Syphilis: RPR/VDRL screen; FTA-ABS/TP-PA confirm. Very high antibody can cause a prozone false negative.
  8. Sensitivity catches true disease (TP ÷ (TP + FN)); specificity clears true non-disease (TN ÷ (TN + FP)).

Innate vs adaptive immunity

Innate immunity works within minutes and treats every invader much the same: skin and mucous barriers, neutrophils and macrophages (which eat microbes), NK cells (which kill infected or cancerous cells without prior exposure) and complement. Eating is much easier when the microbe is tagged with an opsonin ("sauce" that makes it tasty), mainly IgG and C3b. Neutrophils kill using a "respiratory burst" of oxygen radicals, which the NBT (or DHR) test checks.

Adaptive immunity takes days the first time but is precisely targeted, and it creates memory. B cells make antibodies (humoral immunity); T cells kill infected cells and coordinate the response (cell-mediated immunity).

Antigen vocabulary: an epitope is the small part of an antigen that an antibody actually binds. A hapten is a molecule too small to trigger a response alone, but immunogenic when attached to a larger carrier protein (penicillin binding to body proteins causes penicillin allergy this way).

The acute-phase response

When there is infection or injury, cytokines (especially IL-6) tell the liver to change what it makes. It raises acute-phase proteins: CRP (rises in 4-6 hours, peaks at 36-50 hours, falls fast once inflammation ends), fibrinogen, haptoglobin, ferritin and others. It makes less albumin ("negative" acute-phase protein). High-sensitivity CRP measures very low levels to estimate heart disease risk.

Antibodies: structure and the five classes

An antibody is shaped like a Y, made of two heavy chains and two light chains (kappa or lambda) held together by disulfide bonds.

  • The two arms are the Fab parts (fragment antigen-binding); their tips bind antigen.
  • The stem is the Fc part (fragment crystallisable). It "talks" to the rest of the immune system: it binds complement and the Fc receptors on phagocytes.
  • The enzyme papain cuts above the hinge, giving 2 Fab + 1 Fc. Pepsin cuts below it, giving one joined F(ab')2 (and the Fc is broken up).

The heavy chain type sets the class:

  • IgG: about 75% of serum antibody; the main antibody of the secondary (memory) response; the only class that crosses the placenta (protecting newborns, but also causing HDFN). IgG1 and IgG3 fix complement best.
  • IgM: the first antibody made in a new infection; a pentamer (five Y's joined by a J chain) with 10 binding sites, so it is the best at agglutinating and fixing complement. It doesn't cross the placenta, so IgM in a newborn means the baby itself is infected (a TORCH infection).
  • IgA: in secretions (saliva, tears, breast milk, gut) as a dimer with a J chain and a secretory piece that protects it from digestion.
  • IgE: the lowest concentration. It sits on mast cells and basophils; when allergen cross-links it, they release histamine (allergy). Also fights parasites.
  • IgD: mainly a receptor on the surface of B cells.
Memory hook"M"akes it first (IgM); "G"ets through the placenta (IgG); "A" is at the Air-and-gut doors (IgA); "E" is for Explosive allergy (IgE).

Primary vs secondary response: the first exposure gives a slow, small response, mostly IgM. A later exposure triggers memory cells, giving a fast, large, long-lasting response, mostly IgG. That is why vaccines boost, and why IgM suggests a recent infection while IgG alone suggests a past one or immunity.

Immune cells and their markers

  • T cells all carry CD3.
    • Helper T cells: CD4. They recognise antigen shown on HLA class II (on antigen-presenting cells) and direct the response. HIV infects and destroys them.
    • Cytotoxic T cells: CD8. They recognise antigen on HLA class I (on all nucleated cells) and kill infected or cancerous cells.
    • Regulatory T cells: CD4, CD25, FOXP3. They calm the response and prevent autoimmunity.
  • B cells: CD19, CD20, CD21. They mature into plasma cells, which pour out antibody.
  • NK cells: CD16, CD56. They kill cells missing normal HLA class I (a sign of viral infection or cancer).
Memory hook: CD4 and CD8 with class I and II"Rule of 8": CD4 × class II = 8, and CD8 × class I = 8.

MHC (HLA): class I (HLA-A, B, C) is on all nucleated cells (and platelets). Class II (HLA-DR, DQ, DP) is only on antigen-presenting cells: macrophages, dendritic cells and B cells.

Flow cytometry counts these cells: forward scatter reflects size, side scatter reflects granularity, and fluorescent antibodies identify CD markers. Uses include the CD4 count in HIV and CD34 counts of stem cells for transplant.

Cytokines: the immune system's messages

  • IL-1 and TNF-alpha: fever and inflammation.
  • IL-2: makes T cells grow.
  • IL-4 and IL-5: drive B cells to make IgE, and grow eosinophils (allergy).
  • IL-6: the acute-phase response (CRP).
  • IL-8: calls neutrophils to the site.
  • Interferon-gamma: activates macrophages. TB-infected T cells release it when they see TB antigens, which is the basis of the IGRA (QuantiFERON) blood test.

Cytokines are measured by ELISA, multiplex bead assays or flow cytometry.

Check your understanding

A newborn's cord blood IgM is high. What does this suggest?

An infection acquired in the womb (TORCH). IgM can't cross the placenta, so it was made by the baby.

Which enzyme digestion produces two Fab fragments and one Fc?

Papain. (Pepsin produces F(ab')2.)

CD8 T cells recognise antigen on which HLA class?

HLA class I, found on all nucleated cells.

Which cytokine drives CRP production?

IL-6.

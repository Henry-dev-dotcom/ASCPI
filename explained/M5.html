Anaerobes are bacteria that can't survive in oxygen. They live in the gut, mouth and vagina, and cause infections when they get into deep tissue: abscesses, wound infections after bowel surgery, gas gangrene. This lesson also covers the Gram-positive rods (Listeria, Corynebacterium, Bacillus and the branching rods), which are easy to mix up if you don't anchor each one to its key clue.

Working with anaerobes

Because oxygen kills them, everything depends on the specimen:

  • Send tissue or aspirated fluid (pus drawn up in a syringe) in an anaerobic transport container. Swabs are poor.
  • Reject specimens that are always contaminated by normal anaerobic flora: expectorated sputum, voided urine, stool (except for C. difficile), and superficial skin swabs.
  • Culture in an anaerobic jar, pouch or chamber.

Gram-negative anaerobes: Bacteroides fragilis

Bacteroides fragilis is the most important anaerobe in clinical labs. It is the main anaerobe in abdominal abscesses after bowel leakage. Two clues identify it:

  • It grows in 20% bile (bile resistant), which fits its home in the bowel.
  • With special-potency identification disks, it is resistant to all three: kanamycin, vancomycin and colistin.

Like all anaerobes, it is naturally resistant to aminoglycosides, because those drugs need oxygen-dependent transport to enter the cell. It often makes beta-lactamase.

The clostridia: spore-forming Gram-positive rods

Clostridia make spores that survive heat and drying, and most of their diseases come from powerful toxins:

  • C. perfringens: large, boxcar-shaped rods that rarely show spores in the lab. Two clues: a double zone of hemolysis on blood agar (inner complete, outer partial) and a positive Nagler (lecithinase) test, from its alpha toxin, which breaks down cell membranes. "Stormy fermentation" in litmus milk. Causes gas gangrene and food poisoning.
  • C. difficile: makes toxin A and toxin B and causes antibiotic-associated colitis (antibiotics wipe out the normal gut flora and C. diff takes over). Test only liquid or unformed stool, using a toxin/GDH assay or PCR for the toxin gene. Formed stool is rejected: the patient may just be colonised.
  • C. botulinum: makes botulinum neurotoxin, which blocks acetylcholine release at nerve endings and causes flaccid (floppy) paralysis. Sources: home-canned food; honey in infants.
  • C. tetani: terminal round spores that make the cell look like a drumstick or tennis racket. Tetanus toxin causes spastic paralysis and lockjaw. Enters through puncture wounds.
Memory hook: two paralysis toxinsBotulinum makes you Bloppy (flaccid). Tetanus makes you Tight (spastic).

Other Gram-positive rods

  • Listeria monocytogenes: small rods with tumbling motility at 25 °C (room temperature), plus "umbrella" motility in semi-solid agar. It is weakly beta-hemolytic and catalase positive, and it grows in the fridge (4 °C), so "cold enrichment" helps isolate it. That also explains how it spreads: refrigerated deli meats, soft cheeses and unpasteurised milk. It threatens pregnant women, newborns, the elderly and the immunocompromised.
  • Corynebacterium diphtheriae: club-shaped rods in "Chinese letter" arrangements, with metachromatic granules. Grows on Loeffler serum and tellurite media (black colonies). Disease comes from diphtheria toxin (made only by strains carrying a phage). Confirm toxin with the Elek test or PCR. Other Corynebacterium species are usually skin flora ("diphtheroids").
  • Bacillus anthracis: large boxcar rods; non-motile, non-hemolytic, with sticky "medusa head" colonies. A select agent (bioterrorism); the lab must refer suspected isolates.
  • Bacillus cereus: motile and beta-hemolytic; food poisoning from reheated rice.
Exam trap: B. anthracis vs B. cereusOpposites: anthracis is non-motile and non-hemolytic; cereus is motile and beta-hemolytic.

Branching rods: Nocardia and Actinomyces

  • Nocardia: thin, branching Gram-positive rods that are partially (weakly) acid-fast with the modified acid-fast stain. Aerobic, found in soil. Causes lung disease and brain abscesses in immunocompromised patients.
  • Actinomyces: also branching, but not acid-fast and anaerobic. Part of mouth flora. Forms abscesses that drain yellow "sulfur granules" (clumps of organisms, not real sulfur), often after dental problems.

Bacteroides fragilis group.

A boxcar-shaped Gram-positive rod shows double-zone hemolysis and a positive Nagler test. Identify.

Clostridium perfringens.

Why is Listeria a risk in refrigerated foods?

It can grow at fridge temperatures (about 4 °C), unlike most food-borne bacteria.

A formed stool is sent for C. difficile testing. What should the lab do?

Reject it and ask for an unformed (liquid) stool. Testing formed stool detects colonisation, not disease.

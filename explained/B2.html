After ABO, the most important blood group is Rh, and then a handful of others: Kell, Duffy, Kidd, MNS, Lewis, P and I. You don't need to memorise hundreds of antigens. For each system you need to know: is the antibody IgM or IgG (harmless cold reactor, or dangerous warm reactor)? What do enzymes and DTT do to it? And does it cause hemolysis or HDFN?

The Rh system

"Rh positive" and "Rh negative" refer only to the D antigen. D is the most immunogenic antigen after A and B: most D-negative people exposed to D-positive red cells (by transfusion or pregnancy) make anti-D. Unlike ABO, there are no naturally occurring Rh antibodies; they appear only after exposure.

Rh antibodies are IgG, react at 37 °C / AHG, and usually don't activate complement. Coated cells are removed by macrophages in the spleen (extravascular hemolysis). IgG crosses the placenta, which is why anti-D is the classic cause of severe HDFN.

The Rh system has five main antigens: D, C, c, E, e. They are inherited together as a set (haplotype) on one chromosome. The shorthand (Wiener) names, worth learning:

  • R1 = DCe (most common in people of European descent), R2 = DcE, R0 = Dce (common in people of African descent), Rz = DCE (rare).
  • r = dce (the usual D-negative set), r' = dCe, r'' = dcE, ry = dCE.
Memory hook: reading the shorthandCapital R = D present; small r = no D. 1 (or a single prime ') means C; 2 (or a double prime ") means E; 0 means neither C nor E; z / y means both.
Worked exampleCells react with anti-D, anti-C and anti-e, but not anti-c or anti-E. The person has D, C and e only, so most probably R1R1 (DCe/DCe).

Weak D

Some people have D antigen in such small amounts (or slightly altered) that reagent anti-D only reacts after the AHG step. This is weak D.

  • Donors are tested for weak D, and weak-D donors are labeled Rh positive, because their cells could still immunise a D-negative recipient.
  • Patients: practice depends on lab policy. Many patients with serologic weak D are given D-negative blood and RhIG when pregnant until RHD genotyping shows a weak D type (types 1, 2 or 3) that can safely be treated as D-positive.

Rh null cells lack all Rh antigens. The Rh proteins help hold the membrane together, so Rh null cells are stomatocytes with chronic hemolysis.

Enzymes and DTT: chemical tools for antibody work

Treating red cells with enzymes (ficin, papain) strips off some surface structures. That makes some antigens easier to reach and destroys others:

  • Enhanced (react more strongly): Rh, Kidd, Lewis, I, P1.
  • Destroyed (no longer react): Duffy (Fya, Fyb), M, N, S (S is variable) and Xga.

DTT (and AET) break disulfide bonds. They destroy Kell antigens (and others such as Cartwright, Dombrock, Knops), and in serum they break up IgM pentamers. Useful for removing interference from anti-CD38 drugs (daratumumab) in myeloma patients.

Memory hook: enzymes"Enzymes make the Duffy MNS go away." Everything in Rh, Kidd, Lewis, I and P1 gets stronger.

The other blood groups

  • Kell (K, k): IgG, very immunogenic (second only to D). Anti-K causes hemolytic reactions and severe HDFN, because it also attacks red cell precursors in the fetal marrow. About 91% of people are K-negative. Destroyed by DTT and AET.
  • Duffy (Fya, Fyb): IgG, destroyed by enzymes. The Fy(a−b−) phenotype, common in people of African ancestry, lacks the receptor P. vivax malaria uses to enter red cells, so it protects against vivax.
  • Kidd (Jka, Jkb): IgG that binds complement. Kidd antibodies are famous for two dangerous habits: their level falls quickly after an exposure (so they can be undetectable on a later screen), then they bounce back rapidly when the antigen is seen again (anamnestic response), causing a delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction days later. Always honour a past history of anti-Jka or anti-Jkb.
  • MNS: M and N antibodies are usually IgM (cold, often harmless); S and s antibodies are IgG and clinically significant. Show dosage.
  • Lewis (Lea, Leb): IgM. Lewis antigens aren't made by red cells; they are made in tissues, float in the plasma, and are adsorbed onto red cells. Anti-Lewis doesn't cause HDFN (IgM can't cross the placenta, and fetal cells have little Lewis antigen). Lewis antigens may weaken in pregnancy.
  • P1: IgM, usually insignificant. Anti-P1 can be neutralised with hydatid cyst fluid or pigeon egg white.
  • I / i: IgM cold autoantibodies. Adult cells carry I, and cord cells carry i. Anti-I is seen in cold agglutinin disease and after Mycoplasma pneumonia; anti-i after infectious mononucleosis.
Memory hook: the clinically significant warm antibodiesThe IgG antibodies that cause hemolysis and HDFN: "Rh, Kell, Duffy, Kidd, Ss". The IgM ones that are usually harmless and cold: "Lewis, I, P1, M, N" ("Lie Patiently in the MN cold").

Dosage

Some antibodies react more strongly with cells from people who inherited two copies of the antigen gene (homozygous) than with cells that have only one copy (heterozygous). A Jk(a+b−) cell has a double dose of Jka and reacts more strongly with anti-Jka than a Jk(a+b+) cell. Dosage is seen in Rh (except D), Kidd, Duffy and MNS. That's why antibodies like these should be ruled out only using homozygous cells.

Finding compatible blood

Once a patient has an antibody, you need donors negative for that antigen. To estimate how many units to screen, multiply the frequencies of antigen-negative donors.

Worked exampleThe patient has anti-K and anti-Fya. About 91% of donors are K-negative and 34% are Fy(a−). Compatible = 0.91 × 0.34 ≈ 0.31 (31%). To find 2 units, expect to screen about 2 ÷ 0.31 ≈ 7 units.

High- and low-prevalence antigens

  • An antibody to a high-prevalence antigen (one almost everyone has, like k, Kpb, Jsb, Vel) reacts with nearly every panel cell, but the autocontrol is negative because the patient lacks the antigen. Compatible blood is rare: test family members or use the rare donor registry.
  • An antibody to a low-prevalence antigen (Kpa, Wra) reacts with only one odd panel cell; nearly every donor unit is compatible.

Check your understanding

A donor's cells are negative with anti-D at immediate spin but positive after AHG. How is the unit labeled?

Rh positive (weak D). It could immunise a D-negative recipient.

Which antibodies would be destroyed if the panel cells were enzyme-treated?

Anti-Fya, anti-Fyb, anti-M, anti-N (and often anti-S). Rh, Kidd and Lewis reactions would be stronger.

Why can anti-Jka be missed and then cause a reaction a week after transfusion?

Kidd antibodies drop below detectable levels quickly, then rise rapidly after re-exposure (anamnestic response), causing a delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction.

All panel cells react 2+ at AHG, the autocontrol and DAT are negative. Most likely explanation?

An alloantibody to a high-prevalence antigen.

Most laboratory errors happen before the sample ever reaches the analyzer: the wrong patient, the wrong tube, the wrong order, a long tourniquet, a delay. This lesson explains the collection rules and the reasons behind them, then the everyday lab math (dilutions, solutions, conversions) that shows up as quick calculation questions.

Patient identification comes first

Use two independent identifiers (such as full name and date of birth, or name and medical record number), checked against the request, and label tubes at the bedside in front of the patient. Never use a room or bed number; patients move. A mislabeled sample is worse than no sample, because it puts one patient's result in another's chart.

Order of draw: why it matters

When several tubes are filled from one needle, a tiny amount of additive from one tube can be carried into the next. The CLSI order is designed so that each carry-over does the least harm:

  1. Blood culture bottles (sterile; must come first to avoid contamination).
  2. Light blue (sodium citrate): coagulation. Comes before tubes whose clot activators or other additives would start clotting or interfere.
  3. Red or gold (serum, with or without gel/clot activator).
  4. Green (heparin).
  5. Lavender (EDTA): CBC, blood bank. EDTA carried into a chemistry tube adds potassium (K2EDTA) and binds calcium, so it must come late.
  6. Gray (sodium fluoride/potassium oxalate): glucose, lactate. Fluoride and oxalate damage cells and bind calcium, so last.
Memory hook"Boys Love Ray's Garage Late Greasy": Blood culture, Light blue, Red, Green, Lavender, Gray.
Exam trap: EDTA contaminationVery high potassium with very low calcium (and low ALP and magnesium) in a well patient = EDTA carry-over or drawing the chemistry tube after the lavender. Recollect in the right order.

Venipuncture technique and its effects

  • Keep the tourniquet under 1 minute. Longer, and water leaks out of the vein, concentrating proteins, cells and protein-bound substances (calcium, cholesterol).
  • Fist pumping releases potassium from working forearm muscles, falsely raising K.
  • Hemolysis (small needle, forcing blood through a syringe, shaking tubes) releases what is inside red cells: potassium, LD, AST, phosphate and magnesium rise.
  • Avoid an arm with an IV running (the sample would be diluted with IV fluid, often showing high glucose or electrolytes), a hematoma, the side of a mastectomy (lymph drainage problems), or a dialysis fistula.
  • Invert additive tubes gently the right number of times, so the additive mixes without causing hemolysis.

Capillary (skin puncture) collection

  • Infants: the outer side of the heel, no deeper than 2 mm (to avoid hitting the heel bone).
  • Older children and adults: the side of the fingertip pad of the 3rd or 4th finger.
  • Wipe away the first drop, which contains tissue fluid.
  • For capillary collections, the EDTA tube is filled first, the opposite of venipuncture, because platelets start clumping at the wound, which would ruin the CBC.
  • Don't squeeze hard ("milking"), which adds tissue fluid and causes hemolysis.

Special handling: match the condition to the reason

  • On ice: ammonia (keeps rising in the tube), lactate (cells keep making it), blood gases if delayed (cells keep using oxygen), ACTH and some other hormones (unstable).
  • Protect from light: bilirubin (breaks down in light), some vitamins.
  • Keep warm (37 °C): cold agglutinins and cryoglobulins. They would attach to cells or precipitate in the cold and be lost with the clot.
  • Don't refrigerate before separating: potassium. In the cold, the cells' sodium-potassium pumps stop and potassium leaks out.
  • Separate serum or plasma within 2 hours for most chemistry, or cells keep altering the sample (glucose falls, potassium and phosphate rise).
  • Fasting 8-12 hours for glucose and lipids (triglycerides especially).

Specimen rejection and chain of custody

Reject specimens that are unlabeled or mislabeled, in the wrong tube, clotted (an EDTA tube for a CBC), too little volume (wrong blood-to-additive ratio, especially citrate), too old or stored wrongly, or obviously contaminated (drawn above an IV). Document and request a new sample.

For legal samples (forensic drug tests, blood alcohol), a chain of custody form records every person who handles the specimen, with dates and times, so it can be proved in court that it wasn't tampered with.

Lab math made easy

Dilutions. A dilution is sample volume ÷ total volume. Adding 0.5 mL serum to 4.5 mL saline gives 0.5 ÷ 5.0 = 1:10 (not 1:9, because the total includes the sample). To get the real concentration, multiply the diluted result by the dilution factor (10).

Serial dilutions multiply. A 1:2 followed by a 1:5 = 1:10. In a two-fold series starting at 1:2, the tubes are 1:2, 1:4, 1:8, 1:16, 1:32...

C1 × V1 = C2 × V2 (making a weaker solution from a stronger one). To make 100 mL of 10% bleach from 100% stock: 100% × V1 = 10% × 100 mL, so V1 = 10 mL stock + 90 mL water.

Solutions:

  • Percent (w/v) = grams per 100 mL. A 10% NaCl solution has 10 g per 100 mL, so 250 mL needs 25 g.
  • Molarity (M) = moles per liter (moles = grams ÷ molecular weight).
  • Normality (N) = equivalents per liter. For an acid, equivalents = moles × the number of H+ it can release (1 M H2SO4 = 2 N).

Conversions: °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9. Glucose mg/dL ÷ 18 = mmol/L. Creatinine mg/dL × 88.4 = µmol/L.

Worked examples98.6 °F → (98.6 − 32) × 5/9 = 37 °C.
Glucose 126 mg/dL ÷ 18 = 7.0 mmol/L.
Creatinine 1.2 mg/dL × 88.4 = 106 µmol/L.

Equipment basics

  • Temperatures (recorded daily): refrigerator 2-8 °C (blood bank fridge 1-6 °C), freezer −20 °C or colder, incubator 35-37 °C, room temperature 20-25 °C.
  • Centrifuge: the force depends on both speed and radius: RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × radius (cm) × rpm². Check speed (with a tachometer) and timers regularly.
  • Laboratory water: Type I (purest) for sensitive work (reagents, trace metals, molecular); Type II for general lab tests; Type III for rinsing glassware.
  • pH meters: calibrate with at least two buffers that bracket the expected pH.
  • Pipettes: check accuracy and precision regularly; read the meniscus at eye level (bottom of the curve for clear liquids).

Check your understanding

Which tube follows the light blue tube in the order of draw?

The red or gold (serum) tube.

A skin puncture needs a CBC and a chemistry tube. Which is filled first?

The EDTA (CBC) tube, to prevent platelet clumping from affecting the count.

How do you prepare 500 mL of 0.85% saline?

0.85 g per 100 mL × 5 = 4.25 g NaCl, made up to 500 mL with water.

Why must a cryoglobulin sample be kept at 37 °C until separated?

Cryoglobulins precipitate in the cold and would be lost with the clot, giving a falsely negative result.

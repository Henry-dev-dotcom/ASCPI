The Enterobacterales (once called Enterobacteriaceae) are the gut's Gram-negative rods: E. coli, Klebsiella, Salmonella, Shigella, Proteus and their relatives. They are the most common cause of urinary infections and many hospital infections. They look identical on a Gram stain, so you identify them by what they do: which sugars they ferment and which products they make.

What all Enterobacterales share

Every member is a Gram-negative rod that:

  • is oxidase negative (this separates them from Pseudomonas, Vibrio and others),
  • ferments glucose,
  • reduces nitrate to nitrite (the reason a urine nitrite test detects them),
  • grows well on MacConkey agar.

Step 1: lactose fermenter or not?

On MacConkey agar, lactose fermenters produce acid and turn pink; non-fermenters stay colorless. This is the first big split:

  • Lactose fermenters (pink): E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, (Citrobacter, variable).
  • Non-fermenters (colorless): Salmonella, Shigella, Proteus, Yersinia, Serratia (late), and the non-Enterobacterales Pseudomonas.

The stool pathogens Salmonella and Shigella are both lactose negative, which is why stool cultures use lactose-containing plates to spot them among the pink E. coli.

The TSI tube: reading sugar fermentation

The triple sugar iron (TSI) slant has a little glucose, lots of lactose and sucrose, a pH indicator (acid = yellow, alkaline = red) and iron to detect H2S. Read the slant / butt:

  • A/A (yellow/yellow): ferments glucose and lactose or sucrose. There is so much sugar that the acid lasts everywhere (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter).
  • K/A (red/yellow): ferments glucose only. The small amount of glucose is used up; the slant (with air) then breaks down protein and turns alkaline (red), while the butt stays acid (Salmonella, Shigella, Proteus).
  • K/K (red/red): ferments nothing, so it is a non-fermenter such as Pseudomonas, and not an Enterobacterales.
  • Black butt = H2S (Salmonella, Proteus, Citrobacter). Cracks or bubbles = gas.

IMViC: four classic tests

Indole, Methyl red, Voges-Proskauer, Citrate (the "i" is just for pronunciation):

  • E. coli = + + − − (indole +, MR +, VP −, citrate −).
  • Klebsiella and Enterobacter = − − + + (the exact opposite).
Why MR and VP are oppositesBoth tests look at how glucose is fermented. Methyl red detects strong acids from "mixed acid" fermentation (E. coli). VP detects acetoin, a neutral product of the "butylene glycol" pathway (Klebsiella, Enterobacter). An organism usually uses one pathway or the other.

The main organisms

  • E. coli: lactose +, indole +, citrate −. The most common cause of UTIs, plus neonatal meningitis and diarrhoea (see below). Green metallic sheen on EMB.
  • Klebsiella pneumoniae: very mucoid colonies (big capsule), lactose +, non-motile, indole −, VP +. Pneumonia (often in alcoholics), UTIs, hospital infections and carbapenemase producers (KPC).
  • Enterobacter: like Klebsiella but motile; citrate +, VP +. Often resistant through inducible AmpC beta-lactamase.
  • Salmonella: lactose −, H2S +, motile. Food poisoning (eggs, poultry); S. Typhi causes typhoid fever.
  • Shigella: lactose −, non-motile, no H2S, no gas. Bloody diarrhoea (dysentery) from a very small number of organisms.
  • Proteus: swarms across blood agar in waves, urease + (fast), H2S +, PAD + (phenylalanine deaminase). The urease raises urine pH and helps form struvite stones. P. mirabilis is indole −; P. vulgaris is indole +.
  • Serratia: may make a red pigment; DNase +, gelatinase +; hospital infections.
  • Citrobacter: H2S +, citrate +, lactose variable. Can be confused with Salmonella.
  • Yersinia: Y. pestis (plague) shows bipolar "safety pin" staining. Y. enterocolitica grows in the cold (survives refrigerated blood products and foods), is urease +, and gives bull's-eye colonies on CIN agar; causes diarrhoea and pain that mimics appendicitis.
Memory hook: Salmonella vs ShigellaSalmonella Swims (motile) and Stinks (H2S +). Shigella does neither: non-motile, H2S negative.

E. coli types that cause diarrhoea

Different E. coli strains carry different toxins or invasion tools, so they cause different illnesses:

  • ETEC (enterotoxigenic): traveler's diarrhoea. Heat-labile and heat-stable toxins pull water into the gut, giving watery diarrhoea.
  • EPEC (enteropathogenic): infant diarrhoea in developing countries.
  • EIEC (enteroinvasive): invades the gut wall like Shigella; often a slow lactose fermenter and non-motile, so it can be mistaken for Shigella.
  • EHEC / STEC (enterohemorrhagic, Shiga toxin-producing, mainly O157:H7): undercooked hamburger; bloody diarrhoea without fever; the Shiga toxin can cause hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) in children. Unlike most E. coli, O157:H7 doesn't ferment sorbitol, so it forms colorless colonies on sorbitol-MacConkey (SMAC). Shiga toxin assays or PCR detect non-O157 strains.
  • EAEC (enteroaggregative): long-lasting diarrhoea.

Check your understanding

A stool isolate is lactose negative, H2S negative, non-motile, and K/A on TSI with no gas. Identify.

Shigella.

An organism gives K/K on TSI. Is it an Enterobacterales?

No. K/K means no glucose fermentation, which suggests a non-fermenter such as Pseudomonas. All Enterobacterales ferment glucose.

Which E. coli is detected by colorless colonies on sorbitol-MacConkey?

E. coli O157:H7 (EHEC/STEC), which doesn't ferment sorbitol.

A swarming, urease-positive urine isolate is indole negative. Which species?

Proteus mirabilis (P. vulgaris is indole positive).

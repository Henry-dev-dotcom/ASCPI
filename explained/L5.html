A lab isn't just instruments and tests; it is people, money, records and responsibility. This lesson covers how labs keep quality across the whole testing process, manage staff, equipment, reagents and budgets, handle information and ethics, and teach others. Questions here are mostly common-sense "best practice" scenarios, so understanding the purpose of each rule is the key.

Quality across the whole testing process

A result can go wrong at any of three phases, and quality assurance (QA) watches all of them:

  • Preanalytic (before testing): ordering, patient ID, collection, labelling, transport. Most errors happen here.
  • Analytic (the testing itself): calibration, QC, instrument function, technique.
  • Postanalytic (after testing): result entry and transcription, reporting, interpretation, critical value calls.

QA is bigger than QC. It also includes competency assessment, equipment maintenance, proficiency testing, corrective action, document control and monitoring of quality indicators (turnaround times, rejection rates).

Critical values

A critical (panic) value is a result so abnormal that the patient may be in danger unless someone acts now (for example potassium 6.8, glucose 40, a positive blood culture). It must be:

  1. Called promptly to the responsible clinician or caregiver (not left in the system for someone to find).
  2. Read back by the person receiving it, to confirm it was heard correctly.
  3. Documented: who was told, the date and time, the result, and the read-back.

Quality improvement tools

  • PDCA (Plan-Do-Check-Act): plan a change, try it on a small scale, check the results, then act (adopt it, adjust it, or drop it). Repeat.
  • Root-cause analysis (RCA): after an error, keep asking "why?" until you reach the underlying system problem, rather than stopping at "someone made a mistake".
  • Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram: sorts possible causes into categories (people, methods, equipment, materials, environment) on the "bones" of a fish.
  • Pareto chart: a bar chart of problems from most to least frequent, showing the "vital few" causes behind most of the trouble (the 80/20 rule).
  • Incident (occurrence) reports exist to find and fix system problems, not to blame or punish individuals.

People: competency, management and evaluation

Competency assessment (CLIA) proves each person can perform each test correctly: for new staff at 6 months and 12 months (twice in the first year), then annually. It uses six elements: direct observation of testing; monitoring how results are recorded and reported; reviewing worksheets, QC and maintenance records; direct observation of instrument maintenance; testing known samples (blind or PT samples); and problem-solving exercises.

Leadership styles:

  • Autocratic: the leader decides alone. Useful in emergencies, but staff may feel excluded.
  • Democratic (participative): the group helps decide. Builds buy-in; slower.
  • Laissez-faire: hands-off. Works only with highly skilled, self-motivated teams.

Human resources: clear job descriptions, orientation and training, annual performance reviews, and progressive discipline (verbal warning, written warning, suspension, termination) that is documented at each step.

Documents, equipment and reagents

  • Procedure manuals: each test's procedure states its purpose, principle, specimen requirements, reagents, step-by-step procedure, calculations, QC, reference ranges, limitations and references. Reviewed at least annually by the director, and old versions are archived.
  • Instruments: daily, weekly and monthly maintenance logs; verify performance after repairs; calibration verification at least every 6 months (and after major maintenance, reagent lot changes or QC problems).
  • Reagents: label with identity, concentration, date prepared or opened, expiry, storage requirements and the preparer's initials. Test each new lot with QC before using it on patients. Use first in, first out (FIFO), and never use expired reagents, even if QC passes.
  • Point-of-care testing (POCT): tests at the bedside (glucose meters, blood gases). The lab oversees operator training, QC and competency. Know each device's limits: for example, an abnormal hematocrit can bias some glucose meters.

Information systems, privacy and ethics

  • The LIS (laboratory information system) handles orders, specimen tracking, instrument interfaces, results, autoverification rules (automatically releasing results that pass defined checks) and an audit trail of every change.
  • Corrected reports: clearly marked "corrected", the original result kept on record, and the clinician notified.
  • HIPAA protects patient health information. Share only the minimum necessary information, only with people authorised and involved in the patient's care. Never discuss patients in public places or give results to unverified callers.
  • Professional ethics: keep confidentiality, report honestly, never falsify or "dry-lab" results, admit and report errors, and work within your scope of practice.

Money: budgets, costs and break-even

  • Direct costs belong to a specific test: reagents, supplies, labour time. Indirect (overhead) costs are shared: rent, utilities, administration.
  • Fixed costs stay the same however many tests you run (instrument lease, salaries). Variable costs rise with each test (reagents).
  • Cost per test = sum of the costs per test. Reagents $1.50 + labour $0.75 + overhead $0.25 = $2.50.
  • Break-even point (number of tests needed to cover costs) = fixed costs ÷ (price per test − variable cost per test).
  • Productivity is tracked as tests (or billable tests) per FTE (one full-time equivalent = one person working full time).
  • Inventory: FIFO, minimum (par) levels for reordering, and tracking of expiry dates.
  • Adding a new test: compare cost, turnaround time, accuracy and clinical usefulness against sending it to a reference lab.
Worked exampleAn instrument has fixed costs of $50,000 a year. Each test sells for $15 and costs $5 in reagents. Break-even = 50,000 ÷ (15 − 5) = 5,000 tests a year.

Education principles

  • Adults learn best (andragogy) when the material is relevant to their work, practical and partly self-directed.
  • Learning objectives should be measurable, using action verbs: "the student will calculate a corrected WBC", not "will understand".
  • Three learning domains: cognitive (knowledge and thinking: explaining Westgard rules), psychomotor (physical skills: venipuncture, pipetting), affective (attitudes and values: professionalism, respecting confidentiality).
  • Teaching a skill: explain, demonstrate, let the learner practise under supervision, then give prompt, specific feedback.
  • Evaluate with written questions (cognitive), direct observation and checklists (psychomotor), and observed behaviour (affective).

Check your understanding

A potassium of 6.9 must be reported. What three things are required?

Call it promptly to the responsible caregiver, get a read-back, and document who was told, when, and the result.

When must a new employee's competency be assessed?

At 6 months and 12 months in the first year, then annually.

A student practising venipuncture is learning in which domain?

Psychomotor.

A reagent expired yesterday but QC is in range. Can it be used?

No. Expired reagents must not be used for patient testing, even if QC passes.

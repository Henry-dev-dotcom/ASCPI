White cell questions ask one of two things: is this a normal reaction to something (infection, allergy, stress), or is it a cancer of the blood-forming cells? Once you know which white cell is raised and what it looks like, most answers follow. This lesson builds that reasoning and then walks through the leukemias, lymphomas and plasma cell diseases.

Reactive changes: the body responding normally

Each white cell has a job, so the one that is raised hints at the cause:

  • Neutrophils fight bacteria. Neutrophilia means bacterial infection, steroids (they release neutrophils stuck to vessel walls) or physical stress.
  • Eosinophils fight parasites and drive allergy. Think "allergy, parasites, drugs".
  • Basophils rarely rise. A raised basophil count is a red flag for CML or another myeloproliferative disease.
  • Monocytes clean up. They rise in TB, chronic inflammation and during recovery from infection.
  • Lymphocytes fight viruses. Lymphocytosis suggests a viral infection or pertussis (whooping cough); in an older adult with lots of small mature lymphocytes, think CLL.

Left shift and toxic changes. In a bad bacterial infection the marrow releases neutrophils before they are fully mature, so more bands and even metamyelocytes appear ("shift to the left" on the old counting sheets). Rushed neutrophils also show toxic granulation (dark granules), Döhle bodies (blue patches of leftover RNA) and vacuoles. Together these say "bacterial infection".

Reactive lymphocytes are big, with lots of blue cytoplasm that "hugs" the neighbouring red cells. Seen with a positive heterophile (Monospot) test, they mean infectious mononucleosis (EBV). The reactive cells are mostly T cells responding to infected B cells.

Inherited neutrophil oddities that show up as smear questions:

  • Pelger-Huët anomaly: neutrophils with only two lobes ("pince-nez" glasses). They work normally, so it is harmless. Don't mistake them for bands. (Acquired "pseudo-Pelger" cells appear in myelodysplasia.)
  • Chédiak-Higashi: giant fused granules; infections and albinism.
  • May-Hegglin: giant platelets with Döhle-like inclusions in the neutrophils.
  • Alder-Reilly: dark granules in all white cells, seen in mucopolysaccharide storage diseases.

Leukemoid reaction vs CML: the LAP score

A severe infection can push the WBC very high with immature cells, which can look like chronic myeloid leukemia. The LAP score (leukocyte alkaline phosphatase) separates them. Healthy, activated neutrophils are full of LAP, so a leukemoid reaction has a high LAP. The neutrophils in CML come from a cancerous clone and have very little, so CML has a low LAP. Basophilia and the Philadelphia chromosome also point to CML.

Acute vs chronic leukemia

"Acute" and "chronic" describe how mature the cancer cells are, not how long the patient has been ill:

  • Acute leukemia: the cells are stuck as blasts. Blasts can't do the job of mature cells and they crowd the marrow, so the patient gets anemia, bleeding and infections quickly. The WHO cut-off is 20% blasts.
  • Chronic leukemia: the cells mature but are made in huge numbers. It develops slowly.

Then ask: myeloid or lymphoid? That gives four main types: AML, ALL, CML, CLL.

Telling blasts apart with stains and markers

Under the microscope, blasts look alike, so we use cytochemical stains and flow cytometry markers:

  • Myeloperoxidase (MPO) and Sudan black B stain granules found in myeloid cells. Positive = AML.
  • Nonspecific esterase (NSE) stains monocytic cells (AML M4 and M5).
  • TdT is an enzyme found in the nucleus of immature lymphocytes. Positive = ALL lymphoblasts.
  • PAS gives "block" positivity in lymphoblasts and stains abnormal red cell precursors in M6.
  • Auer rods (pink needle-like rods of fused granules) prove the blast is myeloid. They are never seen in ALL.
Memory hook: who stains with what"MPO and Sudan black = Myeloid; NSE = moNocytes; TdT = Tiny lymphoblasts."

The AML subtypes worth knowing

  • APL (M3), t(15;17), PML-RARA. The cells are promyelocytes packed with granules and bundles of Auer rods ("faggot cells"). Those granules release tissue factor, which sets off DIC, so APL is a bleeding emergency. It responds to all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), which forces the stuck promyelocytes to mature.
  • M4 and M5: monocytic blasts, NSE positive, often infiltrating gums and skin.
  • M6: erythroleukemia, abnormal PAS-positive red cell precursors.
  • M7: megakaryoblastic, marked by CD41 and CD61 (platelet glycoproteins); often causes marrow fibrosis and is linked to Down syndrome in children.

The lymphoid and chronic leukemias

  • ALL: the most common childhood cancer. Lymphoblasts are TdT positive and MPO/Sudan black negative. Most are B-cell (CD10, CD19).
  • CLL: older adults, very high counts of small mature lymphocytes. These cells are fragile and burst when the smear is spread, leaving smudge cells. Markers: B cell markers CD19, CD23 plus CD5 (normally a T-cell marker).
  • Hairy cell leukemia: B cells with fine hair-like projections. TRAP positive; CD11c, CD25, CD103; often with a big spleen and a "dry tap" marrow.
  • CML: high WBC with every stage of neutrophil development, basophilia, low LAP. Cause: the Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22), which joins BCR and ABL into a gene that is permanently switched on. Drugs such as imatinib block this BCR-ABL enzyme.

Myeloproliferative neoplasms and MDS

Myeloproliferative neoplasms are marrow cancers where cells mature but are overproduced:

  • Polycythemia vera: too many red cells. The JAK2 V617F mutation keeps the growth signal on, so red cells are made without EPO. That is why EPO is low.
  • Essential thrombocythemia: very high platelets.
  • Primary myelofibrosis: the marrow scars, so blood cells are made in the spleen and liver instead. Cells squeezed through the scarred marrow come out as teardrops, and a marrow aspirate is a "dry tap".

Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) are the opposite problem: the marrow is busy but makes faulty cells. Expect dysplasia (odd-looking cells), macrocytosis, pseudo-Pelger cells and ring sideroblasts. Blasts stay under 20%; above that it is called AML.

Erythrocytosis (too many red cells) has three causes. It can be relative (low plasma volume, as in dehydration). It can be primary: polycythemia vera with low EPO. Or it can be secondary: high EPO driven by hypoxia (smoking, lung disease, altitude) or an EPO-secreting tumour.

Lymphomas

Lymphomas are cancers of lymphocytes that mainly live in lymph nodes. Each type has a hallmark:

  • Hodgkin lymphoma: the Reed-Sternberg cell, a big cell with two mirror-image nuclei ("owl's eyes"), CD15 and CD30 positive.
  • Burkitt lymphoma: fast-growing, "starry sky" look under the microscope (pale macrophages among dark tumour cells), t(8;14) moving MYC.
  • Follicular lymphoma: t(14;18) switching on BCL2, a gene that stops cells from dying.
  • Mantle cell lymphoma: t(11;14), cyclin D1, CD5 positive (like CLL, but CD23 negative).

Plasma cell disorders

Multiple myeloma is a cancer of one plasma cell clone. Because one clone makes one antibody, you get a single sharp monoclonal spike on protein electrophoresis, often free light chains in the urine (Bence Jones protein), and rouleaux on the smear (excess protein makes cells stack). Diagnosis needs more than 10% plasma cells in the marrow plus organ damage, remembered as CRAB: high Calcium, Renal failure, Anemia, Bone lesions.

Waldenström macroglobulinemia makes an IgM paraprotein. IgM is a big pentamer, so the blood becomes thick (hyperviscosity): headaches, blurred vision, bleeding.

Platelet-related emergencies (preview of the next lesson)

  • TTP: missing ADAMTS13, the enzyme that trims vWF. Giant vWF strands grab platelets and form clots in small vessels, giving low platelets, schistocytes, fever, kidney and brain signs. PT and aPTT are normal.
  • HUS: usually children after E. coli O157:H7 diarrhoea; the Shiga toxin damages kidney vessels.
  • HIT: antibodies form against heparin bound to platelet factor 4, activating platelets. The platelet count drops about 5-10 days after heparin starts, and the danger is clotting, not bleeding. Stop heparin and give a direct thrombin inhibitor such as argatroban.

Check your understanding

WBC 60 × 10⁹/L, toxic granulation, Döhle bodies, high LAP, no basophilia. Leukemoid reaction or CML?

Leukemoid reaction. A high LAP and toxic changes point to a reactive process; CML has a low LAP and basophilia.

Blasts are MPO negative, Sudan black negative and TdT positive. Which leukemia?

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL).

Why does APL so often cause DIC?

The abnormal promyelocytes' granules release procoagulant (tissue factor-like) material, which triggers clotting throughout the body.

Why is EPO low in polycythemia vera but high in a heavy smoker with polycythemia?

In PV the JAK2 mutation drives red cell production without EPO, so the kidney turns EPO down. In smokers, carbon monoxide causes tissue hypoxia, so the kidney raises EPO.

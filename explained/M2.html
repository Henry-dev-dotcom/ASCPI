Gram-positive cocci are some of the most common organisms you will meet: staphylococci, streptococci and enterococci. Identifying them is a decision tree. Each test splits the group in two, and you only need a few branches to name almost any isolate. Learn the order of the questions and the answers follow.

Step 1: clusters or chains? Catalase answers it

On a Gram stain, staphylococci grow in grape-like clusters, and streptococci and enterococci grow in pairs and chains. Shapes on a smear can be unclear, so confirm with catalase:

  • Catalase positive (bubbles) → Staphylococcus.
  • Catalase negative → Streptococcus or Enterococcus.

The staphylococci

Next question: coagulase.

  • S. aureus: coagulase positive. The big pathogen: skin abscesses, wound infections, pneumonia, bone and blood infections, toxic shock and food poisoning. Also mannitol positive (yellow on mannitol salt agar), often beta-hemolytic with golden colonies, and DNase positive. MRSA carries the mecA gene, which codes for an altered penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a) that beta-lactam drugs can't bind.
  • Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) are mostly skin flora. To split the two you need to know, use novobiocin:
    • S. epidermidis: novobiocin sensitive. Lives on skin; the usual blood culture contaminant, but a real pathogen on plastic devices (IV lines, prosthetic joints, heart valves) because it forms a biofilm.
    • S. saprophyticus: novobiocin resistant. Causes urinary tract infections in young, sexually active women.
Memory hook"Saprophyticus is Stubborn": it resists novobiocin. "Epidermidis is Easy": it is sensitive.

The streptococci: start with hemolysis

Look at the plate first. The type of hemolysis on sheep blood agar splits strep into groups:

Beta-hemolytic (clear zones):

  • S. pyogenes (group A): bacitracin sensitive and PYR positive. Causes strep throat, impetigo, cellulitis, necrotising fasciitis, and later complications: rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.
  • S. agalactiae (group B): bacitracin resistant, CAMP positive (its CAMP factor teams up with S. aureus beta-toxin to make an arrowhead-shaped zone of hemolysis), and hippurate positive. Lives in the vagina and causes newborn sepsis and meningitis, which is why pregnant women are screened at 35-37 weeks.

Alpha-hemolytic (green):

  • S. pneumoniae: optochin sensitive and bile soluble. Gram stain shows lancet-shaped diplococci (pointed pairs); colonies may look "dimpled" in the middle as the cells break themselves down. The top cause of pneumonia, otitis media and adult meningitis. Its capsule causes disease and swells with specific antiserum (the Quellung reaction).
  • Viridans streptococci: optochin resistant and bile insoluble. Normal mouth flora; after dental work they can seed damaged heart valves and cause subacute endocarditis.
Why optochin and bile solubility both work for S. pneumoniaeS. pneumoniae carries an autolysin, a self-destruct enzyme. Bile salts trigger it, so the colony dissolves. Optochin disrupts the organism's membrane enzyme (ATPase), so it is killed around the disk. Viridans strep lack both weak spots.
Exam trap: optochin in CO2Incubating the optochin disk in CO2 can shrink the zone, so a real pneumococcus may look resistant. When the zone is borderline, confirm with bile solubility.

Enterococcus vs group D strep

Both are usually non-hemolytic (gamma) and both are bile esculin positive (they grow in bile and turn esculin agar black). Two tests separate them:

  • Enterococcus (E. faecalis, E. faecium): grows in 6.5% NaCl and is PYR positive. Causes UTIs, wound infections and endocarditis, and is naturally resistant to many drugs. VRE = vancomycin-resistant enterococci, a major hospital problem.
  • S. bovis group (S. gallolyticus): bile esculin positive but no growth in 6.5% NaCl. A blood infection with S. bovis is linked to colon cancer, so the patient needs a colonoscopy.

The whole tree in one view

  1. Catalase + → Staph → coagulase + = S. aureus; coagulase − → novobiocin S = S. epidermidis, R = S. saprophyticus.
  2. Catalase − → look at hemolysis:
    • Beta → bacitracin S / PYR + = group A; CAMP + / hippurate + = group B.
    • Alpha → optochin S / bile soluble = S. pneumoniae; resistant = viridans.
    • Gamma → bile esculin + → 6.5% NaCl + / PYR + = Enterococcus; NaCl − = S. bovis group.

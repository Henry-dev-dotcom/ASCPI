Toxicology answers two questions: is this patient poisoned, and is this patient's medicine at a safe and effective level? The second part, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), is mostly about when to draw the blood. Get the timing wrong and even a perfect test gives a useless answer.

Why we monitor some drugs and not others

Most drugs are safe across a wide range of doses. A few have a narrow therapeutic range: the effective level sits close to the toxic level, and patients handle the drug very differently (kidney or liver function, other drugs, age). Those drugs need blood levels. Examples: aminoglycosides, vancomycin, digoxin, lithium, phenytoin, theophylline and transplant drugs.

Peaks, troughs and steady state

After each dose, the drug level rises to a peak and then falls to a trough just before the next dose.

  • Trough = drawn just before the next dose. It shows the lowest level and is used for most drugs (too high a trough means the drug is building up toward toxicity).
  • Peak = drawn after the drug has been absorbed and distributed (for IV aminoglycosides, about 30 minutes after the infusion ends). It shows whether the level is high enough to work.
  • Steady state: with regular dosing, levels settle into a repeating pattern after about 5 half-lives. Drawing before then underestimates the long-term level.
Why digoxin must be drawn 6-8 hours after the doseDigoxin moves from the blood into heart and muscle tissue slowly. In the first hours, blood levels are high even though tissue levels (where it acts) are not yet. A level drawn too early reads falsely high and could lead to a wrong dose cut. Toxicity is also worse with low potassium (and low magnesium, high calcium), so check electrolytes alongside it.

Drug groups and their key points

  • Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin): damage the kidney and inner ear. Measure peak (effectiveness) and trough (toxicity).
  • Vancomycin: monitored by trough (or by area-under-the-curve dosing in newer practice).
  • Cardiac drugs: digoxin (above); procainamide is converted into an active metabolite, NAPA, so both are measured.
  • Anticonvulsants (phenytoin, valproic acid, carbamazepine, phenobarbital): phenytoin is about 90% protein-bound. Only the free (unbound) part works. If albumin is low (liver disease, malnutrition, kidney failure), more is free, so a "normal" total level can still be toxic. Measure free phenytoin in these patients.
  • Lithium (bipolar disorder): draw 12 hours after the last dose. Never use a lithium-heparin (green) tube; it adds lithium and gives a falsely high result.
  • Tricyclic antidepressants: toxic to the heart in overdose.
  • Immunosuppressants (cyclosporine, tacrolimus): they concentrate inside red cells, so use whole blood (EDTA), drawn as a trough.
  • Theophylline, methotrexate: narrow ranges; methotrexate levels guide leucovorin rescue.

Common poisonings

Acetaminophen (paracetamol). In overdose, the normal safe pathway is overwhelmed and a toxic metabolite (NAPQI) builds up, destroying liver cells. The level is plotted on the Rumack-Matthew nomogram against hours since ingestion, starting at 4 hours (earlier levels aren't meaningful because absorption is still going on). The antidote, N-acetylcysteine, restores glutathione, which neutralises NAPQI.

Salicylate (aspirin). It first stimulates the breathing centre, so the patient breathes fast: respiratory alkalosis. Then it disrupts cell energy production, causing lactic and keto acids to build up: high anion gap metabolic acidosis. Tinnitus (ringing in the ears) is a classic clue.

Carbon monoxide. CO binds hemoglobin about 200 times more tightly than oxygen. Standard pulse oximeters can't tell carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin, so they read falsely normal. Measure carboxyhemoglobin by co-oximetry. Patients may look "cherry red".

Toxic alcohols. All raise the osmolal gap early. As they are metabolised into acids, the anion gap rises:

  • Methanol (windshield fluid, homemade spirits) → formic acid → damages the optic nerve: blindness.
  • Ethylene glycol (antifreeze) → oxalic acid → calcium oxalate crystals in urine and kidney failure.
  • Isopropanol (rubbing alcohol) → acetone: high osmolal gap but usually no acidosis.
  • Ethanol is measured enzymatically (alcohol dehydrogenase) or by gas chromatography.

Other toxins:

  • Lead: anemia, basophilic stippling, nerve and brain damage (worst in children). Test venous whole blood in a trace-metal-free tube by atomic absorption or ICP-MS.
  • Mercury, arsenic, cadmium: heavy metals, measured by atomic absorption or ICP-MS.
  • Organophosphate insecticides block acetylcholinesterase: low cholinesterase, with drooling, sweating and small pupils.
  • Cyanide blocks cytochrome oxidase, so cells can't use oxygen. Bitter almond smell, lactic acidosis.

Drugs of abuse: screen, then confirm

Urine drug screens are immunoassays: fast and cheap, but antibodies can cross-react with similar-looking medicines (for example some cold remedies with amphetamines). So a positive screen is only "presumptive". It must be confirmed by GC-MS or LC-MS/MS, which identifies the exact molecule. For legal (forensic) testing, a documented chain of custody is required at every step.

Check your understanding

A lithium level is unexpectedly high and was drawn into a green-top tube. Likely cause?

Contamination from lithium heparin. Recollect in a plain or sodium heparin tube.

A patient with very low albumin has a "therapeutic" total phenytoin but shows toxic symptoms. Why?

With less albumin, more phenytoin is unbound and active. Measure the free phenytoin level.

High osmolal gap, high anion gap and envelope-shaped crystals in urine. Which poison?

Ethylene glycol (calcium oxalate crystals).

When can an acetaminophen level first be plotted on the nomogram?

At 4 hours after ingestion.

Proteins do most of the work in the blood: they carry hormones and drugs, hold water in the vessels, fight infection and clot blood. When the body breaks proteins down, the nitrogen ends up as waste (urea, creatinine, ammonia, uric acid) that the liver and kidney have to deal with. So the tests in this lesson tell you about nutrition, the liver, the immune system and the kidney all at once.

Total protein and albumin

  • Total protein is measured by the biuret method: copper ions bind to peptide bonds in alkaline solution and turn violet. Every protein has peptide bonds, so this measures all of them.
  • Albumin is measured by dye binding with bromcresol green (BCG) or bromcresol purple (BCP). Albumin grabs the dye and changes its color. BCP is more specific for albumin; BCG can also react a little with globulins.

Albumin is the most abundant plasma protein. It is made only by the liver and it holds water in the bloodstream (oncotic pressure). Low albumin points to liver disease, malnutrition, or loss through the kidney (nephrotic syndrome) or gut. When albumin is low, fluid leaks into tissues: edema.

Globulins = total protein − albumin. The A/G ratio is normally above 1.

Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP): reading the bands

At pH 8.6, proteins separate by charge into five bands, from the anode: albumin, alpha-1, alpha-2, beta, gamma. Each band contains known proteins, so each pattern tells a story:

  • Alpha-1: mainly alpha-1 antitrypsin. A flat alpha-1 band suggests alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (early emphysema and liver disease).
  • Alpha-2: alpha-2 macroglobulin and haptoglobin. Rises in inflammation and, notably, in nephrotic syndrome. Alpha-2 macroglobulin is too big to leak through the kidney, so it stays behind while smaller proteins are lost.
  • Beta: transferrin, complement C3, beta-lipoprotein.
  • Gamma: the immunoglobulins (antibodies).

Classic patterns:

  • Sharp narrow spike in gamma (or beta) → monoclonal gammopathy such as multiple myeloma. One clone of plasma cells makes one identical antibody, so it all runs to one spot. Confirm and type it with immunofixation.
  • Broad gamma increase (polyclonal) → chronic inflammation or infection: many clones, many antibodies.
  • Beta-gamma bridging (no dip between beta and gamma) → cirrhosis, from a polyclonal rise in IgA.
  • Low albumin, high alpha-2, low gamma → nephrotic syndrome.
  • Raised alpha-1 and alpha-2 → acute inflammation (acute-phase proteins).

Urea (BUN) and creatinine: the kidney pair

Urea is made in the liver from the ammonia released when protein is broken down. Creatinine comes from the steady breakdown of creatine in muscle. Both are filtered by the kidney, so both rise when filtration falls.

Creatinine is the better kidney test because muscle produces it at a steady rate and diet barely affects it. BUN also changes with protein intake, gut bleeding (digested blood is protein), dehydration and liver function.

The BUN/creatinine ratio (normally about 10-20) tells you where the problem is:

  • Above 20 → prerenal. Not enough blood reaching the kidney (dehydration, heart failure, bleeding). The kidney itself is fine and reabsorbs more urea along with water, so BUN climbs faster than creatinine. A GI bleed also raises it.
  • Normal or low → intrinsic kidney damage such as acute tubular necrosis, because damaged tubules can't reabsorb urea.
  • Postrenal (blockage after the kidney, like an enlarged prostate) often starts with a high ratio.
Worked exampleBUN 60 mg/dL, creatinine 1.1 mg/dL. Ratio ≈ 55, far above 20, so think prerenal: check for dehydration or a GI bleed.

Measuring creatinine. The Jaffe method reacts creatinine with alkaline picric acid to form a red-orange color. Other substances also react ("non-creatinine chromogens"), notably glucose, ketones (acetoacetate), protein and some cephalosporins, so results can read falsely high. Enzymatic methods avoid this. The kidney section later covers clearance and eGFR.

Ammonia

Ammonia is toxic to the brain. The liver normally turns it into urea. When the liver fails (cirrhosis, hepatitis) or in Reye syndrome (children given aspirin during a viral illness), ammonia builds up and causes confusion and coma (hepatic encephalopathy).

Exam trap: ammonia handlingAmmonia keeps rising in the tube as red cells and proteins break down. Collect in EDTA or heparin, put the tube on ice, and analyze within 15-30 minutes. Avoid hemolysis, and the patient shouldn't smoke beforehand. A delayed, warm sample reads falsely high.

Uric acid

Uric acid is the end product of breaking down purines (from DNA and RNA). High levels form needle-shaped crystals in joints: gout. Levels also rise when many cells die at once (chemotherapy for leukemia: tumor lysis syndrome) and in kidney failure. It is measured with uricase, an enzyme that breaks down uric acid; the drop in absorbance at 293 nm, or a linked color reaction, gives the result.

Check your understanding

Why is creatinine a better marker of kidney function than BUN?

Creatinine is produced at a steady rate from muscle and isn't much affected by diet, hydration or liver function. BUN changes with all of those.

SPEP shows a narrow spike in the gamma region. What is the next test?

Immunofixation electrophoresis, to confirm it is monoclonal and identify the heavy and light chain (for example IgG kappa).

An ammonia sample sat at room temperature for 2 hours. What happens to the result?

It is falsely high, because ammonia is generated in the tube. Recollect on ice and run promptly.

A diabetic in DKA has a creatinine that seems too high for their kidney function. Why might that be?

With the Jaffe method, acetoacetate (and very high glucose) reacts as a non-creatinine chromogen, falsely raising creatinine. An enzymatic method avoids it.

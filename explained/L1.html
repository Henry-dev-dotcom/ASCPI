Every patient result depends on one assumption: the test was working correctly when it ran. Quality control (QC) is how the lab proves it. You run samples with known values (controls) alongside the patients, and if a control misbehaves, you don't trust the patient results. This lesson explains the statistics behind QC in plain terms, then how to read QC charts and the Westgard rules.

The 8 big ideas, explained

  1. Accuracy vs precision. Accuracy = hitting the true value. Precision = getting the same answer again and again.
  2. Shift vs trend. A shift is a sudden jump to a new level; a trend is a gradual drift. Both are systematic errors.
  3. Westgard rules: 1-2s warns; 1-3s and R-4s mean random error; 2-2s, 4-1s and 10x mean systematic error. Failed QC means patient results are held.
  4. Who does what: CLIA is the federal law, CAP and The Joint Commission accredit, OSHA protects workers, CLSI writes standards.
  5. Standard precautions: treat all blood and body fluids as infectious; never recap needles.
  6. Blood spills: fresh 1:10 bleach.
  7. Order of draw: blood cultures, light blue, red/gold, green, lavender, gray.
  8. Most errors are preanalytic: wrong patient, wrong tube, poor collection.

Accuracy and precision: the dartboard

Picture throwing darts at a target:

  • Accurate and precise: all darts tightly grouped in the bullseye. The ideal.
  • Precise but not accurate: tightly grouped, but off to one side. Every result is wrong by the same amount: systematic error (bias).
  • Accurate on average but not precise: spread all around the bullseye: random error.

Precision is measured by the standard deviation (SD) and the coefficient of variation (CV). Accuracy is judged by comparing with the true value (reference materials, proficiency testing, method comparison).

The basic statistics, made simple

  • Mean = the average (add all values, divide by how many).
  • Standard deviation (SD) = how spread out the values are around the mean. Small SD = tight, precise results.
  • Coefficient of variation (CV%) = (SD ÷ mean) × 100. It expresses spread as a percentage, so you can compare precision between tests with different units or levels. Lower CV = better precision.
Worked exampleControl mean 100 mg/dL, SD 5. CV = 5 ÷ 100 × 100 = 5%. Today's control is 112: (112 − 100) ÷ 5 = +2.4 SD, which breaks the 1-2s warning limit.

The normal (Gaussian) distribution: if you run the same control many times, the results form a bell curve. Within ±1 SD fall about 68% of results; within ±2 SD about 95%; within ±3 SD about 99.7%. That is where QC limits come from: a control outside 2 SD should happen only about 1 time in 20 by chance, and outside 3 SD only about 3 times in 1,000.

Reference intervals are built the same way: the middle 95% of healthy people (about mean ± 2 SD). That means about 5% of healthy people will fall outside the "normal" range just by chance; an abnormal result isn't always disease. Labs establish a reference interval with at least 120 healthy people, or verify a manufacturer's interval with at least 20.

Levey-Jennings charts and the Westgard rules

Control results are plotted each day on a Levey-Jennings chart: the mean is drawn as the centre line, with lines at ±1, ±2 and ±3 SD. Run at least two levels of control (for example normal and abnormal) each day. The Westgard rules are a checklist for spotting trouble. The name tells you the rule: "2-2s" means 2 controls beyond 2 s(D).

  • 1-2s: one control outside ±2 SD. A warning only. It prompts you to check the other rules, but doesn't reject the run by itself.
  • 1-3s: one control outside ±3 SD. Reject. Random error (or a big systematic error).
  • R-4s: within one run, one control above +2 SD and another below −2 SD (a range of more than 4 SD). Reject. Random error: the results are scattering.
  • 2-2s: two controls in a row (or both levels in one run) beyond 2 SD on the same side. Reject. Systematic error.
  • 4-1s: four in a row beyond 1 SD on the same side. Reject. Systematic error.
  • 10x: ten in a row on the same side of the mean. Reject. Systematic error (a shift).
Memory hook: random or systematic?Random rules catch scatter: one wild point (1-3s) or points far apart in opposite directions (R-4s). Systematic rules catch everything leaning one way: 2-2s, 4-1s, 10x.

Shifts, trends and their causes

  • Shift: an abrupt jump to a new level that stays (six or more points on one side). Causes: a new reagent lot, a recalibration, a new control lot, maintenance or a part replaced.
  • Trend: values gradually drifting in one direction (six or more points steadily rising or falling). Causes: deteriorating reagent, an aging lamp, a dirty probe, slow temperature drift.
  • Increased scatter: random error. Causes: pipetting problems, bubbles, poor mixing, an unstable power supply.

What to do when QC fails

  1. Stop. Don't report patient results from that run.
  2. Look for the cause: reagents (expired? new lot? stored properly?), calibration, the instrument (maintenance, lamp, temperature), the control material itself.
  3. Fix it: recalibrate, replace the reagent, do maintenance. Then rerun the controls. Simply rerunning the same control until it "passes" is not acceptable.
  4. Review patient results run since the last acceptable QC, and rerun them if needed.
  5. Document everything (corrective action).
Exam trap: changing the meanNever change the control mean or widen the limits to make QC "pass". Means are set for a new control lot by running it alongside the old lot, collecting at least 20 data points.

Other quality tools

  • Method verification (before a new test is used for patients): check accuracy, precision, the reportable range (linearity) and the reference interval.
  • Delta checks: compare a patient's result with their previous one. A big unexplained change (Hgb 14 yesterday, 6 today) flags a possible mix-up or contaminated sample.

More statistics you should recognise

  • Mean, median, mode: average, middle value, and most frequent value. In a symmetric bell curve they are about equal; in a skewed distribution they separate.
  • 95% confidence interval = mean ± 1.96 SD (about 2 SD).
  • Method comparison (old vs new method on the same samples): the correlation coefficient (r) shows how closely they track (1.0 = perfect). In the regression line, the slope shows proportional error (the gap grows with concentration) and the y-intercept shows constant error (a fixed offset).
  • t-test compares two means (accuracy). F-test compares two SDs (precision).

Check your understanding

Two consecutive controls are both +2.2 SD. Which rule, and what type of error?

2-2s; systematic error (such as calibration drift).

Control level 1 is +2.5 SD and level 2 is −2.3 SD in the same run. Which rule?

R-4s (range over 4 SD): random error. Reject the run.

QC values have been slowly rising for 8 days. Pattern and likely cause?

A trend (systematic). Likely a deteriorating reagent, an aging light source or temperature drift.

Mean 250, SD 10. What is the CV?

10 ÷ 250 × 100 = 4%.

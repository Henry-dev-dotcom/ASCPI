This lesson covers the Gram-negative organisms that are not Enterobacterales. That includes the non-fermenters (which don't ferment sugar at all), the fastidious rods that need special conditions to grow, and the Gram-negative cocci (Neisseria and Moraxella). The key skill is matching each organism to the special clue or culture condition that gives it away.

Non-fermenters: Pseudomonas and friends

Non-fermenters get energy by breathing oxygen, not by fermenting sugar, so they give K/K on TSI. Many are tough, environmental organisms that thrive in hospitals and resist antibiotics.

  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa: oxidase positive, grows at 42 °C, smells like grapes (or corn tortillas), and makes pigments: pyocyanin (blue) plus pyoverdin (yellow-green fluorescent), together blue-green. Often metallic, flat colonies with beta-hemolysis. It loves water and moist places, and infects burns, the lungs in cystic fibrosis (mucoid strains), ventilators, the eyes of contact lens wearers and swimmer's ear. It is naturally resistant to many antibiotics.
  • Stenotrophomonas maltophilia: oxidase negative (unusual for a non-fermenter), oxidises maltose (hence the name), and is naturally resistant to carbapenems. Usually treated with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.
  • Acinetobacter baumannii: oxidase negative, non-motile, plump coccobacilli that can look Gram-variable. A notorious multidrug-resistant hospital organism (ICUs, ventilators, wounds).
Memory hook: oxidase-negative non-fermenters"Steno and Acinetobacter Say no" to oxidase. Pseudomonas says yes.

Fastidious Gram-negative rods: each needs something special

  • Haemophilus influenzae: needs X factor (hemin) and V factor (NAD). It grows on chocolate agar, and on blood agar only right next to S. aureus, which lyses red cells and releases V factor: satellite growth. Type b capsule (Hib) caused childhood meningitis before vaccination. H. ducreyi (chancroid) needs only X; H. parainfluenzae needs only V.
  • Legionella pneumophila: stains faintly or not at all with Gram stain, and grows only on BCYE agar (buffered charcoal yeast extract), which supplies L-cysteine and iron. It lives in water systems (cooling towers, showers) and causes Legionnaires' pneumonia. The urine antigen test detects serogroup 1, the most common.
  • Bordetella pertussis: whooping cough. Grows on Regan-Lowe (charcoal) or Bordet-Gengou agar. Collect a nasopharyngeal swab with Dacron or calcium alginate, because cotton contains fatty acids that kill it. PCR is now the main test.
  • Campylobacter jejuni: curved, "gull-wing" rods; oxidase +; hippurate +. Needs 42 °C and microaerophilic conditions (about 5% O2). A leading cause of bacterial diarrhoea (undercooked poultry), linked to Guillain-Barré syndrome.
  • Helicobacter pylori: curved rod in the stomach. Very strong urease activity: it splits urea into ammonia to neutralise stomach acid around itself. That is the basis of the urea breath test (patient swallows labeled urea and labeled CO2 is breathed out). Stool antigen and biopsy tests also exist. Causes ulcers and stomach cancer.
  • Vibrio cholerae: curved rod, oxidase +, grows in alkaline conditions and salt-tolerant. Gives yellow colonies on TCBS (ferments sucrose). Cholera toxin causes massive watery "rice-water" stools. V. parahaemolyticus (seafood) is green on TCBS; V. vulnificus causes wound infections from seawater.
  • Brucella and Francisella tularensis: tiny coccobacilli from animals (unpasteurised dairy, rabbits). They are among the most common causes of lab-acquired infection, so suspected isolates must be handled in a biological safety cabinet and referred.

The Gram-negative cocci

All are oxidase positive kidney-bean-shaped diplococci. Identify them by which sugars they use (they oxidise rather than ferment them, but the tests are traditionally called "fermentation"):

  • N. gonorrhoeae: uses glucose only. Seen as diplococci inside neutrophils on a urethral smear. Grows on Thayer-Martin or Martin-Lewis (selective chocolate agar) in CO2. Very fragile: plate quickly, keep warm. NAAT is the usual test now.
  • N. meningitidis: uses glucose and maltose. Has a capsule; causes meningitis and sepsis with a purple rash, spreading in dorms and barracks. Handle cultures in a safety cabinet, since lab infections are dangerous.
  • Moraxella catarrhalis: uses no sugars; DNase +, butyrate esterase +; colonies slide across the agar like a "hockey puck". Causes ear infections and bronchitis.
Memory hookMeningitidis uses Maltose (and glucose). Gonorrhoeae uses only Glucose.

Check your understanding

A burn-wound isolate is oxidase positive, smells like grapes and grows at 42 °C. Identify.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

A pneumonia patient's sputum Gram stain shows few organisms, but a urine antigen test is positive. Which organism, and what agar does it need?

Legionella pneumophila (serogroup 1); BCYE agar with L-cysteine and iron.

Why must a pertussis swab not be cotton?

Cotton contains fatty acids that are toxic to Bordetella pertussis. Use Dacron, rayon or calcium alginate.

An oxidase-positive diplococcus from CSF uses glucose and maltose. Identify.

Neisseria meningitidis.

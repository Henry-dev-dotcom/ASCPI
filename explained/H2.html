Anemia means too little hemoglobin to carry the oxygen the body needs. It is not a diagnosis on its own; it is a sign that something is wrong with making red cells, keeping them alive, or keeping them in the body. The exam expects you to work out which anemia from a few clues: cell size, iron studies, the retic count and what the cells look like on the smear.

Step 1: sort the anemia by cell size

The MCV is the fastest way to narrow things down, because each size group has a different underlying problem.

Microcytic (MCV under 80): a hemoglobin-making problem. A developing red cell keeps dividing until it has enough hemoglobin inside. If hemoglobin is made slowly, the cell squeezes in an extra division and ends up small and pale. Hemoglobin needs three things: iron, heme (built from iron plus protoporphyrin) and globin chains. Break any one and you get small cells:

  • No iron available: iron deficiency and anemia of chronic disease (iron is present but locked away).
  • Heme can't be built: sideroblastic anemia and lead poisoning.
  • Globin chains are missing: thalassemia.

Macrocytic (MCV over 100): usually a DNA problem. B12 and folate are needed to make DNA. Without them the nucleus divides slowly while the cytoplasm keeps growing, so the cells come out large (megaloblastic). Liver disease and alcohol give big cells for a different reason: extra cholesterol and lipid in the membrane makes the cell larger, but DNA is normal (so no hypersegmented neutrophils).

Normocytic (MCV 80-100): normal-sized cells, just not enough of them. Here the retic count decides the next step. High retics mean the marrow is working and cells are being lost (bleeding) or destroyed (hemolysis). Low retics mean the marrow isn't producing (aplastic anemia, kidney disease with low EPO, early chronic disease).

Memory hook for microcytic causes: "TAILS"Thalassemia, Anemia of chronic disease, Iron deficiency, Lead poisoning, Sideroblastic anemia.

Step 2: read the iron studies

Four tests describe the body's iron. It helps to picture iron as money:

  • Ferritin = savings in the bank (stored iron). It is the single best test for iron deficiency.
  • Serum iron = cash in your pocket (iron moving in the blood).
  • TIBC (total iron-binding capacity) = the number of empty trucks (transferrin) sent out to collect iron. The body makes more trucks when it is short of iron.
  • Transferrin saturation = how full those trucks are (serum iron ÷ TIBC).

Now each pattern makes sense:

  • Iron deficiency: savings gone (low ferritin), little cash (low iron), and the liver sends out extra trucks (high TIBC). Saturation is very low.
  • Anemia of chronic disease: inflammation raises hepcidin, a hormone that locks iron inside macrophages. The savings are there (ferritin normal or high; ferritin also rises because it is an acute-phase protein), but cash is short (low iron), and the body makes fewer trucks (low TIBC).
  • Thalassemia: iron is fine (normal or high). The problem is the globin chain, not the iron.
  • Sideroblastic anemia: iron gets into the cell's mitochondria but can't be built into heme, so it piles up (high iron, high ferritin). In the marrow the trapped iron forms a ring around the nucleus: the ringed sideroblast.
Exam trap: TIBCIron deficiency and chronic disease both have low serum iron. The tie-breaker is TIBC: high in iron deficiency, low in chronic disease. Ferritin backs it up: low in iron deficiency, normal or high in chronic disease.

B12, folate and pernicious anemia

Both deficiencies cause the same blood picture: macrocytic anemia, oval macrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils (5 or more lobes) and a very high LD (the marrow destroys many faulty cells before they leave, called ineffective erythropoiesis). Often all three cell lines are low.

To tell them apart, use two metabolites: homocysteine rises in both, but methylmalonic acid (MMA) rises only in B12 deficiency. B12 deficiency can also damage nerves (numbness, poor balance), which folate deficiency does not.

Pernicious anemia is the classic cause of B12 deficiency. The body makes antibodies against intrinsic factor (or the parietal cells that make it). Intrinsic factor is the carrier B12 needs to be absorbed in the ileum, so no intrinsic factor means no B12 absorbed, however much you eat.

Aplastic anemia

Here the factory itself shuts down. The marrow is empty (replaced by fat), so all three lines fall: pancytopenia with a low retic count. Causes include drugs, chemicals (benzene), radiation, viruses and autoimmune attack on stem cells. The cells that are made look normal, which helps separate it from leukemia or megaloblastic anemia.

Reading red cell shapes

Each abnormal shape tells a story about what happened to the cell. Instead of memorising a list, link each shape to its cause:

  • Spherocyte (small, round, no pale centre): the cell has lost membrane, so it rounds up. Caused by antibodies (autoimmune hemolysis), an inherited membrane defect (hereditary spherocytosis) or burns.
  • Target cell: too much membrane for the amount of hemoglobin, so the extra membrane bunches in the middle. Seen in thalassemia, HbC, liver disease and after splenectomy.
  • Sickle cell: HbS polymerises into rigid rods when it loses oxygen and bends the cell.
  • Teardrop: cells squeezed out of a scarred, crowded marrow (myelofibrosis).
  • Schistocyte / helmet cell: cells sliced by fibrin strands or a mechanical valve (DIC, TTP, HUS).
  • Bite and blister cells: the spleen plucked out Heinz bodies (G6PD deficiency).
  • Acanthocyte (few, uneven spikes): abnormal membrane lipids in liver disease or abetalipoproteinemia. Echinocyte / burr cell (many even spikes): uremia, or an artifact of drying.
  • Stomatocyte (slit-shaped pale area): alcoholism, hereditary stomatocytosis, Rh-null cells.
  • Rouleaux (stacked coins): too much plasma protein makes cells stick face to face (myeloma, inflammation). Agglutination (random clumps) means antibody, usually cold agglutinins.
  • Polychromasia (bluish-grey cells): young reticulocytes, a sign the marrow is responding.
Exam trap: rouleaux vs agglutinationRouleaux look like neat stacks of coins and disperse when the plasma is replaced with saline. Agglutination forms irregular grape-like clumps and does not disperse with saline.

Red cell inclusions: leftovers inside the cell

Inclusions are pieces of something left behind. Ask: what is it made of, and why is it still there?

  • Howell-Jolly body: a small round piece of DNA. Normally the spleen removes it, so seeing it suggests no spleen (or megaloblastic anemia).
  • Basophilic stippling: clumped ribosomes (RNA), seen as fine blue dots. Coarse stippling points to lead poisoning (lead blocks the enzyme that breaks down RNA) or thalassemia.
  • Pappenheimer bodies: iron granules. They confirm with Prussian blue (iron stain). Seen in sideroblastic anemia and after splenectomy.
  • Heinz bodies: clumps of denatured hemoglobin. Invisible on Wright stain; you need a supravital stain (crystal violet). Seen in G6PD deficiency and unstable hemoglobins.
  • Cabot rings: loops of leftover mitotic spindle in severe anemia.
  • HbH inclusions: beta-chain tetramers in alpha thalassemia, giving a "golf ball" look with brilliant cresyl blue.
Memory hook: which inclusions need a special stain?"Heinz and HbH need a supravital stain; Pappenheimer needs Prussian blue." Everything else shows on a routine Wright stain.

Check your understanding

Low iron, low TIBC, high ferritin. Which anemia?

Anemia of chronic disease. Hepcidin traps iron in macrophages; ferritin is high as stored iron and as an acute-phase protein; the body makes less transferrin, so TIBC is low.

Macrocytic anemia with high homocysteine and normal MMA. B12 or folate?

Folate deficiency. MMA rises only when B12 is lacking.

Why do Howell-Jolly bodies suggest a missing spleen?

The spleen normally "pits" these DNA fragments out of red cells. Without a spleen they stay in the circulating cells.

A smear shows coarse basophilic stippling and the patient paints old houses. What is the likely cause?

Lead poisoning. Lead inhibits pyrimidine 5'-nucleotidase (so RNA clumps) and blocks heme synthesis, giving a microcytic anemia.

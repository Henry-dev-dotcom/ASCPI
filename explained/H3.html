Hemolysis means red cells are being destroyed early. A normal red cell lives about 120 days; in hemolytic anemia it may last only days or weeks. This lesson covers how to prove hemolysis is happening, how to work out the cause, and how the inherited hemoglobin disorders (sickle cell and thalassemia) fit in.

Two places cells can be destroyed

Where a red cell breaks down changes the lab picture, so learn this first:

  • Extravascular hemolysis (the common type) happens inside macrophages, mainly in the spleen and liver. The macrophage eats the cell and recycles it neatly. Hemoglobin is broken into bilirubin, so bilirubin rises, but little free hemoglobin reaches the plasma. Spherocytes, warm autoimmune anemia and hereditary spherocytosis destroy cells this way, and the spleen often enlarges.
  • Intravascular hemolysis happens inside the blood vessels. Cells burst open, spilling hemoglobin straight into the plasma. You see pink plasma, hemoglobin in the urine and later hemosiderin in the urine (iron left in kidney cells). Causes include ABO transfusion reactions, PNH, mechanical valves and severe DIC.

Proving hemolysis: the indicator tests

Each test reflects one part of the clean-up process:

  • Haptoglobin goes down. Haptoglobin is a "sponge" protein that grabs free hemoglobin and carries it to the liver. In hemolysis the sponge is used up faster than it is made, so the level falls. This is one of the most sensitive signs.
  • LD goes up. Red cells are full of lactate dehydrogenase (mostly LD1), which leaks out when they break.
  • Indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin goes up. Broken-down heme becomes unconjugated bilirubin faster than the liver can process it.
  • Reticulocytes go up. The healthy marrow speeds up to replace the lost cells.
  • Free plasma hemoglobin and urine hemosiderin rise mainly in intravascular hemolysis.
Putting it togetherHgb 8.5, retics 9%, LD high, haptoglobin undetectable, indirect bilirubin 3.2 mg/dL. Every marker points the same way: the marrow is working hard (high retics) to replace cells that are being destroyed (low haptoglobin, high LD and bilirubin). That is hemolysis. The next step is to find out why.

Finding the cause: let the smear and DAT guide you

  • Spherocytes + positive DAT → autoimmune hemolytic anemia. The DAT (direct antiglobulin test) detects antibody or complement stuck on the patient's red cells. In warm AIHA the antibody is IgG and works at body temperature. In cold agglutinin disease it is IgM, usually anti-I, often after Mycoplasma pneumonia; it coats cells with complement, so the DAT is positive for C3d.
  • Spherocytes + negative DAT + high MCHC → hereditary spherocytosis. An inherited defect in membrane proteins (spectrin, ankyrin) makes the membrane fragile. The spleen trims it bit by bit until the cell becomes a sphere. Spheres have less surface for their volume, so they burst easily in dilute salt solutions: increased osmotic fragility. The modern confirmation is the EMA binding test by flow cytometry.
  • Schistocytes + low platelets → microangiopathic hemolysis (DIC, TTP, HUS). Red cells are cut by fibrin strands or platelet clumps in small vessels.
  • Bite cells + Heinz bodies → G6PD deficiency. G6PD protects hemoglobin from oxidation. Without it, oxidant drugs (primaquine, sulfa drugs, dapsone), fava beans or infection oxidise hemoglobin into Heinz bodies, and the spleen bites them out. Note: test G6PD after the crisis, because the oldest, most deficient cells have already been destroyed, which can give a falsely normal result.
  • Loss of CD55 and CD59 → PNH (paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria). These proteins protect cells from complement. Without them, complement punches holes in red cells (intravascular hemolysis). Flow cytometry for CD55/CD59 (or FLAER) is the test of choice.

Hemoglobin: what goes wrong

Normal adult hemoglobin (HbA) is made of two alpha chains and two beta chains (α2β2). There are also small amounts of HbA2 (α2δ2, about 2-3%) and HbF (fetal, α2γ2, under 1-2% after infancy). Disorders come in two flavours:

  • Structural (wrong chain): the chain is made in normal amounts but has the wrong amino acid. Example: sickle cell (HbS), HbC.
  • Quantitative (too little chain): the chain is normal but not enough is made. These are the thalassemias.

Sickle cell disease and HbC

In HbS, a single swap at position 6 of the beta chain (valine replaces glutamic acid) makes the hemoglobin sticky when it loses oxygen. The molecules line up into long rigid rods that bend the cell into a sickle. Sickled cells block small vessels (painful crises) and are destroyed early (hemolysis).

  • Sickle trait (AS): about 40% HbS and 60% HbA. Usually no symptoms, because the normal HbA dilutes the sticky HbS.
  • Sickle cell disease (SS): mostly HbS, some HbF, no HbA.
  • HbC has lysine at the same position 6. It causes target cells and crystals, and HbSC disease is milder than SS.

The sickle solubility test is a screen: a reducing agent removes oxygen, and HbS becomes insoluble, making the tube cloudy. Two limits are tested often. It can be falsely negative in babies under 6 months (they still have mostly HbF) or with very low hemoglobin, and it can't tell trait from disease. Always confirm with electrophoresis or HPLC.

Hemoglobin electrophoresis made simple

At alkaline pH 8.6, hemoglobins carry a negative charge and move toward the positive pole (anode). Each amino acid swap changes the charge a little, so different hemoglobins stop in different places. Glutamic acid is negative; replacing it with neutral valine (S) or positive lysine (C) makes the molecule less negative, so it moves less far.

From farthest to closest to the start: A → F → S → C. Remember "A Fast Silly Cat". HbC shares its slow spot with E, A2 and O; HbS shares its spot with D and G.

To separate the look-alikes, use acid citrate agar at pH 6.0-6.2, which separates by a different property: C moves away from E/O/A2, and S moves away from D/G.

Thalassemias

In thalassemia one type of globin chain is under-produced. The leftover partner chains have nothing to pair with, so they clump and damage the cell. Result: small, pale cells (microcytic, hypochromic), often target cells and stippling.

Beta thalassemia (too few beta chains):

  • Trait (minor): mild anemia, very small cells, and a raised HbA2 above 3.5%, because the body makes extra delta chains to make up. This raised A2 is the key lab clue.
  • Major (Cooley anemia): little or no HbA, lots of HbF; severe anemia that needs regular transfusion.

Alpha thalassemia (too few alpha chains). Everyone has four alpha genes, and severity tracks how many are deleted:

  • 1 deleted: silent carrier. 2 deleted: alpha thal trait, mild microcytosis.
  • 3 deleted: HbH disease. Spare beta chains form β4 tetramers (HbH).
  • 4 deleted: Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis. Spare gamma chains form γ4 (Hb Bart's), which can't release oxygen; usually fatal before or at birth.
Memory hook: Mentzer indexMCV ÷ RBC count. Below 13 → thalassemia; above 13 → iron deficiency. Why: in thalassemia trait the marrow makes lots of tiny cells (high RBC count), while in iron deficiency it makes fewer cells.

HPFH and the Kleihauer-Betke test

HPFH (hereditary persistence of fetal hemoglobin) is a harmless condition where HbF stays high into adult life. Red cell indices are normal, which is how you tell it apart from beta thalassemia.

The Kleihauer-Betke test uses the fact that HbF resists acid. A blood smear is put in acid: adult HbA is washed out (leaving pale "ghost" cells), while fetal cells keep their hemoglobin and stain pink. Counting the pink cells in the mother's blood estimates how much fetal blood leaked into her circulation, which sets the dose of Rh immune globulin.

Check your understanding

Why is haptoglobin low in hemolysis?

It binds free hemoglobin, and the complex is removed by the liver. When hemoglobin is released faster than haptoglobin is made, the level drops.

A 3-month-old with suspected sickle cell disease has a negative solubility test. Is the disease ruled out?

No. Infants under 6 months have mostly HbF, which can give a false negative. Use electrophoresis or HPLC.

MCV 62, RBC 6.1, normal ferritin, HbA2 5%. Diagnosis?

Beta thalassemia trait. Mentzer index 62 ÷ 6.1 ≈ 10 (under 13), iron is normal and HbA2 is above 3.5%.

Why can G6PD activity look normal right after a hemolytic crisis?

The oldest cells, which have the least enzyme, were destroyed first. The remaining young cells and reticulocytes have more G6PD. Retest a few months later.

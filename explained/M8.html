Identifying a pathogen is only half the job. The doctor also needs to know which antibiotics will work. This lesson explains how susceptibility testing works, how bacteria resist drugs, which resistance is "built in", and the modern machines that now do much of the identification. It ends with the dangerous agents a routine lab must recognise and hand on.

Disk diffusion (Kirby-Bauer)

A standard amount of bacteria is spread over a plate and paper disks soaked in antibiotics are placed on top. Each drug spreads out into the agar, forming a gradient that is strongest near the disk. Where the drug is strong enough, the bacteria can't grow, leaving a clear zone of inhibition. The zone diameter is compared with CLSI breakpoints to report S (susceptible), I (intermediate) or R (resistant).

Because the zone size depends on many things, every condition is standardised:

  • Mueller-Hinton agar (low in substances that block sulfonamides and trimethoprim), poured to 4 mm depth, pH 7.2-7.4.
  • Inoculum matched to a 0.5 McFarland standard (about 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL).
  • Incubate at 35 °C in room air for 16-18 hours.
Why each variable mattersThink about how each one changes the zone:
• Too heavy an inoculum → more bacteria to overcome the drug → smaller zones → false resistance.
• Too light → larger zones → false susceptibility.
• Agar too thick → drug spreads downward instead of outward → smaller zones. Too thin → larger zones.
• Old, weak disks → smaller zones.

MIC: the more precise answer

The MIC (minimum inhibitory concentration) is the lowest drug concentration that stops visible growth. In broth dilution, the organism is grown in a row of wells with doubling drug concentrations; the first clear well is the MIC. An Etest strip carries a gradient of drug on a plate; the MIC is read where the ellipse of inhibition meets the strip. The MBC (minimum bactericidal concentration) is the lowest concentration that kills 99.9%.

QC strains with known results check every run: E. coli ATCC 25922, S. aureus ATCC 25923 (disk) or 29213 (MIC), P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853, and E. faecalis ATCC 29212.

How bacteria resist, and how the lab detects it

  • MRSA: the mecA gene makes PBP2a, a cell-wall-building enzyme that beta-lactams can't bind. Detected with a cefoxitin disk (the best surrogate; it strongly triggers mecA), oxacillin MIC, PBP2a latex or PCR. MRSA is reported resistant to all beta-lactams except ceftaroline.
  • VRE: vanA or vanB genes change the target of vancomycin (the end of the cell-wall building block) so the drug can't stick.
  • ESBL (extended-spectrum beta-lactamase): enzymes that destroy penicillins, third-generation cephalosporins and aztreonam. They are blocked by clavulanate, so a bigger zone when clavulanate is added confirms an ESBL. Carbapenems still work.
  • Carbapenemases (KPC, NDM, OXA-48): destroy even carbapenems, the "last-resort" drugs. Detected by the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) or molecular tests. These are serious infection-control alerts.
  • Inducible clindamycin resistance: some staph and strep carry a dormant gene (erm) that erythromycin switches on, which then makes them resistant to clindamycin too. The D-test places the two disks near each other; if the clindamycin zone is flattened into a "D" on the erythromycin side, report clindamycin resistant.
  • Beta-lactamase: the nitrocefin (cefinase) disk turns from yellow to red when the enzyme breaks it. Useful for Haemophilus, Neisseria, Moraxella and anaerobes.

Built-in (intrinsic) resistance

Some organisms are naturally resistant to certain drugs, so the lab doesn't report those drugs as options. These are favourite exam questions:

  • Klebsiella → ampicillin (it naturally makes a beta-lactamase).
  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa → many drugs, including ampicillin, first-generation cephalosporins and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (pumps and a tight outer membrane).
  • Enterococcus → cephalosporins and low-level aminoglycosides (its PBPs don't bind cephalosporins well).
  • Stenotrophomonas → carbapenems.
  • Anaerobes → aminoglycosides (uptake needs oxygen).
  • Gram-negative rods → vancomycin (too big to pass the outer membrane).
Exam trap: Enterococcus and cephalosporinsEven if a cephalosporin appears to work in the lab, it never works clinically against Enterococcus. Never report it as susceptible.

Modern identification methods

  • MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry: a bit of colony is mixed with a matrix and blasted with a laser; its proteins (mostly ribosomal) fly through a tube, and the pattern of their masses is matched to a database. It identifies most bacteria and yeasts in minutes.
  • Multiplex PCR panels: test for many organisms and resistance genes (like mecA, vanA, KPC) at once, straight from a positive blood culture, stool, CSF or respiratory swab.
  • 16S rRNA gene sequencing: reads a gene every bacterium has, to name organisms that routine methods can't.
  • Automated ID/AST systems (Vitek, Phoenix, MicroScan): miniature biochemical and antibiotic panels read by machine.
  • Continuous-monitoring blood culture systems (BACTEC, BacT/Alert): sensors detect the CO2 that growing organisms produce, flagging positive bottles automatically.

Select agents and the sentinel lab

Some organisms could be used as bioweapons and are dangerous to lab staff. A routine hospital lab acts as a sentinel lab: its job is only to recognise and rule out, not to fully identify. If an isolate might be one of these, the lab works in a biosafety cabinet, never sniffs plates, stops further testing, notifies public health, and refers the isolate to the Laboratory Response Network (LRN).

  • Bacillus anthracis: large Gram-positive rod, non-hemolytic, non-motile, "medusa head" colonies.
  • Yersinia pestis: Gram-negative rod with bipolar "safety pin" staining, slow growth.
  • Francisella tularensis: tiny Gram-negative coccobacillus that needs cysteine (grows on chocolate), extremely infectious.
  • Brucella: tiny coccobacillus, slow growth, urease positive; from unpasteurised dairy.
  • Burkholderia pseudomallei and mallei: oxidase positive; automated systems can misidentify them.
  • Clostridium botulinum: its neurotoxin is the danger.
Memory hookTiny Gram-negative coccobacilli that grow slowly and came from animals → think Brucella or Francisella → put it in the hood and call it in.

Check your understanding

A Kirby-Bauer plate was inoculated with a suspension much heavier than 0.5 McFarland. Effect?

Smaller zones, so resistance may be falsely reported. Repeat with a correct inoculum.

A staph isolate is erythromycin resistant and clindamycin susceptible, and the clindamycin zone is flattened next to the erythromycin disk. How do you report clindamycin?

Resistant. The positive D-test shows inducible clindamycin resistance.

Which disk best detects mecA-mediated resistance in S. aureus?

Cefoxitin.

Why is vancomycin never tested on E. coli?

Gram-negative rods are intrinsically resistant: the large vancomycin molecule can't cross their outer membrane.

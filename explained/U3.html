Urine findings make sense only when you know how a healthy kidney works. This lesson starts with how the nephron filters and fine-tunes urine, then shows how the main kidney diseases produce their urine patterns, then covers kidney function testing and a few special urine tests.

How the nephron works

Each kidney has about a million nephrons. Think of each as a filter followed by a long processing tube:

  • Glomerulus (the filter): blood pressure pushes plasma through a fine sieve at about 125 mL/min (the GFR). Water, salts, glucose and small molecules pass; cells and large proteins (like albumin) stay in the blood. A damaged filter lets protein and red cells through.
  • Proximal tubule (the big reclaimer): takes back about two-thirds of the filtrate, including nearly all glucose and amino acids, plus sodium, bicarbonate and water. It has a limited number of glucose carriers. Once blood glucose rises above about 160-180 mg/dL (the renal threshold), the carriers are full and glucose spills into urine.
  • Loop of Henle (the concentrator): builds a salty gradient in the kidney's inner layer (the countercurrent mechanism) so that water can later be pulled out of the urine.
  • Distal tubule and collecting duct (fine control): hormones adjust the final urine. ADH (antidiuretic hormone) opens water channels so water is reabsorbed and urine gets concentrated. Aldosterone saves sodium and gets rid of potassium.

Diabetes insipidus is a failure of ADH (none is made, or the kidney ignores it). The patient makes large volumes of very dilute urine with a low specific gravity, even when dehydrated. Don't confuse it with diabetes mellitus, where urine contains glucose.

Kidney disease patterns: match the clue to the site

Nephrotic syndrome: a leaky filter for protein. The glomerulus lets large amounts of protein through, but not many red cells.

  • Heavy proteinuria, above 3.5 g/day.
  • Low blood albumin, so fluid leaks into tissues: edema.
  • The liver overworks making proteins, and lipoproteins with them: high cholesterol, and fat appears in urine as oval fat bodies and fatty casts (Maltese crosses).
  • Common causes: minimal change disease (children), diabetic kidney disease, membranous nephropathy.

Nephritic syndrome (glomerulonephritis): an inflamed, bleeding filter.

  • Blood in urine (often cola-colored), dysmorphic red cells and red cell casts.
  • Only mild proteinuria; high blood pressure, low urine output, rising creatinine.
  • Causes: post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (after strep throat), IgA nephropathy, lupus, Goodpasture.
Memory hookNeph-r-o-tic = protein (heavy) and fat. Neph-r-i-tic = bleeding and inflammation (red cell casts, high blood pressure).

Infections:

  • Cystitis (bladder): white cells and bacteria, positive nitrite and leukocyte esterase, no casts.
  • Pyelonephritis (kidney): the same plus white cell casts, fever and flank pain.

Acute tubular necrosis (ATN): tubular cells die from low blood flow (shock, surgery) or toxins (aminoglycosides, contrast dye, myoglobin). Urine shows RTE cells and "muddy brown" granular casts. Damaged tubules can't reclaim sodium or concentrate urine, so urine sodium is high and urine osmolality is low. That helps separate ATN from prerenal problems (dehydration), where healthy tubules hold on to sodium (urine Na under 20) and concentrate the urine (osmolality over 500).

Chronic kidney disease: waxy and broad casts, and a specific gravity fixed at about 1.010 (isosthenuria): the kidney can no longer concentrate or dilute.

Orthostatic proteinuria: protein in urine collected while upright (daytime) but not in the first morning sample. Common in young people and usually harmless. Compare a first-morning specimen with a daytime one to diagnose it.

Measuring kidney function: creatinine clearance

"Clearance" is the volume of plasma the kidneys clean of a substance per minute. Creatinine is filtered and barely reabsorbed, so its clearance approximates the GFR.

CrCl (mL/min) = (U × V) ÷ P, then corrected to a standard body size: × (1.73 ÷ patient's body surface area).

  • U = urine creatinine, P = plasma creatinine (same units), V = urine volume per minute (24-hour volume ÷ 1440).
  • Normal is about 90-120 mL/min.
Worked exampleUrine creatinine 100 mg/dL, plasma creatinine 1.0 mg/dL, 24-hour volume 1440 mL (V = 1 mL/min), BSA 1.73 m². CrCl = 100 × 1 ÷ 1.0 = 100 mL/min. BSA is already 1.73, so no correction is needed.

Most labs now report an eGFR calculated from serum creatinine, age and sex (and sometimes cystatin C, which doesn't depend on muscle mass). The biggest error in a 24-hour collection is an incomplete collection.

Inherited metabolic disorders seen in urine

  • Phenylketonuria (PKU): can't break down phenylalanine; mousy odor; ferric chloride turns green. Detected by newborn screening.
  • Alkaptonuria: homogentisic acid builds up; urine turns black on standing (as it becomes alkaline and oxidises).
  • Maple syrup urine disease: branched-chain amino acids build up; sweet maple-syrup smell.
  • Cystinuria: a transport defect causes cystine stones and hexagonal crystals.
  • Porphyrias: Ehrlich-reacting porphobilinogen (Watson-Schwartz, Hoesch tests).

Urine pregnancy and drug testing

Pregnancy tests detect hCG. Use first-morning urine (most concentrated) early in pregnancy. Very high hCG (molar pregnancy, later pregnancy) can cause a hook effect false negative; dilute and retest.

Drug testing: an immunoassay screen, then GC-MS or LC-MS/MS confirmation of positives. To detect tampering (adulteration or dilution), check that the sample's temperature is 90-100 °F within 4 minutes of collection, and check specific gravity, creatinine and pH (very dilute or odd values suggest tampering). Forensic tests require an unbroken chain of custody.

Check your understanding

Protein 5 g/day, oval fat bodies, low albumin and edema in a child. Diagnosis?

Nephrotic syndrome (minimal change disease is the most common cause in children).

Hematuria, dysmorphic red cells, red cell casts and hypertension two weeks after strep throat. Diagnosis?

Acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (a nephritic syndrome).

Why does glucose appear in urine when blood glucose is above about 180 mg/dL?

The proximal tubule's glucose carriers are saturated (the renal threshold is exceeded), so the extra glucose isn't reabsorbed.

A patient produces large volumes of urine with SG 1.002, even after water restriction. What is suggested?

Diabetes insipidus (lack of ADH or kidney resistance to it).

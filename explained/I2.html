This lesson covers three ways the immune system's machinery shows up in disease. Complement is a protein cascade that punches holes in invaders. Hypersensitivity is the immune system overreacting and harming the body. Immunodeficiency is the immune system missing a part. Each disease follows logically from which part is affected.

Complement: a cascade like falling dominoes

Complement is a set of over 30 plasma proteins that switch each other on in sequence. Three different "start buttons" all lead to the central step, the splitting of C3:

  • Classical pathway: started by antibody bound to antigen (IgM, or IgG1/IgG3). C1q grabs the antibody's Fc region, then C4 and C2 are cleaved to form the C3 convertase C4b2a. This is how antibodies kill.
  • Alternative pathway: started directly by microbial surfaces (such as bacterial endotoxin), without antibody. Uses factor B, factor D and properdin; convertase C3bBb. It provides fast protection before antibodies exist.
  • Lectin pathway: mannose-binding lectin (MBL) recognises sugars on microbes and then uses C4 and C2 like the classical pathway.

All three end the same way: C5 is split, and C5b, C6, C7, C8 and C9 assemble into the membrane attack complex (MAC), a pore that lets water rush into the cell and burst it.

The leftover pieces have jobs too:

  • C3b: the main opsonin, a tag that helps phagocytes grab the microbe.
  • C3a, C4a, C5a: anaphylatoxins that make mast cells release histamine (swelling, leaky vessels).
  • C5a: a strong chemotaxin that calls neutrophils to the site.

Reading complement levels

When a pathway is switched on, its components are used up, so their levels fall. Which components are low tells you which pathway is active:

  • Low C3 and low C4 → the classical pathway is being used up by immune complexes: SLE, serum sickness, some glomerulonephritis.
  • Low C3, normal C4 → the alternative pathway (C4 isn't part of it): for example post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.
  • Low C4 with C1 inhibitor deficiency → hereditary angioedema. C1 inhibitor normally keeps C1 in check. Without it, C1 constantly cleaves C4 (so C4 is low even between attacks), and bradykinin builds up, causing sudden swelling of the face, throat or gut. C3 is normal.
  • Missing C5-C9 (the MAC) → repeated Neisseria infections, because these bacteria are mainly killed by the MAC.
  • Missing C3 → severe repeated infections with encapsulated bacteria (no opsonisation).

CH50 tests the whole classical pathway in one go: it measures how well the patient's serum lyses antibody-coated sheep red cells. A very low CH50 means a component is missing.

Exam trap: complement is fragileComplement breaks down at room temperature and is destroyed by heating serum to 56 °C for 30 minutes, which is how labs "inactivate" serum on purpose. Samples for complement testing must be fresh or frozen promptly.

Hypersensitivity: four ways to overreact (ACID)

Type I, Anaphylactic (immediate): minutes after exposure. IgE sits on mast cells; allergen cross-links it, and the mast cell releases histamine and other mediators. Examples: hay fever, asthma, food allergy, bee-sting anaphylaxis. Tests: total IgE and allergen-specific IgE (ImmunoCAP), skin-prick tests.

Type II, Cytotoxic: IgG or IgM against antigens on a cell or tissue, often with complement. The body attacks its own cells or transfused cells. Examples: transfusion reactions, HDFN, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, Goodpasture (anti-GBM), and antibodies that block or stimulate receptors such as myasthenia gravis and Graves disease.

Type III, Immune complex: antibody and free (soluble) antigen form complexes that circulate and get stuck in small vessels (kidney, joints, skin), where they activate complement and cause inflammation. Examples: SLE, serum sickness, the Arthus reaction, post-strep glomerulonephritis. Complement C3 and C4 are often low.

Type IV, Delayed: T cells, not antibody, so it takes 48-72 hours to develop. Examples: the TB skin test, contact dermatitis (poison ivy, nickel), and graft rejection.

Memory hook: type II vs type IIIType II: antibody hits a fixed target (a cell). Type III: antibody and antigen meet in the blood and the complexes land somewhere else.

Immunodeficiency: what's missing tells you the infections

  • DiGeorge syndrome: the thymus (and parathyroids) don't develop, so there are few T cells and low calcium (tetany). Heart defects are common. Infections: viruses and fungi.
  • Bruton (X-linked) agammaglobulinemia: boys with no mature B cells and very low immunoglobulins. Infections start at about 6 months, when the mother's IgG runs out: repeated bacterial infections.
  • SCID (severe combined immunodeficiency): both T and B cells missing. Severe infections of every kind in infancy ("bubble boy"). Patients must receive irradiated blood.
  • Selective IgA deficiency: the most common immunodeficiency; often no symptoms or mild sinus and gut infections. Danger: some make anti-IgA and can have anaphylaxis from normal blood products. It can also give false-negative IgA-based tests (like the IgA anti-tTG test for celiac disease).
  • Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD): neutrophils can eat bacteria but can't kill them, because the respiratory burst enzyme (NADPH oxidase) is missing. The NBT test is negative (no color change) and the DHR test shows no burst. Infections with catalase-positive organisms (Staph, Aspergillus, Serratia).
  • Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome: the triad eczema, low (small) platelets, and infections.
  • HIV: acquired; destroys CD4 T cells.

HIV testing today

  1. Screen with a 4th-generation antigen/antibody combination immunoassay. It detects HIV antibodies and the p24 antigen, which appears earlier, shortening the window period.
  2. If reactive, run an HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation immunoassay.
  3. If that is negative or unclear, test for HIV-1 RNA (NAT). A positive NAT with a negative differentiation assay means acute (early) HIV infection: antigen and RNA appear before antibodies.

The Western blot is no longer used as the first confirmation step. Viral load (RT-PCR) and CD4 count monitor treatment. A CD4 count below 200/µL defines AIDS.

Check your understanding

Low C3 and normal C4 after a strep throat infection. Which pathway is active?

The alternative pathway (C4 is used only by the classical and lectin pathways).

A patient has repeated Neisseria meningitidis infections. Which complement deficiency should be checked?

Terminal components C5-C9 (the membrane attack complex).

A positive TB skin test read at 48-72 hours is which type of hypersensitivity?

Type IV (delayed, T-cell mediated).

A boy's infections begin at 6 months, and he has no B cells. Diagnosis and why the timing?

Bruton X-linked agammaglobulinemia. Maternal IgG protected him until it was used up at about 6 months.

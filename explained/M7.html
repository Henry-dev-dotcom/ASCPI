This lesson collects the organisms that don't fit the bacterial culture workflow: parasites (found mostly by looking through a microscope), viruses (found mostly by molecular tests), and a few unusual bacteria that can't be grown on ordinary plates. For parasites, the trick is to tie each one to the shape or structure that identifies it.

How parasites are found

  • Stool ova and parasites (O&P): the stool is concentrated (formalin-ethyl acetate sedimentation, which collects eggs and cysts at the bottom), then looked at wet and with a permanent stain. Trichrome is the permanent stain for intestinal protozoa.
  • Pinworm (Enterobius): the female lays eggs around the anus at night, so press clear tape there first thing in the morning (the Scotch tape test), before bathing.
  • Blood parasites: Giemsa-stained thick and thin smears.

Intestinal and genital protozoa

  • Entamoeba histolytica: the disease-causing amoeba (bloody diarrhoea, liver abscess). The proof is a trophozoite with ingested red cells. Cysts have up to 4 nuclei and cigar-shaped chromatoidal bars. (Harmless E. coli the amoeba has cysts with up to 8 nuclei.)
  • Giardia: pear-shaped trophozoite with two nuclei that look like eyes (a "smiling face") and a "falling leaf" motility. Causes greasy, foul diarrhoea, often from untreated stream water.
  • Cryptosporidium and Cyclospora: tiny oocysts that are acid-fast with the modified stain. Watery diarrhoea, severe in AIDS patients. Crypto survives chlorine (swimming pool outbreaks).
  • Trichomonas vaginalis: pear-shaped flagellate with jerky motility in a fresh wet mount; sexually transmitted vaginitis.

Malaria: thick and thin smears

The thick smear piles up many layers of lysed blood, so it is sensitive for finding parasites. The thin smear keeps red cells intact, so you can see what the parasite looks like and what it does to the cell, which identifies the species and lets you count the percentage parasitemia.

  • P. falciparum: the deadliest. Look for many small rings, often several in one red cell, rings at the cell edge, banana-shaped gametocytes, and red cells of normal size. It infects red cells of any age, so parasitemia can be very high.
  • P. vivax: infects young red cells, which become enlarged with pink Schüffner dots; trophozoites look ragged and ameboid.
  • P. ovale: also enlarged with Schüffner dots, but cells are oval with fringed edges.
  • P. malariae: infects old red cells (normal or small). Band-shaped trophozoites across the cell, "rosette" schizonts, and a 72-hour fever cycle.
  • P. knowlesi: monkey malaria in Southeast Asia; looks like malariae but has a 24-hour cycle and can be severe.
Exam trap: Babesia vs falciparumBoth show small rings in red cells. Babesia (tick-borne, northeastern US) can form a "Maltese cross" (four parasites in a tetrad) and has no pigment and no gametocytes.

Other blood and tissue parasites: Trypanosoma (long wavy flagellates: sleeping sickness, Chagas disease), Leishmania (small amastigotes inside macrophages), and microfilariae (worm larvae in blood).

Worm eggs: each has a signature

  • Ascaris: round egg with a thick, bumpy (mammillated) coat.
  • Trichuris (whipworm): barrel or football shape with a plug at each end.
  • Hookworm: oval with a thin, clear shell; adult worms suck blood and cause iron deficiency anemia.
  • Enterobius (pinworm): flattened on one side ("D" shape).
  • Diphyllobothrium latum (fish tapeworm): egg with a lid (operculum); the worm absorbs vitamin B12, causing megaloblastic anemia.
  • Schistosoma (blood fluke) eggs have a spine whose position names the species: lateral (side) spine = S. mansoni (gut, liver); terminal spine = S. haematobium (bladder; found in urine); a tiny side knob = S. japonicum.
Memory hook: SchistosomaHaematobium has its spine at the Head end (terminal) and lives near the bladder, so look in urine. Mansoni's spine points Midway (lateral).

Unusual bacteria

  • Chlamydia trachomatis: an obligate intracellular bacterium; it can only grow inside human cells, so it won't grow on agar. The common STI. Diagnosed by NAAT on urine or swabs. Giemsa can show inclusions in cells.
  • Mycoplasma pneumoniae: has no cell wall, so it doesn't Gram stain and beta-lactam drugs (which attack cell walls) don't work. "Walking pneumonia"; associated with cold agglutinins (anti-I).
  • Treponema pallidum (syphilis): too thin to see on a Gram stain and can't be cultured; seen as corkscrew organisms by darkfield microscopy of a chancre, but usually diagnosed by serology.

Viruses to know

Viruses are now mostly detected by PCR and antigen tests. A few old-fashioned clues are still tested because they show what the virus does to cells:

  • Herpes simplex: multinucleated giant cells on a Tzanck smear (cells fuse together). PCR is the best test (especially on CSF for encephalitis).
  • Varicella-zoster: chickenpox, and later shingles when it reactivates in a nerve.
  • CMV: enlarged cells with large "owl's eye" nuclear inclusions. Dangerous in transplant patients and newborns (congenital infection). That is why CMV-safe (leukoreduced or CMV-negative) blood is used for at-risk patients.
  • EBV: infectious mononucleosis; also linked to Burkitt lymphoma and nasopharyngeal cancer.
  • Adenovirus: pink eye (conjunctivitis), colds and diarrhoea in children.
  • RSV: bronchiolitis in infants; in cell culture it makes cells fuse into syncytia.
  • Influenza: seasonal; rapid antigen tests (less sensitive) and PCR.
  • Rabies: Negri bodies in brain neurons.
  • Parvovirus B19: "fifth disease" (slapped-cheek rash). It attacks red cell precursors, so in people whose red cells already die early (sickle cell, hereditary spherocytosis) it causes an aplastic crisis. It can also cause hydrops in a fetus.
  • Norovirus (outbreaks on cruise ships, in nursing homes) and rotavirus (infants): viral gastroenteritis.

Check your understanding

A thin smear shows several small rings in single red cells of normal size, plus a crescent-shaped gametocyte. Species?

Plasmodium falciparum.

An egg with a terminal spine is found in urine. Which parasite?

Schistosoma haematobium.

Why doesn't Chlamydia grow on routine culture plates?

It is an obligate intracellular organism and needs living host cells. NAAT is the test of choice.

A child with sickle cell disease suddenly has a very low hemoglobin and almost no reticulocytes. Which virus?

Parvovirus B19, causing an aplastic crisis.

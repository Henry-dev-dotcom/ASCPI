Body fluids other than blood and urine (CSF, the fluids around the lungs, heart, gut and joints, plus amniotic fluid, semen and stool) are tested to answer one main question: what is causing this fluid to be abnormal: infection, inflammation, bleeding, cancer, or just fluid leaking under pressure? Each fluid has a few classic tests that answer it.

CSF: collection and the three tubes

CSF is collected by lumbar puncture into numbered tubes, each sent to a different department:

  • Tube 1: chemistry and serology. It may contain a little blood or skin cells from the needle, which matters least for chemistry.
  • Tube 2: microbiology.
  • Tube 3: cell count. It has the least contamination from blood picked up during the puncture, so counts are most accurate.

CSF is precious (it can't easily be recollected) and urgent: process it immediately, and keep microbiology samples at room temperature.

Normal CSF: clear and colorless; 0-5 white cells/µL in adults (mostly lymphocytes and monocytes; newborns have more); glucose about 60-70% of plasma glucose (so always compare with a blood glucose); protein 15-45 mg/dL.

Traumatic tap or real bleeding?

Sometimes the needle nicks a blood vessel. That blood should be distinguished from a real subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH):

  • Traumatic tap: blood is heaviest in tube 1 and clears by tube 3; the supernatant (after spinning) is clear; the fluid may clot (fresh blood has fibrinogen).
  • Subarachnoid hemorrhage: equal blood in all tubes; the supernatant is yellow (xanthochromia), because red cells that have been there for a few hours break down into oxyhemoglobin and then bilirubin. Old bleeding doesn't clot.

Meningitis patterns: think about what each germ does

  • Bacterial: bacteria eat glucose and attract neutrophils; inflammation leaks protein. So: cloudy fluid, very high neutrophils, high protein, low glucose. A medical emergency.
  • Viral: viruses don't consume glucose. So: clear fluid, lymphocytes, normal or slightly high protein, normal glucose. Usually milder.
  • Fungal (Cryptococcus) or tuberculous: slow, chronic. Lymphocytes and monocytes, high protein, low glucose. A fine "pellicle" or web may form in TB CSF.
Memory hookLow glucose = something is eating it: bacteria, fungi or TB. Normal glucose with lymphocytes = viral.

Other CSF tests: India ink and cryptococcal antigen (Cryptococcus), Gram stain and culture, VDRL (the only test accepted on CSF for neurosyphilis), and oligoclonal bands and the IgG index (immunoglobulin made inside the brain, supporting multiple sclerosis).

Counting cells with a hemocytometer

Fluid cell counts are often done manually in a Neubauer counting chamber, which holds a known volume: each large square is 1 mm × 1 mm, and the chamber is 0.1 mm deep. The formula converts what you count into cells per µL:

Cells/µL = (cells counted × dilution) ÷ (area counted in mm² × 0.1 mm depth)

Worked exampleUndiluted CSF, 45 cells counted in all 9 large squares. Volume counted = 9 mm² × 0.1 mm = 0.9 µL. Count = 45 ÷ 0.9 = 50 cells/µL.

For a red cell count, dilute with saline. For a white cell count, use 3% acetic acid (with methylene blue), which lyses the red cells so they don't get in the way, and stains the white cell nuclei. Count both sides of the chamber; they should agree closely. The differential is done on a cytospin slide, which concentrates the few cells onto a small spot.

Serous fluids: transudate or exudate?

Pleural, pericardial and peritoneal fluids build up in two ways:

  • Transudate: fluid pushed out by pressure or pulled out by low protein, with healthy membranes. Low protein, few cells. Causes: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome.
  • Exudate: fluid leaking from inflamed or damaged membranes. High protein and LD, many cells. Causes: infection, cancer, inflammation.

Light's criteria: the fluid is an exudate if any one is true:

  • fluid protein ÷ serum protein above 0.5,
  • fluid LD ÷ serum LD above 0.6,
  • fluid LD above two-thirds of the upper limit of normal for serum LD.

More clues: many lymphocytes suggest TB or cancer; a pleural pH below 7.2 suggests an empyema (pus) that needs draining; milky fluid with triglycerides above 110 mg/dL is chylous (leaking lymph duct), while milky fluid rich in cholesterol is pseudochylous (long-standing effusion).

Peritoneal (ascitic) fluid uses the SAAG (serum-ascites albumin gradient) = serum albumin − fluid albumin. SAAG ≥ 1.1 g/dL means portal hypertension (cirrhosis, heart failure); lower values suggest other causes (cancer, TB, pancreatitis). More than 250 neutrophils/µL in ascites means spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.

Worked exampleSerum albumin 3.5, ascitic albumin 1.5. SAAG = 2.0 g/dL (≥ 1.1) → portal hypertension, such as cirrhosis.

Synovial (joint) fluid

Normal joint fluid is clear, pale yellow and very viscous (hyaluronic acid makes it stringy), with under 200 white cells/µL. Disease groups:

  • Non-inflammatory (osteoarthritis): slightly raised cells, good viscosity.
  • Inflammatory (rheumatoid arthritis, gout, pseudogout): cloudy, poor viscosity, raised neutrophils.
  • Septic (bacterial infection): very high white cells (often over 50,000), mostly neutrophils, low glucose, positive Gram stain and culture. An emergency.
  • Hemorrhagic (trauma, hemophilia).

Very viscous fluid can be treated with hyaluronidase before counting. Use saline (not acetic acid) as a diluent for synovial white counts, because acetic acid makes the hyaluronic acid clot into a mucin clump.

Crystals are identified with a polarising microscope plus a red compensator:

  • Monosodium urate (gout): needle-shaped, strongly negatively birefringent, yellow when parallel to the compensator's slow axis.
  • Calcium pyrophosphate (pseudogout): rhomboid or rod, weakly positively birefringent, blue when parallel.
Memory hookGout: "Yellow, Needle, Negative" (YNN). Pseudogout: "Blue, Positive, rhomboid" ("BP").

Amniotic fluid, semen and stool

  • Amniotic fluid: L/S ratio ≥ 2.0 (lecithin/sphingomyelin) and the presence of phosphatidylglycerol show fetal lung maturity (enough surfactant to breathe). High AFP suggests a neural tube defect. Bilirubin measurement (for HDFN) needs the sample protected from light.
  • Semen: collected after 2-7 days of abstinence and examined within 1 hour; it should liquefy in 30-60 minutes. WHO lower reference limits (2021): volume about 1.4 mL, concentration 16 million/mL, total motility 42%, normal forms (strict criteria) 4%. (The older 2010 limits were 1.5 mL, 15 million/mL and 40%.) Fructose comes from the seminal vesicles, so it is absent if the ducts are blocked. Eosin-nigrosin stain checks if sperm are alive. After a vasectomy, success means no sperm.
  • Stool: the fecal immunochemical test (FIT) detects human globin, so it is more specific than the older guaiac test, which reacts with red meat and is affected by vitamin C. Sudan III screens for fat (steatorrhea, malabsorption); the quantitative 72-hour fecal fat is under 7 g/day normally. Fecal elastase tests pancreatic function.

Check your understanding

CSF: cloudy, 2,000 neutrophils/µL, protein 250 mg/dL, glucose 15 mg/dL. Cause?

Bacterial meningitis.

Blood in CSF tubes 1 to 3 decreases steadily and the supernatant is clear. Traumatic tap or SAH?

Traumatic tap.

Pleural fluid/serum protein ratio 0.6. Transudate or exudate?

Exudate (ratio above 0.5 meets Light's criteria).

Needle-shaped crystals that are yellow when parallel to the compensator. Which disease?

Gout (monosodium urate, strong negative birefringence).

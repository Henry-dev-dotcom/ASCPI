The platelet plug is soft. Coagulation (secondary hemostasis) reinforces it with a mesh of fibrin, like pouring concrete over a sandbag wall. It works as a cascade: each clotting factor switches on the next, until thrombin turns fibrinogen into fibrin. The lab tests in this lesson each check one part of that cascade, so if you understand the cascade, the test patterns follow logically.

The cascade as three roads to one destination

The traditional lab model has three parts:

  • Intrinsic pathway (everything already in the blood): factors XII, XI, IX, VIII. Tested by the aPTT.
  • Extrinsic pathway (triggered by tissue factor from damaged tissue): factor VII. Tested by the PT.
  • Common pathway (where both roads join): factors X, V, II (prothrombin) and I (fibrinogen). Tested by both PT and aPTT.

At the end, thrombin turns fibrinogen into fibrin strands, and factor XIII cross-links them so the clot is strong.

Memory hooksPT → "Play Tennis Outside" (extrinsic, factor VII). aPTT → "Play Table Tennis Inside" (intrinsic). Intrinsic factors: "12, 11, 9, 8": the price that was $12 is now $11.98. Common pathway: "1 × 2 × 5 = 10" (factors I, II, V, X).
How the tests actually workBlood is collected into citrate, which binds calcium so the sample can't clot in the tube. In the lab, the plasma gets an activator plus calcium back, and the machine times how long a clot takes. For the PT, the activator is tissue factor (thromboplastin), which starts the extrinsic road. For the aPTT, it is a contact activator (silica, kaolin) plus phospholipid, which starts the intrinsic road. A missing factor anywhere along a road slows that test.

Monitoring blood thinners

Warfarin blocks vitamin K, which the liver needs to finish making factors II, VII, IX and X (and the natural anticoagulants proteins C and S). Factor VII has the shortest half-life (about 6 hours), so it falls first, and the PT is the test that tracks warfarin. Because labs use different reagents, the PT is converted to the INR: (patient PT ÷ mean normal PT)ISI. The ISI rates how sensitive the reagent is.

Worked examplePT 18 s, mean normal PT 12 s, ISI 1.2. INR = (18 ÷ 12)1.2 = 1.51.2 ≈ 1.6.

Unfractionated heparin boosts antithrombin, which mainly blocks thrombin and factor Xa, and it is tracked with the aPTT (or anti-Xa). Low molecular weight heparin acts mostly on Xa and barely changes the aPTT, so it needs an anti-Xa assay.

Reading PT and aPTT patterns

Ask which road is broken:

  • PT long, aPTT normal → only the extrinsic road (factor VII): factor VII deficiency, early warfarin, early liver disease or vitamin K deficiency.
  • aPTT long, PT normal → the intrinsic road: hemophilia A (VIII), hemophilia B (IX), factor XI or XII deficiency, heparin, vWD (low VIII), or lupus anticoagulant.
  • Both long → the common road or many factors at once: deficiency of X, V, II or fibrinogen, DIC, severe liver disease, a big warfarin or heparin overdose.
  • Both normal but the patient bleeds → something the tests don't see: factor XIII deficiency (the clot forms but isn't cross-linked; screen with the clot solubility test in 5 M urea), a platelet function problem, or mild vWD.
Exam trap: factor XIIFactor XII deficiency gives a very long aPTT but no bleeding. The body doesn't actually need factor XII to clot; it only matters in the test tube.

The mixing study: missing factor or inhibitor?

When a clotting time is long, mix the patient's plasma 1:1 with normal plasma and repeat:

  • It corrects → a factor deficiency. Normal plasma supplies at least 50% of every factor, which is enough to give a normal time.
  • It doesn't correct → an inhibitor is present and is also blocking the normal plasma. This could be lupus anticoagulant or an antibody against a factor (usually factor VIII). Factor VIII inhibitors are time-dependent, so they may correct at first and then prolong after incubating 1-2 hours at 37 °C.

Lupus anticoagulant is an antibody against phospholipid. In the test tube it steals the phospholipid the test needs, so the aPTT is long. In the body it causes clots (and miscarriages), not bleeding. Adding extra phospholipid overcomes it; confirm with the dRVVT (dilute Russell viper venom time).

Inhibitor strength is measured with the Bethesda assay, reported in Bethesda units.

Thrombin time and reptilase time

The thrombin time adds thrombin directly and times the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin. It only checks the last step, so it is long when fibrinogen is low or abnormal, or when heparin is present (heparin blocks thrombin). Reptilase is a snake enzyme that also clots fibrinogen, but heparin can't block it. So a long thrombin time with a normal reptilase time means heparin; both long means a fibrinogen problem.

DIC and fibrin breakdown

Once a clot has done its job, plasmin breaks it down. The pieces are measured two ways:

  • FDPs (fibrin/fibrinogen degradation products) come from breaking down either fibrin or fibrinogen.
  • D-dimer comes only from cross-linked fibrin, so it proves a real clot was made and then broken down.

In DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation), something such as sepsis, trauma, an obstetric emergency or APL switches on clotting all over the body. Tiny clots use up platelets and factors, then get broken down. The pattern is long PT and aPTT, low fibrinogen, low platelets, high D-dimer and FDPs, schistocytes. The patient can bleed and clot at the same time.

In the rare primary fibrinolysis, plasmin attacks fibrinogen directly without a clot forming. FDPs are high, but D-dimer is normal (no cross-linked fibrin) and platelets are normal.

Specimen rules that change results

  • Use a 3.2% sodium citrate (light blue) tube, filled to the line for the 9:1 blood-to-citrate ratio. Underfilling means too much citrate, which binds the test calcium and falsely prolongs the times.
  • If the hematocrit is above 55%, there is less plasma in the tube, so the citrate is too concentrated. Reduce the citrate volume using the lab's formula.
  • Timing: PT is stable up to 24 hours at room temperature; aPTT within 4 hours, and within 1 hour (centrifuged) for heparin patients, because platelets release PF4, which neutralises heparin.

More tests you should recognise

  • Fibrinogen (Clauss method): low in DIC and liver disease.
  • Factor assays: patient plasma is mixed with plasma lacking one factor. How well it corrects gives that factor's activity as a percentage.
  • Thromboelastography (TEG): whole-blood test of how fast a clot forms, how strong it is and how fast it breaks down. Used in surgery and trauma.
  • Heparin neutralisation: protamine, polybrene or heparinase removes heparin so other tests can be read.

Thrombophilia: too much clotting

The body has brakes on clotting: antithrombin, protein C and protein S. Clots form too easily when a brake fails or a factor resists the brake:

  • Factor V Leiden (the most common inherited cause): factor V resists being switched off by activated protein C ("APC resistance").
  • Prothrombin G20210A: too much prothrombin.
  • Protein C, protein S or antithrombin deficiency. Antithrombin deficiency causes heparin resistance, because heparin works through antithrombin.
  • Antiphospholipid antibodies and high homocysteine.
Exam trap: testing on warfarinProteins C and S are vitamin K-dependent, so they fall on warfarin. Testing them during warfarin therapy can falsely suggest a deficiency.

Check your understanding

PT normal, aPTT long, corrects on mixing, boy with joint bleeds. Which factors are likely?

Factor VIII (hemophilia A) or factor IX (hemophilia B). Both are intrinsic, X-linked, and cause joint bleeding.

Thrombin time long, reptilase normal. Why?

Heparin. It blocks thrombin but not reptilase.

Why is D-dimer more specific than FDPs for a real clot?

D-dimer comes only from cross-linked fibrin, which exists only after factor XIII has stabilised a real clot. FDPs also come from fibrinogen breakdown.

A patient on LMWH has a normal aPTT. Is the drug not working?

Not necessarily. LMWH mainly inhibits Xa and barely affects the aPTT. Use an anti-Xa assay to monitor it.

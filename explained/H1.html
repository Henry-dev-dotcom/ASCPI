Hematology is the study of blood cells: how they are made, what they look like, and what their numbers tell you. Almost every hematology question comes back to three skills: knowing how a normal cell grows up, reading the red cell indices, and knowing what "normal" looks like so you can spot what isn't. This lesson builds those three skills.

The big picture: one factory, three product lines

All blood cells are made in the bone marrow from one parent cell, the hematopoietic stem cell (CD34 positive). Think of the marrow as a factory with three product lines:

  • Red cells carry oxygen. Their job depends on hemoglobin, so most red cell disease is about making too little hemoglobin, making the wrong kind, or losing cells too fast.
  • White cells fight infection. When they go wrong you get either a reaction to something (infection, allergy) or a cancer of the factory (leukemia).
  • Platelets plug holes in vessels. Too few means bleeding; too many can mean a marrow disease.

Each line is controlled by a signal: erythropoietin (EPO) from the kidney drives red cells, thrombopoietin from the liver drives platelets, and colony-stimulating factors drive white cells. When the exam says "low EPO", it is hinting that the kidney is not asking for red cells, or that the marrow is making them on its own (polycythemia vera).

How a cell grows up (maturation)

Every line follows the same three rules as a cell matures. Learn the rules and you can stage almost any cell you see:

  1. The cell gets smaller. Blasts are big; mature cells are small.
  2. The nucleus tightens. A young nucleus is fine and lacy with visible nucleoli. An old nucleus is dark and clumped.
  3. The cytoplasm changes colour. Young cytoplasm is deep blue because it is full of RNA (the cell is busy building proteins). As the cell finishes its work, the blue fades and it turns pink or lilac.

Red cell line: pronormoblast → basophilic normoblast → polychromatic normoblast → orthochromic normoblast → reticulocyte → mature RBC. The names describe the colour. "Basophilic" means blue (lots of RNA). "Polychromatic" means mixed blue-grey, because hemoglobin (pink) is now being made alongside the RNA (blue). "Orthochromic" means the cytoplasm is nearly the right pink colour. The orthochromic normoblast pushes its nucleus out and becomes a reticulocyte, which still has a little RNA left. That leftover RNA is what new methylene blue stains in a retic count.

Neutrophil line: myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte → metamyelocyte → band → segmented neutrophil. Two landmarks are worth remembering. The promyelocyte is where the big primary (azurophilic) granules appear. The myelocyte is the first stage with secondary (specific) granules, and the last stage that can still divide. After that, the nucleus just indents (metamyelocyte, kidney bean), stretches into a band, and finally pinches into segments.

Platelet line: megakaryoblast → promegakaryocyte → megakaryocyte → platelets. Megakaryocytes don't divide their cells; they copy their DNA over and over (endomitosis), become huge, and then shed thousands of platelets from their cytoplasm.

Why "megaloblastic" cells look strangeIn B12 or folate deficiency the cell cannot make DNA fast enough, but it can still make RNA and hemoglobin normally. So the cytoplasm matures on schedule while the nucleus lags behind. You get a big cell with a young-looking, open nucleus sitting in grown-up cytoplasm. This mismatch is called nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony, and it is the signature of megaloblastic anemia.

The red cell indices, explained

The analyzer measures a few things directly (RBC count, hemoglobin, and cell size) and calculates the rest. The indices describe the average red cell:

  • MCV (mean cell volume) = how big the average red cell is. Formula: Hct × 10 ÷ RBC. Normal 80-100 fL. Under 80 is microcytic (small); over 100 is macrocytic (big).
  • MCH (mean cell hemoglobin) = how much hemoglobin is in the average cell. Formula: Hgb × 10 ÷ RBC. Normal 27-33 pg.
  • MCHC (mean cell hemoglobin concentration) = how tightly packed the hemoglobin is. Formula: Hgb × 100 ÷ Hct. Normal 32-36 g/dL. Low MCHC means hypochromic (pale cells).
Worked exampleHct 30%, RBC 4.0 × 10¹²/L, Hgb 9.0 g/dL.
MCV = 30 × 10 ÷ 4.0 = 75 fL → microcytic.
MCH = 9.0 × 10 ÷ 4.0 = 22.5 pg → low.
MCHC = 9.0 × 100 ÷ 30 = 30 g/dL → hypochromic.
Small, pale cells: think iron deficiency first, then thalassemia.

Why the MCHC is so useful as a check. Red cells can't really be "over-full" of hemoglobin. An MCHC above about 36-37 g/dL is almost always one of three things: spherocytes (cells lost membrane and became dense balls), an artifact that falsely raised the hemoglobin (lipemia, very high WBC), or an artifact that falsely lowered the hematocrit (cold agglutinins clumping the cells). So a high MCHC should make you suspicious, not just record it.

Memory hook: the rule of threeHgb × 3 ≈ Hct (within about 3). Hgb 10 → Hct about 30. If the numbers don't fit, something is interfering with the sample. Look for lipemia, cold agglutinins or unusual cells before you release the result.

Reticulocytes: what the marrow is doing right now

The reticulocyte count is the marrow's report card. A red cell lives about 120 days, but a reticulocyte is only a day or so old, so the retic count tells you how many red cells the marrow is releasing today.

  • High retics = the marrow is working hard to replace lost cells. Think bleeding or hemolysis.
  • Low retics in an anemic patient = the marrow can't keep up. Think iron, B12 or folate deficiency, aplastic anemia, or marrow being crowded out (leukemia, fibrosis).

Why we "correct" the retic count. The retic count is reported as a percentage of red cells. If a patient is anemic, there are fewer red cells overall, so the same number of retics looks like a bigger percentage. The correction shrinks it back: corrected retic = retic % × (patient Hct ÷ 45).

Worked exampleRetic 6%, Hct 30%. Corrected = 6 × (30 ÷ 45) = 4%. Still raised, so the marrow is genuinely responding.

Correcting the WBC for nucleated red cells

Many analyzers and the counting chamber can't tell a nucleated red cell (nRBC) from a white cell, because both have a nucleus. If the smear shows nRBCs, the WBC count is falsely high. The fix: corrected WBC = WBC × 100 ÷ (100 + nRBCs per 100 WBCs). With WBC 10.0 and 25 nRBCs per 100 WBC, corrected WBC = 10.0 × 100 ÷ 125 = 8.0.

Normal values: your baseline

You can't spot abnormal without knowing normal. These are approximate adult ranges (each lab sets its own):

  • Hemoglobin: men 13.5-17.5, women 12.0-16.0 g/dL. Men run higher because testosterone stimulates EPO.
  • Hematocrit: men 41-53%, women 36-46%.
  • WBC: 4.5-11.0 × 10⁹/L.
  • Platelets: 150-450 × 10⁹/L.
  • Differential: neutrophils 50-70%, lymphocytes 20-40%, monocytes 2-9%, eosinophils 1-4%, basophils 0-1%. A handy order from most to least common is "Never Let Monkeys Eat Bananas".

Newborns are the big exception: they have much higher hemoglobin and MCV, and children have relatively more lymphocytes. If a question gives you a baby's results, don't judge them by adult ranges.

The 8 facts, and why each one is true

  1. Low MCV = iron deficiency, thalassemia, chronic disease, sideroblastic anemia, lead. All of these are problems making hemoglobin (no iron, faulty globin chains, or blocked heme). With less hemoglobin to fill them, the cells divide an extra time and end up small. High MCV = B12 or folate deficiency, liver disease, alcohol, because DNA is slow (megaloblastic) or the membrane carries extra lipid.
  2. The retic count tells you if the marrow is responding, as explained above.
  3. Spherocytes plus a positive DAT = autoimmune hemolysis; negative DAT = hereditary spherocytosis. In both, the spleen nibbles membrane off the cells and they round up. The DAT tells you why: antibody on the cell (immune) or an inherited membrane protein defect (spectrin, ankyrin).
  4. Schistocytes = DIC, TTP, HUS or a mechanical heart valve. Fragments mean red cells are being sliced, either by fibrin strands in small vessels or by a metal valve.
  5. Auer rods = AML; t(15;17) = APL; t(9;22) = CML. Auer rods are fused myeloid granules, so they prove the blasts are myeloid.
  6. PT tests the extrinsic pathway (factor VII) and warfarin; aPTT tests the intrinsic pathway and heparin. Warfarin hits factor VII first because it has the shortest half-life.
  7. DIC: everything is used up. Clotting is switched on all over the body, so platelets and fibrinogen are consumed (low), clotting times lengthen (PT and aPTT long), and the clots are broken down (D-dimer high).
  8. Light blue citrate tubes must be filled to the line (9:1). Citrate works by binding calcium. Too little blood means too much citrate, which soaks up the calcium added during testing and falsely prolongs the clotting times.

Check your understanding

Hgb 12 g/dL and Hct 45%. Is anything wrong?

By the rule of three, Hgb 12 should give a Hct of about 36. A Hct of 45 is far off, so suspect an error or interference (and the MCHC would be a low 26.7). Recheck the sample before reporting.

Which neutrophil stage is the last one able to divide?

The myelocyte. It is also the first stage with secondary (specific) granules.

Why does a megaloblastic cell have a "young" nucleus in "old" cytoplasm?

B12 and folate are needed to make DNA. Without them the nucleus matures slowly, while RNA and hemoglobin production (the cytoplasm) carry on normally.

An MCHC of 39 g/dL is reported. Name three possible causes.

Spherocytes, a falsely high hemoglobin (lipemia, extreme leukocytosis) or a falsely low hematocrit (cold agglutinins). Warm the sample or replace the plasma with saline to sort out the artifacts.

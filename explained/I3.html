Autoimmune disease happens when the immune system attacks the body's own tissues. Each disease tends to make antibodies against a particular target, so those autoantibodies work as fingerprints. This lesson explains how to read them, how the ANA screen works, and how to sort out the plasma cell disorders that produce a monoclonal protein.

Screening vs specific antibodies

Autoimmune testing usually goes in two steps:

  1. A sensitive screen that rarely misses disease but isn't specific. The classic example is the ANA: positive in almost all SLE, but also in other autoimmune diseases, with some drugs, and in about 5% of healthy people (more in older adults).
  2. A specific test that confirms the diagnosis, such as anti-dsDNA or anti-Smith for SLE.

So a negative ANA makes SLE very unlikely, but a positive ANA alone doesn't prove it.

How the ANA test works and what the patterns mean

The ANA is done by indirect immunofluorescence on HEp-2 cells (human tumour cells with large nuclei). The patient's serum is put on the cells; any antibody against nuclear parts sticks, and a fluorescent anti-human antibody makes it glow. The result has a titer (positive at 1:40 or 1:80 or above, depending on the lab) and a pattern that hints at the target:

  • Homogeneous (the whole nucleus glows evenly): antibodies to dsDNA or histones (SLE, drug-induced lupus).
  • Rim / peripheral (the edge of the nucleus glows): anti-dsDNA, very specific for SLE.
  • Speckled (fine or coarse dots): antibodies to Sm, RNP, SSA, SSB (SLE, mixed connective tissue disease, Sjögren).
  • Nucleolar (the nucleoli glow): scleroderma.
  • Centromere (a fixed number of discrete dots): CREST (limited scleroderma).

A positive ANA is then "reflexed" to specific antibody tests (ELISA or multiplex). Anti-dsDNA can be confirmed with the Crithidia luciliae test, which uses an organism with a pure DNA structure (the kinetoplast).

The autoantibody fingerprints

Group them by organ or disease to remember them:

Connective tissue diseases

  • SLE: ANA screen; anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith specific; low C3 and C4 during flares (immune complexes use up complement). Anti-dsDNA levels track kidney involvement.
  • Drug-induced lupus (hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid): anti-histone; usually no kidney disease, and it improves when the drug stops.
  • Sjögren syndrome (dry eyes and mouth): anti-SSA (Ro) and anti-SSB (La). Anti-SSA in a pregnant woman can cause heart block in the newborn.
  • Diffuse scleroderma: anti-Scl-70 (topoisomerase I). CREST (Calcinosis, Raynaud's, Esophageal problems, Sclerodactyly, Telangiectasia): anti-centromere.
  • Mixed connective tissue disease: high anti-U1 RNP.
  • Polymyositis: anti-Jo-1.

Rheumatoid arthritis

  • Rheumatoid factor (RF) is usually an IgM antibody against the Fc part of IgG. It is not specific: also positive in other autoimmune diseases, chronic infections (hepatitis C, endocarditis) and healthy older adults.
  • Anti-CCP (cyclic citrullinated peptide) is much more specific for RA and can appear years before symptoms.

Gut and liver

  • Celiac disease: anti-tissue transglutaminase (tTG) IgA and anti-endomysial IgA. Always check total IgA: IgA deficiency is more common in celiac disease and gives false negatives (use IgG-based tests instead).
  • Primary biliary cholangitis: anti-mitochondrial antibody (AMA).
  • Autoimmune hepatitis: anti-smooth muscle and ANA (type 1); anti-LKM (type 2).
  • Pernicious anemia: anti-intrinsic factor (more specific) and anti-parietal cell.

Nerve, muscle, kidney and vessels

  • Myasthenia gravis: anti-acetylcholine receptor antibodies block the signal from nerve to muscle, causing weakness that worsens with use.
  • Goodpasture syndrome: anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM), attacking both kidney and lung.
  • Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (formerly Wegener): c-ANCA (cytoplasmic pattern), target PR3.
  • Microscopic polyangiitis: p-ANCA (perinuclear pattern), target MPO.

Endocrine

  • Graves disease: TSH receptor antibodies (TSI) that stimulate the thyroid.
  • Hashimoto thyroiditis: anti-TPO (thyroid peroxidase) and anti-thyroglobulin, which damage the thyroid.
  • Type 1 diabetes: anti-GAD, anti-islet cell, anti-insulin.

Monoclonal gammopathies

Normally, thousands of plasma cell clones make thousands of different antibodies, which spread across the gamma region as a broad band. If one clone grows out of control, it makes huge amounts of one identical antibody, the M-protein (monoclonal protein), which shows up as a sharp spike.

  • Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) finds the spike.
  • Immunofixation (IFE) identifies it: which heavy chain (G, A, M) and which light chain (kappa or lambda). A monoclonal protein has only one light chain type.
  • Serum free light chains (kappa/lambda ratio) and urine tests detect free light chains (Bence Jones protein). The urine dipstick misses it, because the dipstick reacts mainly with albumin.

The conditions:

  • MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance): small M-protein (under 3 g/dL), under 10% plasma cells in the marrow, and no organ damage. Not cancer, but about 1% per year progress, so it is monitored.
  • Multiple myeloma: more than 10% plasma cells plus organ damage, "CRAB": high Calcium, Renal failure, Anemia, Bone lesions. Rouleaux on the smear; high ESR.
  • Waldenström macroglobulinemia: IgM M-protein from lymphoplasmacytic cells. Big IgM pentamers thicken the blood: hyperviscosity (headache, blurred vision, bleeding).
  • Cryoglobulinemia: proteins that precipitate in the cold and redissolve when warmed, blocking small vessels in the fingers and skin. Often linked to hepatitis C. Collect and keep the sample at 37 °C until the serum is separated, or the cryoglobulin will precipitate with the clot and be missed.
  • Amyloidosis (AL): abnormal light chains fold into fibres that deposit in the heart, kidney and nerves. Congo red stain shows apple-green birefringence under polarised light.

Check your understanding

ANA positive, rim pattern, low C3 and C4, proteinuria. Which disease and which confirmatory antibody?

SLE; anti-dsDNA (and/or anti-Smith).

A patient on hydralazine develops joint pain and a positive ANA. Which antibody?

Anti-histone (drug-induced lupus).

A celiac screen (tTG IgA) is negative but symptoms strongly suggest celiac disease. What should be checked?

Total IgA. IgA deficiency causes false negatives; use IgG-based tests if IgA is low.

IgG kappa M-protein 1.5 g/dL, 4% marrow plasma cells, no CRAB features. Diagnosis?

MGUS.

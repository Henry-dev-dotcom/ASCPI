Transfusion is safe, but reactions still happen, and the lab plays a central role in working them out. This lesson covers the types of transfusion reaction (and how to tell them apart by timing and symptoms), hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn, how Rh immune globulin prevents it, and the autoimmune hemolytic anemias the blood bank investigates.

The first response to any suspected reaction

Whatever the cause, the first steps are the same, because the most dangerous possibility (an ABO mismatch) gets worse with every extra millilitre:

  1. Stop the transfusion, but keep the IV line open with saline (the patient may need fluids or drugs fast).
  2. Check the identification: patient wristband against the unit label and paperwork, at the bedside. Most fatal reactions come from clerical errors.
  3. Notify the physician and the blood bank.
  4. Send a post-transfusion blood sample (and the bag and tubing) to the blood bank.

The lab then does three quick checks: a clerical check, a visual check of the post-transfusion plasma for pink/red color (free hemoglobin), and a DAT on the post-transfusion sample. It repeats ABO and Rh on the pre- and post-transfusion samples and the unit.

The reactions, sorted by timing

Acute (during or within 24 hours):

  • Acute hemolytic: almost always ABO incompatibility from a clerical error. ABO antibodies fix complement and burst the donor cells inside the vessels. Fever, chills, back or flank pain, a feeling of doom, low blood pressure, red plasma and red urine (hemoglobinuria), positive DAT, possibly DIC and kidney failure.
  • Febrile non-hemolytic (FNHTR): temperature rise of 1 °C or more, from cytokines released by white cells in the unit (or the patient's antibodies against donor white cells). It is a diagnosis of exclusion: you must first rule out hemolysis and bacterial contamination, which also cause fever. Prevented by leukoreduction.
  • Allergic (urticarial): hives and itching, from the patient reacting to proteins in donor plasma. If hives are the only symptom, stop, give an antihistamine, and the transfusion may continue when they settle.
  • Anaphylactic: within minutes: wheezing, low blood pressure, no fever. Classic cause: an IgA-deficient patient with anti-IgA receiving normal plasma containing IgA. Use washed red cells or products from IgA-deficient donors.
  • TRALI (transfusion-related acute lung injury): within 6 hours. Donor antibodies (anti-HLA or anti-HNA, often from multiparous female donors) attack the recipient's white cells in the lungs, causing leaky lung vessels. Non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema: breathing difficulty, often low blood pressure and fever, normal heart pressures.
  • TACO (transfusion-associated circulatory overload): within 6 hours. Simply too much volume, too fast for the heart (elderly, heart failure, small patients). Cardiogenic edema with high blood pressure and a high BNP. Treat with diuretics; prevent by transfusing slowly.
  • Septic: bacteria in the unit, usually platelets (stored warm). High fever, shock. Gram stain and culture the bag and the patient.
Exam trap: TRALI vs TACOBoth cause breathing problems within 6 hours. TACO = too much fluid: high blood pressure, high BNP, responds to diuretics. TRALI = antibody lung injury: often low blood pressure, normal BNP, doesn't respond to diuretics.

Delayed (days to weeks):

  • Delayed hemolytic, 3-14 days later: the patient was sensitised before (pregnancy or transfusion), but the antibody had faded below detection. The new transfusion triggers a rapid memory (anamnestic) response. Classic culprits: Kidd, Rh, Duffy, Kell. Signs: falling hemoglobin, mild jaundice, a new positive antibody screen, and a positive DAT (often mixed-field). Destruction is mostly extravascular.
  • Transfusion-associated GVHD, 4-30 days: donor lymphocytes attack the recipient's tissues (skin rash, diarrhoea, liver damage, and marrow failure with pancytopenia). Almost always fatal. Prevented only by irradiation.
  • Post-transfusion purpura, 5-10 days: a sudden severe drop in platelets, usually in a woman sensitised by pregnancy who lacks the platelet antigen HPA-1a. Her anti-HPA-1a destroys her own platelets too.

Hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN)

HDFN happens when the mother has IgG antibodies against an antigen on her baby's red cells (inherited from the father). IgG crosses the placenta, coats the baby's red cells, and the baby's spleen destroys them. The baby becomes anemic and jaundiced, and in severe cases develops heart failure and swelling (hydrops fetalis).

ABO HDFN vs Rh HDFN:

  • ABO HDFN: mother usually group O (her anti-A,B is partly IgG), baby group A or B. It can happen in a first pregnancy. Usually mild, because the A and B antigens are weak on fetal cells and also found on other tissues that soak up the antibody. DAT weak or negative; spherocytes on the smear. There is no way to prevent it.
  • Rh (anti-D) HDFN: D-negative mother sensitised by a previous D-positive baby (or transfusion). The first pregnancy is usually spared because sensitisation mostly happens at delivery. Can be severe: strongly positive DAT, many nucleated red cells (the fetal marrow pours out immature cells). Prevented by RhIG. Anti-K is also severe, because it destroys red cell precursors.

Cord blood testing: ABO (forward only), Rh and DAT. If the mother is D-negative and the baby appears D-negative, test the baby for weak D, because the result decides whether she needs RhIG.

Exchange transfusion removes coated cells and bilirubin. The blood should be group O, negative for the antigen (D-negative for anti-D), compatible with the mother's serum (the antibody comes from her), fresh (under 7 days, so potassium is low and 2,3-DPG is high), irradiated and CMV-safe.

Rh immune globulin (RhIG): how it works and when to give it

RhIG is concentrated anti-D. When given to a D-negative mother, it coats any D-positive fetal cells in her circulation, and they are cleared before her immune system notices them. So she never makes her own anti-D.

  • Give at about 28 weeks of pregnancy and within 72 hours after delivery of a D-positive baby.
  • Also after events that can mix fetal and maternal blood: miscarriage, abortion, ectopic pregnancy, amniocentesis, abdominal trauma.
  • Not given if the mother has already made her own anti-D (it's too late), or if she is D-positive. Practice for mothers with weak D depends on lab policy and RHD genotyping.
  • After an antenatal dose, a weak anti-D (low titer) in the mother is usually passive RhIG, not true sensitisation.

Immune hemolytic anemias

  • Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (WAIHA): IgG autoantibody active at body temperature, usually panreactive (reacts with every cell). Spherocytes; DAT positive for IgG (with or without C3). Associated with lupus, CLL and drugs. Crossmatching is difficult; adsorption is used to look for hidden alloantibodies.
  • Cold agglutinin disease: IgM anti-I that binds at cold temperatures (fingers, ears) and fixes complement, then falls off as the blood warms, leaving C3 only on the cells. Red cells clump in the tube at room temperature (falsely high MCV and MCHC). Follows Mycoplasma (anti-I) and infectious mononucleosis (anti-i), or lymphoma.
  • Paroxysmal cold hemoglobinuria (PCH): an IgG anti-P "biphasic" hemolysin. It binds red cells in the cold and then, when warmed, activates complement and bursts them. Usually children after a viral infection. Diagnosed by the Donath-Landsteiner test (blood is chilled then warmed; hemolysis appears).
  • Drug-induced: penicillin (drug on the cell), methyldopa (true autoantibody), quinidine (immune complexes).

Check your understanding

Twenty minutes into a transfusion, the patient has fever, back pain and red urine. Most likely cause, and the first action?

Acute hemolytic reaction, most likely an ABO error. Stop the transfusion, keep the line open with saline, check identification, notify, and send a post-transfusion sample.

Hives only, normal vital signs, normal clerical check. What happens next?

Mild allergic reaction: pause, give an antihistamine, and resume if the hives resolve (per policy).

Why is ABO HDFN usually milder than Rh HDFN?

A and B antigens are weakly developed on fetal red cells and are also present on other tissues and in fluids, which absorb much of the maternal antibody.

Why does cold agglutinin disease give a DAT positive for C3 only?

The IgM antibody binds in the cold and fixes complement, then detaches as the blood warms, leaving only C3 on the cells.

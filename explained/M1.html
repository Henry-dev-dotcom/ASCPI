Microbiology is detective work. You start with a specimen and a few clues (what the organism looks like under the microscope, what it grows on, and how it reacts to a few quick tests) and narrow it down step by step. This lesson gives you the toolkit: stains, culture media and the basic identification tests. Every later micro lesson builds on it.

The 8 big ideas, explained

  1. Gram stain order: crystal violet, iodine, decolorizer, safranin. Too much decolorizer washes the purple out of Gram-positives, so they falsely look pink.
  2. Catalase separates Staph (positive) from Strep (negative); coagulase identifies S. aureus.
  3. Strep identification uses a few disks and tests: bacitracin (group A), CAMP (group B), optochin (S. pneumoniae), bile esculin (Enterococcus and group D).
  4. Oxidase splits Gram-negative rods. Enterobacterales are oxidase negative; Pseudomonas, Neisseria, Vibrio and Campylobacter are oxidase positive.
  5. MacConkey agar: pink colonies ferment lactose (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter); colorless colonies don't (Salmonella, Shigella, Proteus, Pseudomonas).
  6. Haemophilus needs X and V factors, supplied by chocolate agar.
  7. Malaria: thick smear to find, thin smear to identify.
  8. Never refrigerate CSF. Neisseria meningitidis and H. influenzae die in the cold.

How the Gram stain works

Bacteria have one of two wall designs, and the Gram stain exploits the difference:

  • Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan wall. Crystal violet and iodine form a large complex that gets trapped in the thick wall. Alcohol dehydrates the wall and seals the dye in, so they stay purple.
  • Gram-negative bacteria have a thin peptidoglycan layer plus an outer lipid membrane. Alcohol dissolves the lipid membrane and the dye washes out. The red counterstain (safranin) then makes them pink.

Steps: crystal violet (primary stain) → iodine (mordant, locks the dye) → alcohol/acetone (decolorizer, the critical step) → safranin (counterstain).

Exam trap: wrong Gram reactionsOver-decolorizing, old cultures (worn-out walls) and antibiotic-treated cells make Gram-positives look pink. Under-decolorizing or a smear that is too thick makes Gram-negatives look purple. Always check the control organisms on the slide.
Memory hook"Purple Positive" (thick wall holds the purple). Gram-negatives "lose" the purple, then turn pink.

Other stains and when to use them

  • Acid-fast stains: mycobacteria have waxy mycolic acid in their wall that resists ordinary stains. Carbolfuchsin is driven in with heat (Ziehl-Neelsen, "hot") or a stronger detergent (Kinyoun, "cold"). Once in, acid-alcohol can't remove it, so they stay red ("acid-fast").
  • Modified acid-fast uses a weaker acid (1% sulfuric acid) for organisms that are only partly acid-fast: Nocardia, Cryptosporidium, Cyclospora, Cystoisospora.
  • Auramine-rhodamine: a fluorescent acid-fast stain. More sensitive and quicker to scan, so it is used to screen; positives are confirmed with a traditional stain.
  • India ink: makes the dark background light up around the clear capsule of Cryptococcus in CSF. (The antigen test is more sensitive.)
  • KOH and calcofluor white: KOH dissolves human cells so fungal elements stand out; calcofluor binds fungal cell walls and fluoresces.
  • Lactophenol cotton blue: stains fungal structures for identification.
  • Giemsa: blood parasites (malaria, Babesia), Chlamydia inclusions, Histoplasma inside cells.
  • Acridine orange: fluorescent stain that finds bacteria in blood cultures or specimens when the Gram stain is hard to read.
  • Trichrome: intestinal protozoa in stool.

Culture media: three types

Media fall into three groups, and many plates are two at once:

  • Enriched media feed fussy (fastidious) organisms. Blood agar grows most bacteria. Chocolate agar is blood agar heated until the red cells burst, releasing X factor (hemin) and V factor (NAD) for Haemophilus and Neisseria.
  • Selective media contain inhibitors that stop unwanted organisms. For example, MacConkey has bile salts and crystal violet that stop Gram-positives.
  • Differential media make different organisms look different. MacConkey has lactose and a pH indicator: fermenters make acid and turn pink.

Hemolysis on blood agar is your first clue for streptococci: alpha = partial, greenish (hemoglobin oxidised); beta = clear, complete lysis; gamma = none.

Plates to remember for specific organisms (tie each to its reason):

  • EMB: E. coli gives a green metallic sheen (strong lactose fermentation).
  • Hektoen, XLD, SS: stool pathogens. H2S production gives black colonies (Salmonella).
  • CIN: Yersinia enterocolitica, "bull's-eye" colonies.
  • Thayer-Martin / Martin-Lewis: chocolate agar with antibiotics to select N. gonorrhoeae from contaminated genital sites.
  • Campy-BAP at 42 °C, microaerophilic: Campylobacter (other gut flora don't like 42 °C).
  • TCBS: Vibrio; V. cholerae yellow (ferments sucrose), V. parahaemolyticus green.
  • Mannitol salt: high salt selects staph; S. aureus ferments mannitol and turns it yellow.
  • Bile esculin: Enterococcus and group D strep hydrolyse esculin in bile and turn the agar black.
  • Regan-Lowe, Bordet-Gengou: Bordetella pertussis. Loeffler, Tinsdale (tellurite): Corynebacterium diphtheriae.
  • Löwenstein-Jensen, Middlebrook: mycobacteria. Sabouraud dextrose: fungi. BCYE: Legionella (needs cysteine and iron).

The basic identification tests, and why they work

  • Catalase: breaks hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, which makes bubbles. Staph are positive; strep and enterococci negative. Don't pick up blood agar: red cells contain catalase and cause false positives.
  • Coagulase: S. aureus makes an enzyme that clots plasma. The slide test detects bound coagulase (clumping factor); the tube test detects free coagulase (read at 4 hours and again at 24 hours).
  • Oxidase: detects cytochrome c oxidase, giving a purple color in seconds. Use a platinum, wooden or plastic loop: iron (nichrome) loops give false positives.
  • PYR: positive (red) for group A strep and Enterococcus.
  • Urease: splits urea into ammonia (pink). Rapid in Proteus, H. pylori, Cryptococcus, Brucella.
  • Indole: tryptophanase breaks tryptophan into indole, giving a red ring with Kovac reagent. E. coli and Proteus vulgaris are positive.
  • Bile solubility: S. pneumoniae carries an autolysin that bile switches on, so the colony dissolves.
  • DNase: S. aureus, Serratia, Moraxella.
  • Hippurate hydrolysis: group B strep and Campylobacter jejuni.
  • Nitrate reduction: almost all Enterobacterales positive (this is also why the urine nitrite test works).

Check your understanding

Gram-positive cocci appear pink in a smear from an old culture. What happened?

Old cells have damaged walls that lose the crystal violet, so they stain Gram-variable or pink. Over-decolorization causes the same thing. Use a fresh culture.

Why does chocolate agar grow Haemophilus but blood agar usually doesn't?

Heating lyses the red cells, releasing X factor (hemin) and V factor (NAD). Intact blood agar also contains enzymes that destroy V factor.

A catalase test on colonies picked from blood agar bubbles weakly. Why be careful?

Red cells carried over from the agar contain catalase and can give a false positive.

Which stain is used to screen a sputum for mycobacteria quickly and sensitively?

Auramine-rhodamine (fluorescent acid-fast). Positives are confirmed with Ziehl-Neelsen or Kinyoun.

When a blood vessel is cut, the body stops the bleeding in two stages. Primary hemostasis is the fast first response: platelets stick to the injury and form a soft plug within minutes. Secondary hemostasis (next lesson) then reinforces the plug with fibrin. This lesson is about the platelet plug, the proteins it depends on, and the classic inherited and acquired disorders.

The platelet plug in three steps

Think of it as patching a hole in a hose:

  1. Adhesion: platelets stick to the wound. Damage exposes collagen under the vessel lining. Von Willebrand factor (vWF) acts like double-sided tape: one side grabs the collagen, the other grabs the platelet's GPIb receptor. Without vWF or GPIb, platelets can't hold on, especially in fast-flowing blood.
  2. Activation: platelets change shape and send signals. Stuck platelets turn spiky, release ADP and serotonin from their granules, and make thromboxane A2. These signals call more platelets in.
  3. Aggregation: platelets stick to each other. Activated platelets switch on the GPIIb/IIIa receptor. Fibrinogen forms bridges between GPIIb/IIIa receptors on neighbouring platelets, building a plug.
Memory hook: the receptorsGP Ib is used Initially, to adhere (with vWF). GP IIb/IIIa is used second, to aggregate (with fibrinogen).

Bleeding pattern tells you the stage

Platelet problems and clotting-factor problems bleed differently, which is a common question:

  • Platelet / primary hemostasis problems: bleeding from skin and mucous membranes. Expect petechiae (pinpoint red dots), easy bruising, nosebleeds, gum bleeding, heavy periods, and bleeding immediately after a cut.
  • Clotting factor problems (like hemophilia): deep bleeding into joints and muscles, and bleeding that starts again hours after surgery.

Testing the platelet plug

  • Platelet count: normal about 150-450 × 10⁹/L. Spontaneous bleeding becomes likely below about 10-20.
  • PFA-100 closure time: blood is pushed through a tiny hole coated with collagen plus ADP or epinephrine, and the test times how long platelets take to plug it. It is prolonged by aspirin, vWD and platelet defects. It has replaced the old bleeding time.
  • Platelet aggregation studies: agonists are added to platelet-rich plasma and the machine measures how much light gets through as platelets clump. Each agonist tests a different pathway (see below).

The four classic disorders, and why each test pattern happens

Bernard-Soulier syndrome: missing GPIb. Platelets can't bind vWF, so ristocetin (an antibiotic that forces vWF to bind GPIb) produces no clumping. Adding normal plasma doesn't help, because the defect is on the platelet, not in the plasma. Platelets are giant and the count is low.

Glanzmann thrombasthenia: missing GPIIb/IIIa. Platelets can stick but not link to each other. So they fail every agonist that needs aggregation (ADP, collagen, epinephrine, arachidonic acid), but ristocetin is normal, because ristocetin only tests the GPIb-vWF step. Platelet size and count are normal.

Von Willebrand disease: low or faulty vWF. The most common inherited bleeding disorder. Platelets are normal, but the "tape" is missing, so the ristocetin response and ristocetin cofactor activity are low. Unlike Bernard-Soulier, adding normal plasma (which contains vWF) corrects the defect. vWF also carries and protects factor VIII, so factor VIII may be low and the aPTT may be long.

ITP (immune thrombocytopenia): antibodies destroy platelets. The marrow responds by making more megakaryocytes and releasing young, large platelets. The count is low, but everything else (hemoglobin, WBC, clotting times) is normal. In children it often follows a viral illness and settles by itself.

Exam trap: Bernard-Soulier vs vWDBoth fail ristocetin. Add normal plasma: if it corrects, the missing piece was in the plasma (vWF, so vWD). If it doesn't correct, the platelet is missing GPIb (Bernard-Soulier). Giant platelets also point to Bernard-Soulier.
Why aspirin affects platelets for their whole lifeAspirin permanently blocks cyclooxygenase, so the platelet can't make thromboxane A2. Platelets have no nucleus and can't make new enzyme, so the effect lasts the platelet's lifespan (7-10 days). Aggregation to arachidonic acid is absent, and responses to collagen and epinephrine are weak.

A falsely low platelet count

Sometimes the analyzer reports very low platelets in a patient who isn't bleeding. A common cause is EDTA-dependent antibodies that make platelets clump in the purple tube, or stick around neutrophils (platelet satellitism). The analyzer counts each clump as one big cell or misses it. Always look at the smear: if you see clumps, recollect in a sodium citrate tube, recount, and multiply by about 1.1 to correct for the citrate dilution.

Check your understanding

Platelets are normal in size and number. They fail to aggregate with ADP, collagen and epinephrine but respond normally to ristocetin. Diagnosis?

Glanzmann thrombasthenia (missing GPIIb/IIIa).

Ristocetin aggregation is absent and corrects when normal plasma is added. Diagnosis?

Von Willebrand disease. The normal plasma supplied the missing vWF.

A child has platelets of 12 × 10⁹/L with large platelets two weeks after a cold. Everything else is normal. What is most likely?

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP).

Why is bleeding from gums and skin typical of platelet disorders?

Small vessels in skin and mucous membranes rely on the quick platelet plug to seal them. Without it, they ooze straight away, causing petechiae and mucosal bleeding.

Blood banking (immunohematology) is about giving blood safely. The core idea is simple: antigens sit on red cells, and antibodies in plasma attack red cells carrying antigens the body doesn't recognise. Every test in the blood bank looks for one or the other. The ABO system matters most, because its antibodies are already present without any previous transfusion and can destroy incompatible blood within minutes.

The 8 big ideas, explained

  1. Group O red cells are the universal donor; group AB plasma is the universal plasma. O red cells have no A or B antigen for anyone's antibodies to attack. AB plasma has no anti-A or anti-B to attack anyone's red cells. Forward and reverse typing must agree before you report a group.
  2. ABO antibodies are IgM and react at room temperature; Rh, Kell, Kidd and Duffy antibodies are IgG and react at 37 °C / AHG. IgM is a big pentamer that can bridge cells directly; IgG is small and usually needs help (AHG) to show agglutination.
  3. After A and B, D is the most immunogenic antigen: a D-negative person exposed to D-positive blood is very likely to make anti-D.
  4. DAT vs IAT. The DAT asks "is the patient's own red cell coated in the body?" The IAT asks "is there antibody in the patient's serum?" (tested in the tube). A negative antiglobulin test needs check cells to prove it was valid.
  5. Kidd antibodies cause delayed hemolytic reactions and fade quickly. Duffy antigens are destroyed by enzymes.
  6. Suspected transfusion reaction: stop the transfusion, keep the IV line open with saline, check all identification, and send a post-transfusion sample.
  7. RhIG protects D-negative mothers from making anti-D. Give it within 72 hours of delivery of a D-positive baby; the Kleihauer-Betke test measures how much fetal blood entered the mother.
  8. Shelf lives: red cells 42 days (in additive solution), platelets 5 days at room temperature with agitation, plasma 1 year frozen.

How the ABO system works

All four blood groups start with the same building block, the H antigen. Genes then add one sugar to it:

  • The A gene adds N-acetylgalactosamine → A antigen.
  • The B gene adds galactose → B antigen.
  • The O gene adds nothing, so group O cells carry lots of unchanged H.

Early in life, gut bacteria carry sugars that look like A and B antigens. The immune system makes antibodies against whichever of these it doesn't have itself. That's why ABO antibodies are "naturally occurring" and present without any transfusion or pregnancy:

  • Group A: A antigen, anti-B in the plasma.
  • Group B: B antigen, anti-A.
  • Group AB: both antigens, no ABO antibodies.
  • Group O: neither (only H), anti-A, anti-B and anti-A,B.
Why ABO mistakes are so dangerousAnti-A and anti-B are mostly IgM and strongly activate complement. If group A blood is given to a group O patient, the patient's anti-A coats the donor cells and complement punches holes in them inside the blood vessels: acute intravascular hemolysis, which can cause shock, kidney failure, DIC and death. Most of these errors are clerical (wrong patient, wrong label).

Forward and reverse typing: two tests that check each other

  • Forward typing tests the patient's red cells with reagent anti-A and anti-B. Which antigens are on the cells?
  • Reverse typing tests the patient's serum/plasma with reagent A1 and B cells. Which antibodies are in the plasma?

The two must match what's expected. A group A person should show A on the cells and anti-B in the serum. If they disagree, you have an ABO discrepancy, and you must resolve it before giving group-specific blood (give group O red cells meanwhile if needed).

Worked exampleForward: anti-A 4+, anti-B 0. Reverse: A1 cells 0, B cells 4+. Cells carry A; serum has anti-B → group A.

Solving ABO discrepancies

Ask: is something missing, or is something extra? Then match the clue to the cause:

  • Missing reverse reaction (weak or absent antibodies): newborns (who don't make their own ABO antibodies until about 3-6 months, so cord blood is forward-typed only), elderly people, and patients with low immunoglobulins. Fix: incubate longer or at 4 °C (with controls) to strengthen weak IgM.
  • Extra reverse reaction with A1 cells only in a group A person: an A2 subgroup with anti-A1. A2 cells have less A antigen and some A2 people make anti-A1. Confirm by testing their cells with anti-A1 lectin (Dolichos biflorus): A2 cells don't react.
  • Extra reactions at room temperature everywhere: a cold autoantibody (often anti-I). Fix: prewarm to 37 °C, or wash the cells with warm saline.
  • Stacked cells in forward and reverse: rouleaux from high protein (myeloma). Fix: saline replacement, which disperses rouleaux but not true agglutination.
  • Weak extra B on a group A patient: acquired B. Bacterial enzymes in colon cancer or gut infection convert A sugar into something that looks like B. Fix: acidified anti-B or a different reagent clone; the patient's own serum still contains anti-B but doesn't react with their own cells.
  • Mixed-field (a mix of clumps and free cells): two red cell populations, as after transfusion of group O cells to an A patient, or after a stem cell transplant. Fix: review the history.

The Bombay phenotype

Bombay people (genotype hh) can't make the H antigen at all, so they can't make A or B either (there's no H to build on). Their cells type as group O. But because they lack H completely, they also make anti-H, which reacts strongly with group O cells (which are full of H). So a Bombay patient's serum reacts with every group O cell, including screening cells. They can only receive Bombay blood.

ABO genetics

A and B are codominant (both show if both present) and both are dominant over O, which is silent. Each person has two genes, one from each parent:

  • Group A can be AA or AO; group B can be BB or BO; group AB is AB; group O is OO.
  • Parents AO × BO can have children of all four groups (AB, AO, BO, OO).
  • Parents AB × OO can only have A (AO) or B (BO) children; never O or AB.
  • Two group O parents can only have O children.

For Rh: D-negative people are dd, so two D-negative parents can only have D-negative children. A D-positive parent may be DD or Dd.

Check your understanding

Forward: no reaction with anti-A or anti-B. Reverse: 4+ with A1 and B cells. Group?

Group O.

Why is reverse typing not done on cord blood?

Newborns haven't made their own anti-A or anti-B yet; any antibody present is maternal IgG, so the reverse type would be misleading.

A patient types as group O but their serum reacts with all group O screening and donor cells. What do you suspect?

Bombay phenotype (anti-H). Only Bombay donor blood is compatible.

A group A patient's serum reacts 2+ with A1 cells, and their cells don't react with anti-A1 lectin. Explanation?

A2 (or weaker A) subgroup with anti-A1.
